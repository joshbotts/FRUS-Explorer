# How have U.S. policymakers' thinking about Cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows Cuba's value and danger being redefined in each generation. In 1874 the Secretary of State called Cuba a cause of "perpetual solicitude" (1874, Document 550). By 1897 an envoy said its sugar plantations were "as necessary to the food supply of the United States as are the cotton fields of India" (1898, Document 498). The treaty proclaimed in 1904 joined independence, intervention and naval stations (1904, Document 207). In 1944 the Joint Chiefs asked for a defence facility under American jurisdiction (1944, Volume VII, Document 878). After 1959 officials proposed to weaken Cuba's economy as a weapon (1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 499), and by 1969 a study called Cuba a base for Soviet military power (1969–1976, Volume E–10, Document 205). By 1977 the Treasury judged American leverage "quite modest" (1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Document 21). I read twenty-three printed documents, chosen from about 4,900 in the editors' Cuba chapters and about 13,900 that mention Cuba.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The phrase "national security" cannot carry this question, because it enters the Cuba record late. I searched all the volumes for it together with Cuba or Havana and found about 2,600 documents, almost all after 1950. Before 1940 I found nine, and eight contain the words; none is about Cuba's bearing on American security. The phrase also appears in Cuba documents about as often as in the whole series, so it measures the decade's vocabulary, not Cuba. "Prosperity" and its relatives gave the same result, and I dropped them as evidence. As controls, a search for "department of state" found documents in every decade and a nonsense word found none. The nineteenth-century volumes are arranged by correspondent and have no Cuba chapters, so my account of 1874 to 1898 rests on five documents and not on a count of its vocabulary; I did not count "defence" or "safety" there. FRUS does not hold the Cuban government's or Spain's own papers, and Congress, the sugar lobbies and public opinion appear only as officials describe them. I extracted no trade statistics, and the one investment total is the Cuban ambassador's claim. I did not read the occupation of 1898 to 1902, the intervention of 1906 to 1909, the Bay of Pigs, the Mariel boatlift, Cuba's African commitments or the consuls' despatches from Cuba. The shelf for the earlier period is the State Department's microfilmed Record Group 59 series. These are despatches from ministers to Spain, 1792–1906 (identifier 177380726) and to Cuba, 1902–1906 (177380758). They also include despatches from consuls in Havana, 1783–1906 (196006797), and notes from the Cuban legation in the United States, 1844–1906 (183303950). I give no access status for them, because my sources state none.

**Scope.** I searched all 553 volumes indexed, 311,429 documents once front matter, editors' notes and the two second editions of volumes are set aside, and I rely on twenty-three printed documents dated 1874 to 1981. The library holds Cuba documents to January 1989, but my findings stop in 1981.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Which documents count as "about Cuba".** Counting every document that mentions Cuba gives about 13,900 and reaches back before 1900, but about three in five mention Cuba fewer than five times. Counting only the editors' Cuba chapters gives about 4,900 and begins in 1900. Meanwhile I used both, never added together, and drew the collection from the chapters where they exist and from word searches where they do not.
- **Whether "national security" means the phrase or the idea.** If you mean the phrase, the answer begins in the 1940s. If you mean the idea, the earlier documents must be read under other names, as I read them (independence, defence, commerce), and those readings rest on few documents.
- **Whose prosperity.** American producers, traders and investors dominate the record, and Cuban living standards appear only when an official raises them; if you want the second, the Cuban side needs sources outside FRUS.
- **Who counts as a policymaker.** I took presidents, secretaries, envoys and military chiefs, because that is whom FRUS prints; widening to Congress and lobbies needs sources outside FRUS.
- **Where to stop.** I ended in 1981; the library holds Cuba material to 1989.

My inclusion rule meanwhile: every document the argument rests on, taken from the editors' Cuba chapters where they exist and from word searches where they do not, and nothing else.

## Findings

### 1. Until the war, Cuba was a rich neighbour to be kept out of European hands

The earliest documents describe Cuba by geography and commerce, and they name independence as the aim. Secretary Fish told the new minister to Spain that Cuba "ought to belong to the great family of American republics" and was "the one great cause of perpetual solicitude in the foreign relations of the United States" (1874, Document 550). Secretary Blaine called it "the key to the Gulf of Mexico" and "a part of the American commercial system" though Spain held it (1881, Document 405). In 1886 the minister in Madrid put the trade at "about 95 per cent" of Cuba's exported sugar (1886, Document 414). None of this is the language of national security. About one document in fifteen among those indexed for the 1870s mentions Cuba, but nearly three in ten of those fall in the single volume for 1874, the year of the Virginius affair, so that rate may reflect one episode.

### 2. In 1897 and 1898 the case for war was a prosperity case, with a security clause

Minister Woodford, explaining American aims to a foreign ambassador, said the United States "does not seek to annex Cuba or to establish a protectorate over Cuba". He then listed what Cuba was worth, calling its sugar plantations "as necessary to the food supply of the United States as are the cotton fields of India", set against a home crop that "does not furnish more than one-tenth of the sugar we consume" (1898, Document 498). The President's message of April 1898 gave injury "to the commerce, trade, and business of our people" as a ground for intervening. It also called the condition of Cuba "a constant menace to our peace" (1898, the President's message of April 1898). The attention shows in the counts: about one document in thirteen among those indexed for the 1890s mentions Cuba, though much of that rate is the single volume for 1898.

### 3. After 1898 security and prosperity were written into one treaty system

The proclamation of 1904 reprints the Platt Amendment's articles. Cuba consents that the United States may exercise "the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence", and sells or leases land "for coaling or naval stations" (1904, Document 207). In 1929 the Cuban ambassador told the Secretary of State that "nearly 1,500 million dollars of American capital" was invested in Cuba; that is Cuba's own claim, the only investment total in my set (1929, Volume II, Document 924). Secretary Stimson in 1930 said the Platt Amendment gave no protectorate over Cuba's internal affairs (1930, Volume II, Document 747). Yet Ambassador Welles in 1933 still treated avoiding American intervention as the purpose of his mission (1933, Volume V, Document 299). In 1934 Secretary Hull wrote that dropping "the permissive right of intervention" had "removed a chronic source of friction". He said the cut in the sugar duty showed a wish "to assist the Cuban people in regaining an adequate and satisfactory standard of living" (1934, Volume V, Document 127).

### 4. From 1941 to 1958 Cuba became a hemispheric defence problem, and sugar a strategic one

The Navy wanted to enlarge the Guantánamo naval station in 1941, and the State Department replied that leased areas under American jurisdiction could not be applied to the rest of the hemisphere (1941, Volume VII, Document 119). Late in 1944 the Secretary of State relayed that "Plans of the President and of the Joint Chiefs of Staff call for a defense facility in western Cuba under the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States Government" (1944, Volume VII, Document 878). The ambassador reported in January 1945 that "President Grau, as a matter of sovereignty, is adamant on the proposition that there shall not be a lease of the bases to anyone" (1945, Volume IX, Document 741). In 1954 the Secretary of State defended Cuba's share of the sugar market because Cuba "has been a reliable expansible source of sugar in both war and peace, a strategic concept which should not be jeopardized" (1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 342). At a meeting with the Joint Chiefs in 1958, a State Department official tied Cuban internal stability to hemispheric defence (1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 70). Sugar and tariff words are in about one in four Cuba documents of the 1940s but only about one in twenty-seven documents overall, so the sugar theme belongs to Cuba. In the editors' Cuba chapters, sugar appears in about two in five documents of the 1940s.

### 5. After Castro the threat was Soviet and revolutionary, and prosperity became an instrument

The Director of Central Intelligence wrote in March 1959 that Castro was advocating a neutralist bloc while supporting Communist-dominated groups (1958–1960, Volume V, Document 112). In April 1960 Mallory wrote that "every possible means should be undertaken promptly to weaken the economic life of Cuba", and that "The principal item in our economic quiver would be flexible authority in the sugar legislation" (1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 499). In the first White House meeting on the missiles, the transcript records McNamara saying the Chiefs answered "substantially" when asked how gravely the balance changed, and adding: "My own personal view is, not at all" (1961–1963, Volume XI, Document 21). The minutes of 20 October 1962 report the President's view differently: the planes did not affect the balance of power, but missiles had "an entirely different impact throughout Latin America" (1961–1963, Volume XI, Document 34). By December 1963 Bundy told President Johnson that "The bare minimum objective of our policy is a Cuba which poses no threat to its neighbors and which is not a Soviet satellite" (1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, Document 729). The words Communist, Soviet and missile are in nearly two in three of the editors' Cuba chapter documents of the 1960s, against one in twenty in the 1930s. About half of all documents of the 1960s use them, so the excess is real but moderate.

### 6. By the 1970s Cuba was a managed problem whose economic leverage was modest

The interdepartmental study of 1969 called Cuba "a double-barreled problem for the U.S.: —as a base for Soviet military power, and —as an exporter of revolutionary doctrine and violence to Latin America", and ranked preventing strategic weapons as the only vital interest (1969–1976, Volume E–10, Document 205). In 1977 Treasury Secretary Blumenthal advised that "The political significance of both sides of the trade issue far outweighs economic effects". He added that "Even on sugar, our leverage in economic terms is quite modest", and he put American claims at about two billion dollars (1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Document 21). In January 1981 Secretary Haig described the aim of the 1962 understanding as preventing Cuba "from becoming a threat to the security of the US" (1981–1988, Volume III, Document 5). The phrase "national security" now fills the editors' Cuba chapters: about one document in twenty in the 1950s, one in three in the 1960s, two in three in the 1970s, and nearly nine in ten of the 44 documents of 1980. Documents that mention Cuba use the phrase about as often as the whole series does, about one in three in the 1960s in both, so this shows the vocabulary of the era, not a special alarm about Cuba. Sugar fell to about one in ten of the Cuba chapter documents in the 1960s and 1970s, and to none of the 44 documents of 1980.

## How this was done

I worked in a copy of the app's index of all 553 volumes, taken on 25 October 2026, and I read it without changing it. Counts are of documents under the search's own rules, which treat "sugar" and "sugars" as the same word, and they do not separate a footnote from a body. I first read the editors' chapter headings, then ran word searches by era for the security and prosperity vocabulary, then read the documents themselves. I kept two scopes apart: every document that mentions Cuba, and the editors' Cuba chapters. I checked each theme count against the literal words in two ways, one strict and one allowing inflections. I verified each of the 35 quoted passages word for word against its document, and a first draft of one theme count, whose term matched nearly every document, was discarded. Two documents reached the collection through a search by meaning, not through any word search. That search ranks passages by closeness of content rather than shared words. It finds about nine in ten of the closest passages, so it can only suggest leads, and I read each before using it. I set aside a reprint of the 1881 despatch that the 1894 appendix repeats. Before finishing I checked again that every quotation in this overview is one of those verified passages and that every document cited is in the collection.
