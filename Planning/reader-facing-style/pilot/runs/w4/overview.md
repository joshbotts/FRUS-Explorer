# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** In the printed record, corruption enters U.S. diplomacy first as a judgment on other governments, and from 1974 as a liability that American firms created. In 1922 Crowder, reporting from Cuba on graft in the Cuban administration, admitted he could find few specific instances (1922, Volume I, Document 841). American reporting from China in 1944–1948 carried Nationalist corruption as a cause of failure and a subject for reform (1944, Volume VI, Document 292; 1947, Volume VII, Document 583; 1948, Volume VII, Document 471). In 1961 graft framed U.S. assessments of Korea and the Philippines (1961–1963, Volume XXII, Document 244; 1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 356). From 1974 the bribes at issue were paid by American companies, and officials weighed the harm to relations (1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents 122 and 155; 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Document 159). By 1980 the question was whether the law against such bribes cost exports (1977–1980, Volume III, Document 248). The language of corruption appears in about one document in a hundred across all 553 volumes.

**Scope.** I searched all 553 volumes, leaving out front matter, editorial notes and the second editions of reissued volumes. About three thousand documents use the corruption words, about one in a hundred of the documents searched.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Does "corruption" mean only official and political corruption?** If so, about a quarter of the matching documents fall away, because only about three in four of a sample I read use the word in that sense; if not, cable text "corrupted" in transmission and other senses stay in. Meanwhile I counted every use and read only the political ones.
- **Do American companies' payments to foreign officials count as corruption affecting diplomacy?** If they do, the 1974–1980 documents are the centre of the answer; if not, the answer ends with the Korea and Philippines assessments of 1961. Meanwhile I included them.
- **Does a complaint about allegations count, as well as corruption itself?** If it does, a Chinese official's protest at American press coverage of graft belongs here (1948, Volume VII, Document 43); if not, it drops out. Meanwhile I included it.
- **Do bribes paid by the United States itself count?** If they do, covert payments to foreign politicians, which the matching documents hold, need a reading I did not do; if not, they stay outside. Meanwhile I left them unread.

## Findings

### Graft as grounds for pressure on a dependent government: Cuba, 1922

Crowder wrote a memorandum on graft in the Cuban administration, and the editors did not print it. His covering report concedes the evidence was hard to get:

> "I have not been able to present much in the way of specific instances of corruption. There is here almost a conspiracy to conceal facts" (1922, Volume I, Document 841)

A search by similarity of content, which compares each document's subject matter rather than its words, places about twenty more Crowder telegrams beside this one that do not use the words. I did not read them.

### A measure of an ally's viability: China, 1944–1948

Wartime reporting from one province described corruption as routine. A 1944 report found that "graft, bribery and corruption are standard practices to be found in every section of the province" (1944, Volume VI, Document 292). By 1947 Sprouse was listing it among the reforms to seek: "A thorough investigation should be made into the charges of corruption among army officers" (1947, Volume VII, Document 583). In 1948 Clubb transmitted Fu Tso-yi's verdict that "Nationalist failures in suppression rebellion entirely due to Nationalist corruption and impotence" (1948, Volume VII, Document 471).

### The allegation itself as a problem in relations: China, 1948

The charge of corruption, and not only the fact, became a grievance between the two governments. Stuart transmitted Hollington Tong's complaint, as translated:

> "many US reporters, when they come across any corruption or graft, play up the cases, making the US public believe that the entire Chinese Government is corrupt" (1948, Volume VII, Document 43)

### Graft and the standing of aid recipients: Korea and the Philippines, 1961

In 1961 U.S. assessments counted a government's handling of graft. One Korean report recorded the "Military government’s efforts to deal with wholesale graft, bribery and corruption in government and business" (1961–1963, Volume XXII, Document 244). A Philippine memorandum identified "the graft and corruption in the Garcia Administration which he views as the principal cause for the low state of Garcia’s public image" (1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 356).

### American payments abroad as a diplomatic liability, 1974–1975

The Jidda airgram of 1974 framed corruption as a threat to American interests, not a moral question: "As we have watched corruption mount we have asked ourselves what we might do to counter it—not from any moralistic concern, but as a means of guarding U.S. interests." It added: "Our own U.S. government reputation for incorruptability may one day prove to be our most valued asset in Saudi Arabia." (1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Document 122). In the United Brands case of 1975, "The Department just learned of the bribe. It refused to help cover it up." (1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Document 159). Atherton's 1975 briefing, which I found by its similarity to the Jidda airgram and by no word search, warned:

> "Problems arising from publicity over the enormous commissions being paid by American firms to promote sales of defense articles and services to the Saudi Government threaten to strain US-Saudi relations." (1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Document 155)

The reach ran to Europe: "At that point apparently, Lockheed bribed Prince Bernard to weigh in with the Germans." (1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292).

### From scandal to statute: the export cost of the law, 1980

By 1980 the debate was over what the prohibition cost American trade. The 1980 document restated the principle:

> "As a nation we have decided that bribery in international trade is reprehensible and that American companies must forego exports that can be attained only by illicit payments." (1977–1980, Volume III, Document 248)

## How this was done

I searched the full text of all 553 volumes for any word beginning "corrupt" and the forms of "bribe", "bribery", "graft", "kickback" and "embezzle"; the search treats "bribe" and "bribes" as the same word. I left out front matter, editorial notes and the second editions of reissued volumes, and found about three thousand documents, about one in a hundred of those searched. A sample I read showed that about three in four use the word of political or official corruption, so the count is an upper bound on documents about the subject. The share rose from about one in three hundred in the 1930s to about one in fifty in the 1960s and 1970s. About eighteen of the matching documents are printed twice in different volumes, and I did not adjust the rates for them. From the documents the search ranked as most relevant I chose threads and read seventeen documents whole. I cite twelve of them here. A search by similarity of content, run from some of those documents, surfaced one more that I then read and cite. I checked every quotation word for word against its document's text and found all thirteen exact. Every document this overview cites is in the collection, and the collection holds no others.

## Read next

The collection "Corruption and U.S. foreign policy: scope run" holds the twelve documents cited here, each with its verified passage, grouped by thread and in date order. Its closing note lists the threads I did not read: South Vietnam, Iran in 1951–1954, covert U.S. payments to foreign politicians and the 1976 guidelines on bribery of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. Your additions to it would decide the open questions above. A ruling on covert payments would open the Congo documents, and adding South Vietnam would test whether the 1961 pattern held through the war. Crowder's telegrams around his 1922 report would show whether his report on graft became policy.
