# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series prints, in chapters the editors themselves titled for the recognition of a government or for the revolution or coup that produced one, the tests the United States applied and the demands it made, from Wilson in 1913 to the Carter administration in 1978, and the tests change in a traceable sequence. Wilson refused recognition until a government had been chosen by a free election in which the usurper did not stand (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 2, 1913*, Documents 7 and 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028); in Central America from 1923 to 1936 the United States applied Article II of the five republics' own treaty, which barred recognition of any government born of a coup (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II*, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345); outside Central America Stimson's three questions of 1930 asked only whether a government was in control without resistance, would meet its international obligations and would hold elections in due course (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I*, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375); in the war years the question became whether the new government was friendly to the Allied cause, decided by consultation among the republics (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486); after 1948 the Department decided that recognition should not be a political weapon and the Bogotá conference replaced the word with continuing relations (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX*, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115); Kennedy returned to conditions in 1962 and 1963 (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics*, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359); and by 1973 the stated practice was to recognize countries, not governments (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973*, Document 349, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d349). The set those findings rest on is about three and a half thousand documents in fifty-two volumes, dated 1895 to 1980; the word "recognition" appears in roughly one document in four of them, against about one in twenty across the whole index, so the set is about the question and not merely about Latin America (the exact counts, rates and denominators are in the memo).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels lead out of the printed page, and they were never added together. The first is the series the printed documents came from. All but about a hundred of the core documents carry a source line, and more than nine in ten of those cite the Department of State's Central Decimal File, the Department's own filing system of 1910 to 1963 in which a number names a country and a subject: the heaviest classes are the internal political affairs of Mexico, Cuba, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Bolivia, Guatemala, Peru, the Dominican Republic, Argentina and Venezuela, and the recognition sub-class of the Mexican, Salvadoran and Bolivian files (Record Group 59, General Records of the Department of State; no bundled series record, identifier or access status exists for the Central Decimal File itself, so for 1895 to 1949 this channel resolves to a class of file, not a shelf). After 1963 the documents came from the National Archives' Central Files 1964–66 and 1967–69 (Record Group 59, including the file on Honduran government, POL 15 HOND), from the Nixon Presidential Materials' NSC Files, and from three presidential libraries: the Kennedy Library's Papers of John F. Kennedy, National Security File (collection identifier 1164; Country Files series identifier 193236, 1961–1963); the Johnson Library's National Security Files (identifier 567979; Latin America Country Files identifier 567985, 1963–1969); and the Carter Library's Records of the Office of the National Security Advisor (identifier 560131; Zbigniew Brzezinski's Country Files identifier 560151, 1977–1981), none with a recorded access status, plus a few documents from the Eisenhower Library's Whitman File. Two office files, called lot files because an office's retired papers are shelved under a lot number, are in this channel as well: lot 66D149, Presidential Memorandums of Conversation, Executive Secretariat, 1956–1964, Record Group 59, entry A1 5158, identifier 610077, unrestricted; and lot 84D241, Briefing Books of Cyrus R. Vance, Office of the Secretary, 1978, entry UD-14D 69, identifier 26309216, partly restricted. The second channel is the series the editors cited in footnotes without printing, a hundred and sixty citations over a hundred and eighteen core documents, most to the Department of State's own files and the rest to the Carter and Nixon materials above, and among them six lot files: lot 63D127, Subject and Country Files, Office of Central American and Panamanian Affairs, 1955–1963, entry A1 3157, identifier 2363840, possibly restricted; lot 64D16, Records Relating to El Salvador, Office of Central American Affairs, entry A1 3159, identifier 1750062, partly restricted (it is cited both ways, as a source and in footnotes); lot 65D330, The Secretary's and the Under Secretary's Memorandums of Conversation, Executive Secretariat, 1953–1964, entry A1 1566, identifier 602231, unrestricted; lot 66D95, Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, Executive Secretariat, 1947 onward, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, partly restricted (also both ways); lot 71F154, Records Relating to Petroleum Issues in Lima, Peru, U.S. Embassy Peru, 1962–1966, entry UD-WX 660, identifier 27023004, fully restricted; and lot 82D100, Official Files and Briefing Books of Edmund Muskie, Office of the Secretary, 1977–1981, entries UD-05D 71–72, identifier 26309182, fully restricted. All the lot files are Record Group 59 at College Park; the identifiers are the National Archives Catalog's.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The 1950s are almost missing: the core holds four documents dated 1950 to 1959, because the editors of those volumes titled no chapter for recognition. A full-text search for "recognize", "recognition" and their other forms within six words of "government", "regime" or "junta", run over the two hundred and thirty-three Latin American volumes (with "department of state" as a positive control, found in all but one volume, and a nonsense string as a negative control, found nowhere), does find hits inside the country chapters of the 1952–1957 volumes, for Cuba, Bolivia, Guatemala and Argentina, but none was read, so this is a limit of the scope and not a finding that the series is silent; the same is true of Brazil in 1964 and Argentina in 1976, which have hits that were not read. Decades before 1895 show hits in the hundreds each and were outside the scope chosen. The texts of the Estrada doctrine, Mexico's rule of recognizing states rather than governments, and of the inter-American resolutions of 1943 and 1965 are cited by the editors but not printed, and the Latin American governments' own reasoning survives only as reported in United States despatches. Acheson's recognition speech of September 19, 1949, to which one printed document refers by an editorial note, could not be found: a search of the 1949 volume's editorial notes for "recogn*" with "Acheson" and "September" returned nothing. No shelf outside the Department's own files is identified for any of these gaps.

**Scope.** Fifty-two volumes of the series, from the 1895 volume to the 1977–1980 South America volume, holding about three and a half thousand documents in ninety editors' chapters on recognition or revolution and eight hand-marked runs of documents in the post-1950 country chapters; this copy of the index holds five hundred and fifty-three volumes in all. Excluded were the editors' front matter and editorial notes, both second editions, and chapters on claims, asylum, border raids, the Cuban intervention of 1906, Haiti in 1914–1915, the Dominican Republic in 1912–1915, Mexican finance in 1916, the Mexican insurrection of 1929 and the fall of Machado in 1933.

## Decisions this needs from you

- Whether a government that "came to power by force" includes the fall of an elected president (Cuba 1933) or a failed insurrection (Mexico 1929): both were left out here, and including them would add two chapters whose subject is a collapse rather than a rise. The rule used meanwhile is the editors' own chapter title, taken when it names the recognition of, or a revolution, coup or overthrow producing, a government in a Latin American republic.
- Whether "recognition" after 1948 should include the "continuing relations" the Bogotá resolution substituted for it: the documents of Peru 1948, Venezuela 1948, Panama 1949, Haiti 1950, Argentina 1966, Chile 1973 and Bolivia 1978 are in the set only because it was read that way, and a strict reading of the word would end the answer in 1947.
- Whether the 1950s cases inside the general country chapters count: as the scope stands they are absent, and reading them would be the first step to a different decade shape.
- Whether to go below 1895: the earlier decades are in the index and were not read.

## Findings

### Wilson's test was the origin of the government, not its control of the country

From March 1913 the Wilson administration told the Latin American republics that "just government rests always upon the consent of the governed" and that "We can have no sympathy with those who seek to seize the power of government to advance their own personal interests or ambition" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 2, 1913*, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). In Mexico that became four demands carried by John Lind: "(a) An immediate cessation of fighting throughout Mexico, a definite armistice solemnly entered into and scrupulously observed; “(b) Security given for an early and free election in which all will agree to take part; “(c) The consent of General Huerta to bind himself not to be a candidate for election as President of the Republic at this election; and “(d) The agreement of all parties to abide by the results of the election" (same volume, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028), which Mexico's foreign secretary refused, saying that "His Excellency Mr. Wilson is laboring under a serious delusion" (Document 1029, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1029). The Costa Rican formula of 1917 made the test explicit: "no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 4, 1917*, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379). In Bolivia in 1920 the United States and Argentina agreed "to wait until a government has been formed as a result of the elections in accordance with the Constitution before recognizing it" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume I*, Document 391, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d391), and recognized the result "not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I*, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238). The Mexican chapters of 1913 to 1916 are about a quarter of the whole set, and two thirds of the 1910s documents are Mexico.

### In Central America the rule was a treaty the five republics had written for themselves

In June 1923 Hughes announced that the United States, though not a party, would follow Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity, under which the republics "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II*, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). Its hardest case was El Salvador: in 1932 the Department held that "there is no other way open than for Martínez to resign the Presidency outright" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 639, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d639), and Welles told Roosevelt in 1934 that "In accordance with a policy, already announced and well established, of supporting the Treaty, the United States also declined to recognize General Martínez" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199). In April 1936 Hull concluded that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). Across the whole set the phrase "withhold recognition", in any of its forms, occurs in only about forty documents; the demand was more often stated as a condition than as a refusal.

### Outside Central America the test was control, obligations and elections in due course

When revolutions overturned Argentina, Peru and Bolivia in September 1930, Stimson's press release stated the three questions the Department would ask of a government that held power in fact, whatever its legal title: "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I*, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). The three questions recur for forty years. The phrase "international obligations" appears in about one core document in twenty-seven, "de facto" in about one in sixteen, and "free elections" in fifty-four documents, each phrase five to nine times more common in the set than across the index as a whole (see the memo).

### In the war the test became friendliness to the Allied cause, decided by consultation

Of Argentina in June 1943 Hull wrote that "recognition of the new Government, if and when accorded, should be as nearly simultaneous as possible" and that "it is obviously necessary that there be the fullest exchange of information and consultation between the several governments" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 334, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d334). Of Bolivia in December he said that "Considerations of the security of the Hemisphere and of the war effort of the United Nations must have first importance in any matter of this or similar character. Included in the relevant considerations is the question whether outside influence unfriendly to the Allied cause played any part" (same volume, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486). Nonrecognition of Argentina in 1944 was put as a demand for a war contribution: "it is almost incredible that any American regime should expect recognition and the advantages of membership in the American family of nations without sincerely contributing to this common effort" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII*, Document 231, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d231). After the war the Stimson questions returned in the Venezuelan case of 1945, "the seven man Junta in Venezuela is in control of that country; (2) that it enjoys general support without active opposition and (3) that it intends to fulfill its international obligations" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX*, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170), and in Bolivia in 1946, where the Department was "favorably impressed by public assurances of Junta that elections will be called in near future and that Bolivia’s international obligations will be fulfilled" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The American Republics, Volume XI*, Document 312, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d312). By 1947 the method was exhausted in Nicaragua: "like it or not, present regime in Nicaragua has all attributes and qualities of stable de facto Govt" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The American Republics, Volume VIII*, Document 742, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v08/d742). The word "consult" and its forms appear in about one core document in fourteen.

### From 1948 recognition was replaced by continuing relations and three assurances

Kennan's Policy Planning Staff paper on Venezuela laid down that "Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs" and that "Recognition should not be withheld as a political weapon or to express moral censure except in extreme cases when U.S. national interests would be served thereby" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX*, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115). The Bogotá resolution of that year, the Department explained over Peru, "was also intended to simplify the whole process of establishing relations with new governments, and to this end avoids use of the formal term “recognition”" (same volume, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89). What was asked of a junta became a fixed list of three: in Panama in 1949, "(1) all agree that there was no outside intervention in the recent political upheavals in Panama; (2) the government has a broad popular basis of public support" and "(3) the present government apparently intends to live up to its international commitments" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II*, Document 448, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d448); in Haiti in 1950, "(1) Junta will honor all Haiti’s internatl obligations, (2) authority Junta recognized throughout Haiti, and (3) Junta intends maintain democratic practices and call for new legislative elections soon as practicable" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II*, Document 478, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d478).

### Kennedy returned to conditions; after 1965 the United States recognized countries, not governments

The Salvadoran note of December 1960 still rested on assurances, "In view of the assurances set forth in your note and of the official affirmations made public concerning the holding of free elections, my Government has instructed me to state that it welcomes these assurances" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Volume V, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics*, El Salvador, Document 43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05mSupp/es_d43). Under Kennedy the suspension of relations was used to extract them: of Peru in 1962, "It seems clear that the sharp reaction of the United States and other Latin American countries has led the junta to take a position with respect to new elections" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics*, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421); of the Dominican Republic in 1963, "USG will not recognize or deal with this regime as it stands" and "Regime must find some way to return toward representative constitutional democracy" (same volume, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359). After the Argentine coup of 1966, "In keeping with international practice in such cases, diplomatic relations are suspended. We will be consulting with other OAS members in accordance Resolution XXVI of 1965 Rio Conference" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico*, Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132); the criteria of that resolution follow the sentence in the printed document as the editors' summary, not the Department's words. In 1968 the Assistant Secretary proposed "moving U.S. recognition doctrine and practice toward a variant of Estrada Doctrine" (same volume, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512), and after the Chilean coup Kissinger told Nixon the public line was that "we do not recognize individual governments, but rather countries" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973*, Document 349, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d349). The Bolivian guidance of 1978 closes the sequence: "When we continue relations with a country, as in this instance, it is not necessary to address the question of recognition and it has not been our practice to do so" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, South America; Latin America Region*, Document 137, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d137).

## How this was done

The work was done over a read-only copy of FRUS Explorer's full-text index of five hundred and fifty-three volumes, with front matter and editorial notes excluded from every count. Two hundred and thirty-three volumes were taken as Latin American by their titles and subject tags, and a search for the forms of "recognize" near "government", "regime" or "junta" found about five and a half thousand documents in two hundred and twenty-eight of them, with a positive and a negative control run in the same pass. The editors' chapter titles were then read for the recognition of a government or for the revolution or coup that produced one, giving ninety chapters, and eight post-1950 episodes were marked off by hand from headers and dates, since the later volumes have no such chapter titles; the core so defined holds about three and a half thousand documents in fifty-two volumes. Within it, each phrase the question turns on was counted by the index and then checked by reading the document texts for the literal words, because the index treats "constitutional government" and "constituted government" as the same words and matches "withhold" and "recognition" in either order; the literal checks are what the phrase figures above report. The same phrases were counted across the whole index to confirm that they are concentrated in the core. The archival sources were read from each document's source line and from the footnotes and resolved against the bundled National Archives series and presidential-library catalogues; the two channels were kept apart. Fifty-two documents were read whole and thirty passages quoted, each passage checked word for word against the document's stored text. Every quotation above is one of those thirty and every document cited is in the collection; both were checked against the collection file before this overview was finished.

## Read next

The collection "Recognizing governments that came to power by force in Latin America" holds the fifty-two documents read, in six sections from Wilson's constitutional test to the drift toward the Estrada doctrine, with a note on each and the thirty verified excerpts. Reading the 1950s hits inside the country chapters of the 1952–1957 volumes (Cuba, Bolivia, Guatemala, Argentina), and the Brazilian 1964 and Argentine 1976 hits, would fill the gap between the Haitian note of 1950 and the Salvadoran note of 1960 and test whether the Bogotá rule held through the Eisenhower years; adding the decades before 1895 would show whether Stimson was right that his three questions were the rule since Jefferson; and the Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948, printed earlier in the 1948 volume and not read here, would show where Kennan's December formula came from.
