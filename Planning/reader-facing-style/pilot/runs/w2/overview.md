# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From 1877 to 1973 the Department of State wrote down, case by case, a short test for recognizing a Latin American government that had taken power by force: that it actually controls the territory and the machinery of government, that the people accept it or at least do not substantially resist it, and that it is able and willing to meet its international obligations. The statement of the rule written for Colombia in 1900 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 3, 1900, Document 530, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530>) and the Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7>) say it in nearly the same terms across almost half a century. Beyond the test, the Department sometimes added a constitutional condition (a government elected legally and established under the constitution, as in Costa Rica in 1917) or, in Central America, a treaty bar on coup leaders, and it often asked for specific things before recognizing: pledges from Nicaragua in 1911 of a claims commission, a financial adviser and a loan convention (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 7, 1911, Document 933, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933>); a treaty protecting American property from Mexico in 1921 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344>); waiting for elections in Bolivia in 1920; for Argentina in 1944, a suggestion Hull relayed that the road to recognition would open with collaboration against the Axis, new cabinet ministers and a date for elections; and civil liberties, free elections and observers from the Organization of American States from Peru's military junta in 1962 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423>). From 1948 the Department said openly that withholding recognition was a poor way to influence a country's internal affairs (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115>). The finding rests on a collection of 56 documents chosen from about 2,700 documents that the editors filed under chapters on recognition and on revolutions in 41 volumes, and from about 450 later documents in 20 volumes on the Western Hemisphere.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The printed documents came mostly from State Department files, and the editors' footnotes cite more of the same files and a few others. These are two separate channels and their counts are never added together. The data on hand cannot name most of these series fully, and each gap is stated.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.**

- **The text of the Estrada Doctrine, and the Latin American governments' own reasoning.** The Estrada Doctrine is the position, associated with Mexico, that a government should not pass judgment on another's new regime by granting or withholding recognition (this gloss is general background, not drawn from the documents). I searched the phrase "Estrada Doctrine" across the whole library of 553 volumes. It occurs in 28 documents in 18 volumes dated 1931 to 1977 (27 of the 28 hits show the phrase in exactly that form). The control phrase "Department of State" returned nearly 94,000 documents in 552 of the 553 volumes, and a nonsense string returned none, as it should. The count shows that the doctrine is discussed, not that its text is printed; I read two of the 28 mentions (a footnote of 1943 and a document of 1948) and I did not search the Latin American side's vocabulary (foreign ministry, chancery), so I make no claim that anything is missing before the war. I found no shelf for this in the data on hand.
- **Anything after 1980 beyond one volume.** The library holds fourteen volumes dated 1981 or later and only one of them concerns Latin America (South America, 1981 to 1988). Searches for the full names "Raoul Cedras" and "Jean Bertrand Aristide", and for Cedras's name in reversed order, each returned no documents. The control above was met. These zeros describe this library, not the world. I found no shelf for this in the data on hand.
- **The years 1861 to 1894.** The library has 57 volumes for these years. A loose search (recognition or recognize, together with revolution, provisional government or de facto, together with the name of any of a list of Latin American countries) returned about 450 documents in 55 of the 57 volumes. I read two of them, both on Mexico (1877 and 1878).
- **Whether the pledges were kept.** I did not search for outcomes, so I make no claim about them.

**Scope.** I searched the library of 553 volumes (a copy taken on 25 October 2026) and built the main set from about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes dated 1895 to 1950 that sit under chapters whose editors' titles name recognition, non-recognition, a revolution, coup or overthrow, or a recognition decision for a Latin American government, adding about 450 later documents in 20 volumes found by word search, 1949 onward. Front matter and editors' notes were excluded from every count, and no second-edition volume is among the documents used.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "by force."** If you count only coups and revolutions, the set shrinks to the chapters whose titles say so; if you also count irregular or unconstitutional successions and juntas that promised elections, the set widens to the recognition chapters I used, some of which concern governments that did not come to power by force. Meanwhile I used the editors' chapter titles as the rule.
- **What counts as "Latin America."** If you include Mexico, the Caribbean republics (Cuba, Haiti, Panama and the Dominican Republic) and Central America with South America, the Mexican and Cuban cases, which carry the largest groups of documents by file number in the chapter set (about 350 for Mexico and about 300 for Cuba), enter the count; if you restrict the set to South America and Central America, they leave. Meanwhile I included all of them.
- **What counts as "recognition."** The Bogota conference of 1948 chose language that avoids the formal word, so if you count only documents that say recognition, you lose the *resumption of relations* cases of 1948 onward; if you count both, you take in documents that use the word in other senses (about one in ten of the other documents in the same volumes also contains it). Meanwhile I counted both and read each decision document.
- **What counts as "asked of them."** If you count only conditions put to the new regime itself, you keep cases such as Nicaragua in 1911 and Peru in 1962; if you also count the Department's internal criteria and what it asked other governments to do, you add the consultation cases of 1943 to 1945 and the criteria of 1948. Meanwhile I counted all three.
- **Where the period begins.** If the story begins in 1895, where the chapter set begins, the 1877 and 1878 Mexican documents are a prelude; if it begins earlier, the 57 volumes for 1861 to 1894 are mostly unread. Meanwhile I began at 1877 because those are the only earlier documents I read.

## Findings

### 1. Before 1913 the test was control, assent and treaty-keeping, and the extra asks were practical

In 1877 the Department told its minister in Mexico that it would not recognize Porfirio Diaz until it was assured of three things: that the Mexican people approved his administration, that it was stable, and that it would keep its treaties. It wanted a guarantee of frontier order as a condition, not a hope after recognition (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 3, 1877, Document 222, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222>, 16 May 1877). Ten months later Evarts authorized recognition, citing peace largely kept, authority accepted throughout the republic, better frontier measures and two claims installments paid (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 2, 1878, Document 344, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344>, 23 March 1878). The instructions of 1895 for Ecuador (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President, Transmitted to Congress December 2, 1895, Part I, Document 246, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d246>) and 1899 for Venezuela (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 5, 1899, Document 792, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1899/d792>) restate the test in terms of a de facto government, meaning one that actually holds power. The words "de facto" appear in about one chapter document in fifteen, and the rule written for Colombia in 1900 states the test at full length. In 1911 the practical asks appear: the new government of Nicaragua had already been recognized as a de facto government, and the Department then told its minister what it must do next.

> “the Government of the United States before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico waited to be assured that his administration was approved by the Mexican people, and was possessed of due stability and animated by a disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 2, 1878, Document 344, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344>

> “the rule of the United States is to defer recognition of another executive in its place until it shall appear that it is in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority, and that it is in a position to fulfill all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state under treaties and international law.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 3, 1900, Document 530, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530>

> “The Government of Nicaragua is to proceed at the earliest possible date to the signature of a convention with the United States which shall authorize the contemplated bankers’ loan contract, the terms of the latter to be approved thereafter by both Governments, and to make provision, among other things, for the service of the loan secured by a certain percentage of the customs receipts of the Republic collected in a manner satisfactory to both Governments.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 7, 1911, Document 933, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933>

### 2. Wilson to Hughes added a constitutional test and asked for elections and a treaty

Under Wilson the Department began to ask for specific things first. In August 1913 the President reported to Congress the proposals made to Huerta, among them an armistice, an early free election in which all would take part, and Huerta's undertaking not to be a candidate; the proposals were put to Huerta's Mexico and refused (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 2, 1913, Document 1028, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028>). In February 1917 the Department told its minister in Costa Rica to say to Tinoco that no government set up by him would be recognized, and in June it urged his retirement (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 4, 1917, Document 379, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379>; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 4, 1917, Document 407, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d407>). After the Bolivian coup of July 1920 it said it could not tell whether the de facto government represented the people and did not expect to before the December elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume I, Document 387, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d387>); it agreed with Argentina to wait for elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume I, Document 391, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d391>) and on 31 January 1921 determined to recognize the Bolivian government and asked Argentina to act at the same time. In June 1921 Hughes made recognition of Obregon's Mexico subordinate to a treaty guaranteeing property acquired before the constitution of 1917, and a letter to Obregon the next month repeated that entering the treaty would itself bring recognition (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 349, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d349>). Ecuador shows the other pattern: in July 1925 the United States said business would continue but this was not recognition of the new regime as other than the de facto authorities (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1925, Volume II, Document 54, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1925v02/d54>), and full recognition came three years later (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1928, Volume II, Document 741, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1928v02/d741>, 13 August 1928).

> “the desire which this Government has of seeing the will of the people prevail in governmental matters in Costa Rica has forced it to the conclusion that no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Address of the President to Congress December 4, 1917, Document 379, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379>

> “has now determined to extend recognition to the existing Government of Bolivia, not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I, Document 238, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238>

> “In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344>

### 3. In Central America a treaty rule barred coup leaders, until the Department dropped it

In June 1923 Hughes told the missions that the United States was in the most hearty accord with Article II of the Central American general treaty, which the United States had not signed; it barred recognition of leaders who came to power by coup d'etat and of recent ministers or military commanders, and Hughes urged free and fair elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345>). A press release of September 1930 explained that exception when relations were resumed with Argentina, Peru and Bolivia on the usual tests (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375>). In December 1931 the Department applied the rule to General Martinez of El Salvador, because he had come to power by revolution and had been Minister of War within six months, and asked other governments to act in harmony (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232>). Just over two years later, after El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced the treaty, Welles proposed a procedure for recognition and described the Martinez government as relatively efficient (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 199, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199>); Guatemala, Nicaragua and Honduras recognized it on 25 January 1934 and Welles asked to act the next day so that the United States would be seen to act independently (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 255, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d255>); Hull instructed recognition on 26 January (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 257, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d257>). In April 1936 the Department decided to stop being guided by the treaty and told its officers to keep the decision confidential; an earlier memorandum had worried that announcing the change would read as an invitation to rebel (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 109, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d109>; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112>).

> “will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375>

> “The United States Government, therefore, will not recognize General Martínez as President of Salvador.”
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232>

> “the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America, nor should it endeavor to utilize that Treaty as justification for any other action it may take or fail to take”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112>

### 4. Cuba in 1934 and the wartime consultation test

In November 1933 the Department stated its criteria for recognizing a Cuban government as popular support and the ability to maintain law and order, and its telegram reports a judgment that Grau met neither (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 508, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d508>). On 14 January 1934 the President decided that no person or group could be promised recognition in advance (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 85, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d85>), and nine days later the Department instructed formal and cordial recognition of the Cuban government (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 103, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d103>). During the war a fourth test was added, set by the American republics' Committee for Political Defense of Montevideo. After the Argentine coup of June 1943 Hull wanted recognition to be as nearly simultaneous among the American republics as possible and noted that some governments favored the Estrada Doctrine, the position described above (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 334, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d334>). In December 1943 and January 1944 it weighed whether outside influence unfriendly to the Allies had played a part in the Bolivian coup, and sent the other republics a summary of evidence of Nazi and Argentine involvement, which it called very strong, and said recognition was unlikely (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 486, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486>; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 388, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d388>). On 3 April 1944 Hull relayed a British suggestion on Argentina and asked the ambassador's view of it. On 4 April 1945, after Argentina had declared war on Germany and Japan, the Department proposed that the republics resume relations on 9 April and on 8 April the Secretary of State reported to the President that the other nineteen were unanimous (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 251, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d251>; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 254, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d254>).

> “This Government will recognize a Cuban provisional Government which is substantially supported by the Cuban people and is able to maintain law, order and the normal functions of Government.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 85, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d85>

> “the road would be open for reconsideration of our attitude on recognition: 1. Thorough and effective collaboration to foil Axis activities in the Argentine, particularly those relating to espionage, smuggling, and telecommunications; 2. Appointment of new Cabinet Ministers, particularly in Foreign Relations and Interior, that would inspire confidence of collaboration for hemisphere security; and 3. Fixing of a date for general elections.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 226, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d226>

> “There are certain well established requirements of international law for recognition: (a) Control of the machinery of Government; (b) General popular support without active opposition; and (c) willingness and ability to observe international obligations. Due to the war, the Committee for Political Defense of Montevideo established a fourth—whereby when a Government came into power through force, the countries of the hemisphere would consult to determine whether its assumption to power demonstrated direct or indirect Axis influence.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 883, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d883>

### 5. In 1948 the Department separated recognition from approval, and from leverage

The Policy Planning Staff paper of 15 March 1948, approved by the Secretary, gave three factors for recognition, said that recognition implies neither approval nor disapproval, and said that it should not be withheld as a political weapon except in extreme cases (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7>). A memorandum of November 1948 on Peru notes that the Bogota resolution lets the United States keep relations with all Latin American governments and avoids the formal word recognition (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 89, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89>). Venezuela in December 1948 was the test case: Kennan argued against protracted withholding, the Army favored recognition for the supply of oil and the American military mission, and the circular of 16 December said non-recognition was not a suitable approach to dictatorship (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115>; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 118, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d118>; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 119, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d119>). In December 1949 the Secretary instructed delivery of a note acknowledging the new Panamanian government's assurances, among them to maintain the treaties and to have majority support and control of its territory, before the United States recognized it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 450, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d450>). On 30 May 1951 the Secretary of State told the President that Bolivia's junta had met the criteria applied in other recent cases.

> “The recognition policy of the Department should be based upon the three following factors, any or all of which would be applied in a given case according to U.S. national interests: (a) de facto control of the territory and the administrative machinery of State, including the maintenance of public order; (b) the ability and willingness of a government to discharge its international obligations; (c) general acquiescence of the people of a country in the government in power.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7>

> “non-recognition is not a suitable weapon for influencing the course of internal events in Venezuela at this time.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115>

> “From appearances to date the Junta has met the criteria which the United States has applied in other recent cases involving the recognition of Latin American governments which have come to power through irregular procedures. It has established its authority over Bolivian territory, with the substantial acquiescence of the people, it has declared its intent to honor Bolivia’s international obligations, and its assumption of power has not been due to any external influences.”
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1951, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 688, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v02/d688>

### 6. From 1955 to 1973, quick recognition gave way to suspended relations, conditions, and back

In the 1950s the Department moved to recognize military and revolutionary regimes quickly: the American ambassador recommended being among the first to recognize Argentina's new junta in September 1955 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Central and South America, Volume VII, Document 193, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v07/d193>); Dulles recommended prompt recognition of Colombia's military junta in May 1957 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Central and South America, Volume VII, Document 465, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v07/d465>); and Cuba's provisional government was recognized on 7 January 1959, the day it was recommended (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 217, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217>). Under Kennedy it suspended relations and set conditions. In May 1962 it considered asking Colombia and Brazil to line up with it on at least temporary non-recognition if a Peruvian takeover came (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 416, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d416>); after the coup it listed the guarantees it wanted, said resuming relations would not mean resuming aid, and noted that it had waited three weeks in the Argentine case. In October 1963 it told the Dominican regime it would not be recognized as it stood (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 359, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359>); on 13 December the Department explained that the Dominican and Honduran regimes were being recognized, because the aim of withholding had been a return to constitutional government and the prospects of further influence were judged negligible (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 363, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363>). The editors give the period of non-recognition of Honduras as 3 October to 14 December 1963, ten weeks (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 72, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d72>, in an editors' footnote). Under Johnson the Department's recommendation to recognize Bolivia's junta in December 1964 noted private pledges to reduce communist influence and not to restore relations with Cuba or Czechoslovakia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 152, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152>); in June 1966 it suspended relations with Argentina (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 132, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132>) and the next month asked for standby authority to recognize (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 139, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d139>). On 12 September 1973, after the Chilean coup, a meeting agreed that the United States should not be the first to recognize, and a State Department official said the biggest errors in Latin America in recent years had involved too hasty recognition of military regimes, Brazil among them (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 348, <https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348>).

> “1. Guarantee of continued civil liberties. 2. ## How this was done

I searched a copy of the 553-volume FRUS library taken on 25 October 2026, opened read-only and checked first against the expected counts, and I read the index of the volumes' text, not the underlying XML, so every phrase count is a count of search hits, including footnote text. I took the main set from the editors' own chapter titles (about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes) and added about 450 later documents in 20 volumes found by word search, whose hits I treat as candidates, not as recognition decisions. The search index reduces words to a root, so recognize, recognized and recognition are normally found together; to test the term the question supplied, I compared the chapter documents with the other documents in the same 41 volumes and found a word starting with recogn in about three of every eight chapter documents against about one in ten of the others, while the control phrase "Department of State" was less common in the chapter documents (about one in eight) than elsewhere (about one in five). The phrase "international obligations" turns up in about a hundred chapter documents, nearly all in exactly that form. I retrieved 124 documents whole and read in full 55 of the 56 documents in the collection and part of the other; every quotation above was cut from the stored document text and checked to be an exact character-for-character substring of it. Counts like these are conditional on the library of 553 volumes; the exact figures, rates and denominators are in the memo (see the memo). Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this overview is one the memo verified against its document and that every document cited here is in the collection.
