# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series prints, in the editors' own chapters on recognition and revolution, the tests Washington applied to Latin American governments that took power by force between 1895 and 1980, and what it asked of them first. Wilson demanded a government elected under the constitution, in an election the usurper did not contest (1913, Document 7; 1913, Document 1028; 1917, Document 379). In Central America the Department applied Article II of the 1923 treaty, which barred recognition of any government born of a coup, until it gave the rule up in 1936 (1923, Volume II, Document 345; 1936, Volume V, Document 112). Elsewhere Stimson asked whether a government was in control, would meet its international obligations and would hold elections (1930, Volume I, Document 375). The war added a test of friendliness to the Allied cause and a procedure of consultation (1943, Volume V, Document 486). After Bogotá the Department continued relations rather than recognized, asking each junta for the same assurances (1948, Volume IX, Document 115; 1950, Volume II, Document 478), returned to conditions under Kennedy (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 359), and by 1973 recognized countries, not governments (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 349). The finding rests on about three and a half thousand documents in 52 volumes.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The 1950s are almost absent: the chapters the editors titled for recognition hold four documents dated 1950–1959 out of about twelve and a half thousand Latin American documents of that decade. The cases of Cuba in 1952, Bolivia in 1952, Guatemala in 1954 and Argentina in 1955 appear only inside general country chapters of the 1952–1954 and 1955–1957 volumes, and I did not read those passages, so this is a limit of my scope, not a finding that the series is silent; those volumes are the shelf. The same holds for Brazil in 1964 and Argentina in 1976, where the search found passages I did not read. My search was for "recognize", "recognition" and their other forms within six words of "government", "regime" or "junta", over the 233 volumes whose titles or subject tags mark them as Latin American; as controls, the phrase "department of state" found about ninety-four thousand documents across the whole series and an impossible string found none. The texts of the Estrada doctrine, of the 1943 resolution of the Emergency Advisory Committee and of the 1965 Rio resolution are cited by the editors but not printed. A search of the 1949 volume's editorial notes for Acheson's recognition speech of September 1949 found no note, though a printed document refers the reader to one. The Latin American governments' own reasoning survives only as United States officers reported it, with one exception quoted below (1913, Document 1029). Practice before 1895 was outside my scope, though the search found the phrase in use in every earlier decade.

**Scope.** The finding rests on the documents in 90 chapters whose editors' titles name the recognition of, or a revolution or coup producing, a government in a Latin American republic, and in eight date ranges I bracketed by hand in the volumes after 1950: about three and a half thousand documents in 52 volumes, dated 1895 to 1980, out of the 553 volumes indexed. I excluded chapters on claims, asylum, border incursions and revolutionary bands, the Cuban intervention of 1906, the Haitian political chapters of 1914–1915, the Dominican chapters of 1912–1915, the Mexican financial chapter of 1916, the Mexican insurrection of 1929 and the fall of Machado in 1933, which was an elected president's fall rather than a government's rise by force.

## Decisions this needs from you

- Whether "came to power by force" includes the fall of an elected president as well as the rise of a usurper: I excluded Machado's fall in 1933, and including such cases would add Cuba 1933 and change the 1930s count.
- Whether the 1950s cases belong in the answer: the editors titled no chapter for recognition in that decade, so I left the Cuban, Bolivian, Guatemalan and Argentine passages of 1952–1955 unread, and including them means reading the general country chapters rather than relying on the editors' headings.
- Whether "recognition" after 1948 means the resumption of relations: the Department deliberately stopped using the word, so a narrower reading would end the story at Bogotá, and I treated continuing relations as the same decision.
- Whether Mexico 1913–1916 is to count as one case or as the bulk of the evidence: its chapters are about a quarter of the whole set and two thirds of the 1910s, so a finding weighted by document count is a finding about Mexico.
- Whether practice before 1895 is wanted: the search finds the question in every earlier decade and I did not read any of it.

Meanwhile my inclusion rule was the editors' own: a document is in if it sits in a chapter whose title names recognition or a revolution, coup or overthrow in a Latin American republic, or in one of the eight post-1950 ranges, and the two second editions in the library were excluded from every count.

## Findings

### Wilson made the legitimacy of a government's origin the test, not its control of the country

The Wilson administration refused recognition to governments that had seized power and said what it wanted instead: a free election in which the usurper did not stand, and a government elected under the constitution. Bryan's circular of March 1913 carried the president's declaration:

> We hold, as I am sure all thoughtful leaders of republican government everywhere hold, that just government rests always upon the consent of the governed, and that there can be no freedom without order based upon law and upon the public conscience and approval. (1913, Document 7)

The same statement added that "We can have no sympathy with those who seek to seize the power of government to advance their own personal interests or ambition." In August Wilson told Congress that "Mexico has a great and enviable future before her, if only she choose and attain the paths of honest constitutional government", and the four proposals John Lind had carried to Huerta spelled out the price of recognition (1913, Document 1028):

> (a) An immediate cessation of fighting throughout Mexico, a definite armistice solemnly entered into and scrupulously observed; “(b) Security given for an early and free election in which all will agree to take part; “(c) The consent of General Huerta to bind himself not to be a candidate for election as President of the Republic at this election; and “(d) The agreement of all parties to abide by the results of the election and cooperate in the most loyal way in organizing and supporting the new administration.

Huerta's foreign secretary refused, and his reply is one of the few places where the other side's reasoning is printed: "His Excellency Mr. Wilson is laboring under a serious delusion when he declares that the present situation of Mexico is incompatible with the compliance with her international obligations" (1913, Document 1029). The rule was restated for Costa Rica in 1917, when Lansing wrote that "no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition" (1917, Document 379). In Bolivia in 1920 the United States and Argentina agreed "to wait until a government has been formed as a result of the elections in accordance with the Constitution before recognizing it" (1920, Volume I, Document 391), and in 1921 Colby recognized the Saavedra government "not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic" (1921, Volume I, Document 238). A de facto government is one in actual control whether or not lawfully constituted, and the distinction runs through the whole record.

### In Central America the Department borrowed the five republics' own rule, and dropped it in 1936

Article II of the 1923 General Treaty of Peace and Amity, which the United States had not signed, became its recognition policy in the region. Hughes announced through the Minister in Honduras that the United States "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country" (1923, Volume II, Document 345). The hardest case was El Salvador, where General Martínez proposed to deposit power temporarily in a designate. Stimson answered that "there is no other way open than for Martínez to resign the Presidency outright" (1932, Volume V, Document 639), and Welles later told Roosevelt that "In accordance with a policy, already announced and well established, of supporting the Treaty, the United States also declined to recognize General Martínez" (1934, Volume V, Document 199). In April 1936 Hull instructed the Central American missions that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (1936, Volume V, Document 112).

### Outside Central America Stimson asked three questions: control, obligations, elections

When revolutions overturned Argentina, Peru and Bolivia in September 1930, the Department applied a different test and said so publicly. Stimson's press release is the clearest statement in the series of what was asked of a government that came to power by force:

> In reaching the conclusion to accord recognition to these three governments, the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status. (1930, Volume I, Document 375)

The phrase "international obligations" appears in about one core document in twenty-seven, and the phrase "de facto" in about one in sixteen, against one in two hundred and fifty and one in ninety across the whole series: the vocabulary of the test belongs to these chapters. The words "free election" or "free elections" appear in 54 of the core documents.

### The war added a security test and a procedure of consultation

From 1943 the question was no longer only how a government had come to power but whether it was friendly to the Allied cause, and the decision was taken, at least in form, together with the other American republics. Of Argentina, Hull instructed that "recognition of the new Government, if and when accorded, should be as nearly simultaneous as possible", adding that "it is obviously necessary that there be the fullest exchange of information and consultation between the several governments regarding developments in Argentina" (1943, Volume V, Document 334). Of Bolivia, his press statement put the test first: "Considerations of the security of the Hemisphere and of the war effort of the United Nations must have first importance in any matter of this or similar character" (1943, Volume V, Document 486). Nonrecognition was also leverage: "it is almost incredible that any American regime should expect recognition and the advantages of membership in the American family of nations without sincerely contributing to this common effort" (1944, Volume VII, Document 231). Byrnes's circular on the Venezuelan junta in 1945 shows Stimson's questions surviving inside the consultation, finding that the junta "is in control of that country; (2) that it enjoys general support without active opposition and (3) that it intends to fulfill its international obligations" (1945, Volume IX, Document 1170). By 1947 the method was exhausted. Lovett conceded of Nicaragua that "like it or not, present regime in Nicaragua has all attributes and qualities of stable de facto Govt" (1947, Volume VIII, Document 742).

### After Bogotá the Department continued relations instead of recognizing, and asked three assurances

In 1948 the Policy Planning Staff concluded that "Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs" and that "Recognition should not be withheld as a political weapon or to express moral censure except in extreme cases when U.S. national interests would be served thereby" (1948, Volume IX, Document 115). The Bogotá conference's resolution on the right of legation was the instrument: Daniels explained that it "was also intended to simplify the whole process of establishing relations with new governments, and to this end avoids use of the formal term “recognition”" (1948, Volume IX, Document 89). The coups in Panama in 1949 and Haiti in 1950 were handled under that rule, with a decent interval, a note of acknowledgment and assurances asked of the new government. Haiti's junta gave three:

> (1) Junta will honor all Haiti’s internatl obligations, (2) authority Junta recognized throughout Haiti, and (3) Junta intends maintain democratic practices and call for new legislative elections soon as practicable. (1950, Volume II, Document 478)

The Panama consultation had asked the same questions in a different order, finding that "there was no outside intervention", that "the government has a broad popular basis of public support" and that "the present government apparently intends to live up to its international commitments" (1949, Volume II, Document 448). Truman asked to be consulted himself on such decisions (1950, Volume II, Document 477).

### Kennedy returned to conditions; after 1965 the Department drifted toward recognizing countries, not governments

In 1960 the Department recognized El Salvador's junta "In view of the assurances set forth in your note and of the official affirmations made public concerning the holding of free elections" (1958–1960, Volume V, Microfiche Supplement, El Salvador, Document 43). Under Kennedy the suspension of relations was again a lever. Ball reported of Peru that "the sharp reaction of the United States and other Latin American countries has led the junta to take a position with respect to new elections, with respect to current civil rights, with respect to leaders of the previous government and of APRA" (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 421), and after the Bosch coup the Embassy was to state "that USG will not recognize or deal with this regime as it stands" (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 359). In 1966 the Department told Argentina that "In keeping with international practice in such cases, diplomatic relations are suspended. We will be consulting with other OAS members in accordance Resolution XXVI of 1965 Rio Conference" (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 132); the criteria of that resolution are given in the editors' footnote, not in the Department's text. By 1968 Oliver proposed "moving U.S. recognition doctrine and practice toward a variant of Estrada Doctrine" (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 512), the Mexican doctrine of recognizing states rather than governments. After the Chilean coup Kissinger's public line was that "we do not recognize individual governments, but rather countries" (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 349), and in Bolivia in 1978 the Department's guidance read: "When we continue relations with a country, as in this instance, it is not necessary to address the question of recognition and it has not been our practice to do so" (1977–1980, Volume XXIV, Document 137).

## How this was done

I worked over a read-only copy of the app's search index of all 553 volumes, with the front matter and the editors' notes excluded from every count, and I did not read the volumes' source files. I selected the 233 volumes whose titles or subject tags mark them as Latin American, and searched them for "recognize", "recognition" and their other forms within six words of "government", "regime" or "junta"; the search treats inflected forms of a word as the same word. That found about five and a half thousand documents in 228 of the volumes. I then walked the editors' chapter titles for recognition, revolution, coup, overthrow and related words, chose 90 chapters by hand, bracketed eight ranges by hand in the post-1950 volumes, and took the documents in them as the core. Each phrase count was made by the search and then checked by reading a sample of the matching texts, with the phrase "department of state" as a positive control and an impossible string as a negative one; the one count that fell short on checking, "constitutional government", did so because the search folds it with "constituted government", so I report the family under both names. Every count is relative to the volumes in this copy, and the archival channels were resolved against the catalogue data bundled with the app, each creator and access status read from its row and none inferred. I retrieved 52 documents whole, quoted 30 passages, and verified each passage against the stored text of its document before quoting it. I have checked that every quotation above is one of those verified passages and that every document cited above is in the collection.

## Read next

The collection is "Recognizing governments that came to power by force in Latin America". It holds the 52 documents I read, in six sections following the periods above, with the verified passages as excerpts and a note on each document saying why it is there, and it closes with the list of archival sources the app generates from the documents' own source lines. Reading the 1950s country chapters of the 1952–1954 and 1955–1957 volumes, and the Brazil 1964 and Argentina 1976 passages, would close the largest gap and test whether the Bogotá rule held through the Eisenhower years. Adding the inter-American resolutions of 1943, 1948 and 1965 from their own editions would let the Department's reading of them be compared with their texts. Reading the earlier Policy Planning paper of March 1948 that the collection notes and the editorial note on Acheson's speech of September 1949 would complete the Department's own statement of the Bogotá rule.
