# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows corruption entering U.S. policy in three ways. It was a measure of whether a government the United States backed could hold its people and its army: Nationalist China from 1944 to 1948, and Northeast and Southeast Asia in 1961 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, China, Volume VI, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v06/d292; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 583, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d583; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d244). It was a ground for pressing a dependent government, as in Cuba in 1922 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, Document 841, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d841). And from 1974 it became a diplomatic liability of America's own making, when payments by American firms abroad strained relations with Saudi Arabia, in Central America and in Western Europe, and led to the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the 1980 argument over what it cost in exports (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Documents 122 and 155, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d155; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Documents on Mexico; Central America; and the Caribbean, 1973–1976, Document 159, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d159; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 248, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d248). The set this rests on is about one document in a hundred across all 553 volumes of the series, about three thousand documents spread over some four hundred and fifty volumes; in a sample read by hand about three in four of them use the words of official or political corruption, the rest using them in other senses. The language is rarest in the 1920s and 1930s (about one document in three hundred in the 1930s) and commonest in the 1960s and 1970s (about one in fifty). Exact counts, rates and denominators for every figure here are in the memo.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The archives reach the historian by two channels, kept apart here and never added together: the files the printed documents *came from* (about five matching documents in six carry such a source line), and the files the editors' footnotes *point to* without printing them (about one matching document in seven carries such a reference). **Came from:** (a) Department of State central files, Record Group 59 — the Central Decimal File, the commonest form of source line (about half of them), then the Central Files of 1964–66, 1967–69 and 1970–73 and the Central Foreign Policy File; the reference tools used here give no NARA series identifier, entry or access status for any of these. (b) Central Intelligence Agency files, the largest single source; no series named. (c) Presidential library files: the Kennedy Library's National Security Files (NARA identifier 1164, 1961–1963), the Johnson Library's National Security File (567979, 1963–1969), the Nixon Presidential Materials' National Security Council Files (matched by title to "National Security Files (Nixon Administration)", 572235, 1969–1974, a match not verified), the Carter Library's National Security Affairs files, the Eisenhower Library's Whitman File and the Ford Library's National Security Adviser files (these three not resolved to a NARA series); access status for the library files is not in the tools. (d) Record Group 59 lot files: "Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers" (Lot 63D351; entry A1 1586E; NARA identifier 2839192; 1947–1979; partly restricted on national-security grounds; College Park); "Subject and Special Files" (Lot 62D430; entry A1 1586C; 2839191; 1953–1961; partly restricted); and the Marshall Mission Files (Lot 54D270), which NARA arranges as ten series under the Office of the Special Envoy to China (creator identifier 10481258), with no single entry, dates or access status. (e) Record Group 84, the Jidda embassy's post files (Lot 79F80), the source of the 1974 Jidda airgram; not resolved. **Pointed to, unprinted:** (a) Department of State central file classes, Record Group 59, by far the most frequent reference; no series identifier in the tools. (b) The same presidential library files as above (Carter National Security Affairs, Johnson National Security File, Nixon NSC Files, Eisenhower Whitman File). (c) Record Group 59 lots: Lot 66D95 and Lot 63D351, both in "Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers" (entry A1 1586E, 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted); Lot 62D430, "Subject and Special Files" (A1 1586C, 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted); Lot 62D1, "Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council," Policy Planning Council (entries A1 1583E and A1 1583F, 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted); and Lot 89D149, not resolved. NARA's headings were taken from its catalogue data and not checked against finding aids. Of the series the reference tools describe with dates, only about one in sixty begins before 1940, so the 1922 Cuban thread has no series entry here; that is a limit of the tools, not of the archive.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Each of these was established by a search of the full text of all 553 volumes, front matter, editorial notes and the two second editions excluded, with two controls run the same session: the phrase "Department of State" was found in every volume but one (a volume of Paris Peace Conference minutes, which is itself searchable), and a nonsense string was found nowhere, so the search was working and was not matching everything. *Anything after the series ends:* the corruption words in any form matched none of the documents dated in the 1990s, against about a hundred in the 1980s; no shelf for later records was identified in this run. *The withheld passages of the 1970s reporting:* of the matching documents dated from January 1969, about one in six contains a passage marked "not declassified" (a search for that phrase); no shelf opens these. *Enclosures the editors did not print:* Crowder's Memorandum No. 8, "Graft, Corruption and Immorality in the Public Administration," is marked "Not printed" in his 1922 telegram; its shelf is the Department's Central Decimal File, file 837.51/764. *The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act as a subject:* the law's name appears in only four documents in two volumes, and the OECD's bribery language in twelve documents of 1976–1980; four documents are too few to trace the law through FRUS, and no shelf for it was identified here. *"Kleptocracy":* the index holds no word beginning "klepto" at all; the nearest, "anti-corruption" as a phrase, is in about fifty documents. *Improper, illicit or questionable payments:* this second set of phrases matched only twenty-seven documents, and fewer than three in four of them actually contain the phrase, so it cannot be counted. *The Church subcommittee's and the SEC's own records:* the subcommittee is named in over a hundred documents (not read), but its records and the SEC's are not in FRUS, and no reference tool used here names their shelf.

**Scope.** The set is every document in the 553 volumes indexed whose full text uses a word of the corruption, bribery, graft, kickback or embezzlement families — about three thousand documents in some four hundred and fifty volumes — with front matter, editorial notes and the two second editions left out. Seventeen documents were read whole and twelve are cited; about eighteen texts printed twice in the series are in the set twice, so the rates above slightly overstate it.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Does "corruption" mean the word or the thing?** Counting the word, as done here, includes about one document in four that uses it of decayed parcels, garbled cables or moral failing; counting only political corruption means reading each of about three thousand documents. Meanwhile every document that uses the word is counted, and the count is treated as an upper bound.
- **Do payments by American firms abroad count?** If they do, the 1970s are the core of the question and the "improper payments" language must be read by hand, since its search is unreliable; if they do not, the subject ends with corruption in foreign governments. Meanwhile they are included where the corruption words are used, and the one document reached otherwise is cited.
- **Do allegations made by foreign actors count?** A Chinese official's complaint about American reporting, or a general's speech blaming defeat on corruption, is an allegation reported to Washington rather than a U.S. judgment; counting them widens the record of how allegations shaped relations. Meanwhile they are included.
- **Are U.S. covert payments to foreign politicians corruption?** Including them (the Congo, 1964–1968, is in the set) turns part of the question on U.S. conduct; excluding them keeps it on others'. Meanwhile they are in the count but were not read.
- **Is a document printed twice counted twice?** Counting once lowers the totals slightly and changes no finding; meanwhile the rates are not adjusted, and each cited document is counted once.

## Findings

### 1. In Cuba in 1922 corruption was a ground for U.S. pressure, and the evidence was hard to get

The earliest case followed is Crowder's reporting on Cuba. His 1922 telegram names his Memorandum No. 8, "Graft, Corruption and Immorality in the Public Administration," which the editors marked "Not printed," and admits the limit of his evidence:

> "I have not been able to present much in the way of specific instances of corruption. There is here almost a conspiracy to conceal facts"
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, Document 841, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d841

About two dozen more of Crowder's telegrams of 1921–1923 sit closest to this one when the documents are compared by their overall wording; they were not read.

### 2. In China, 1944–1948, corruption was read as the cause of Nationalist failure

Reporting from China treats corruption as pervasive, and Sprouse's 1947 memorandum makes investigating it among army officers one of the things the United States should seek:

> "graft, bribery and corruption are standard practices to be found in every section of the province"
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, China, Volume VI, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v06/d292

> "A thorough investigation should be made into the charges of corruption among army officers"
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 583, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d583

By 1948 the charge was made on the Chinese side too, in a speech by Fu Tso-yi that Clubb transmitted:

> "Nationalist failures in suppression rebellion entirely due to Nationalist corruption and impotence"
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 471, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d471

### 3. The allegation itself became a grievance between governments

In 1948 Stuart transmitted Hollington Tong's complaint that American press coverage of corruption was turning U.S. opinion against the whole government — the allegation, not the corruption, as the issue between the two:

> "many US reporters, when they come across any corruption or graft, play up the cases, making the US public believe that the entire Chinese Government is corrupt"
> — Hollington Tong, as translated in Stuart's telegram, Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d43

### 4. In Asia in 1961, a government's record on graft was weighed in judging its prospects

In the Northeast Asia volume a military government is judged by its campaign against graft; in the Southeast Asia volume President Garcia's poor standing is laid to his administration's corruption:

> "Military government’s efforts to deal with wholesale graft, bribery and corruption in government and business"
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d244

> "the graft and corruption in the Garcia Administration which he views as the principal cause for the low state of Garcia’s public image"
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXIII: Southeast Asia, Document 356, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d356

### 5. In Saudi Arabia, 1974–1975, corruption was framed as a threat to U.S. interests, and then as a strain caused by American firms

The embassy at Jidda argued in 1974 that rising corruption was a matter of interest, not morals, and that American probity was an asset:

> "As we have watched corruption mount we have asked ourselves what we might do to counter it—not from any moralistic concern, but as a means of guarding U.S. interests."
> "Our own U.S. government reputation for incorruptability may one day prove to be our most valued asset in Saudi Arabia."
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 122, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122

A year later Atherton's 1975 briefing on agents' fees put the problem the other way round. This document uses none of the corruption words; it was found because its wording as a whole most resembles the Jidda airgram's, and was read before it was cited:

> "Problems arising from publicity over the enormous commissions being paid by American firms to promote sales of defense articles and services to the Saudi Government threaten to strain US-Saudi relations."
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 155, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d155

### 6. American firms' bribes abroad became a matter for the Department, and then for law

In 1975 the Department learned of a bribe paid by United Brands in Central America, and in Western Europe the Lockheed payments were traced to a prince; by 1980 the question was what the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act cost American exporters:

> "The Department just learned of the bribe. It refused to help cover it up."
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Documents on Mexico; Central America; and the Caribbean, 1973–1976, Document 159, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d159

> "At that point apparently, Lockheed bribed Prince Bernard to weigh in with the Germans."
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Documents on Western Europe, 1973–1976, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292

> "As a nation we have decided that bribery in international trade is reprehensible and that American companies must forego exports that can be attained only by illicit payments."
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 248, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d248

## How this was done

The full text of all 553 published volumes of Foreign Relations of the United States was searched for any word of the corruption, bribery, graft, kickback and embezzlement families, the search treating "bribery" and "briberies", or "corrupt" and "corruption", as the same word; front matter, editorial notes and the two second editions were left out, and the matching documents were counted by the decade of their date. Controls were run in the same session: a phrase every volume should contain, and a nonsense string none could. A sample of forty matching documents, evenly spaced through the set, was read to measure how often the words carry the political sense. The eighty documents that matched most strongly were listed, five threads were chosen from them, and seventeen documents were read whole; twelve are cited, with thirteen quotations, each checked word for word against its document's text. Documents alike in wording were found by comparing each document's overall vocabulary with every other's, which surfaced one cited document that no word search returned and identified about eighteen texts the series prints twice. The archival sources were counted from the documents' source lines and the editors' footnotes, and the series were identified from NARA catalogue data bundled with the research tools; NARA's catalogue was not consulted directly.
