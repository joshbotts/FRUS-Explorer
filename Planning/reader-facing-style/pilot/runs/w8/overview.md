# How have U.S. policymakers' thinking about Cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record answers, in officials' own words, what Cuba was said to mean to American security and prosperity, and it shows six successive pictures rather than one. In 1874 and 1881 Cuba is a rich island next door that should end up independent and outside European hands (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 7, 1874*, Document 550 (index number 552), https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552). In 1897 and 1898 it is a sugar supply, a market and a source of disorder, and the President's message of April 1898 grounds intervention partly in injury to American commerce (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 5, 1898*, the President's message on Cuba, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). From 1904 to 1934 defence and trade are written into the same treaties: the Platt Amendment, which gave the United States a right to intervene and to naval stations (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 6, 1904*, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d207), and the 1934 instruction that announces the end of the right to intervene together with a cut in the sugar duty (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d127). From 1941 Cuba is a problem of hemisphere defence, argued over air and naval bases, and sugar is still defended in 1954 as strategic. After 1959 the threat is Soviet weapons and Cuban subversion and prosperity becomes a weapon: an April 1960 memorandum says "every possible means should be undertaken promptly to weaken the economic life of Cuba" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI*, Document 499, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d499). From 1969 Cuba is a managed problem, and by 1977 the Treasury could tell President Carter that "our leverage in economic terms is quite modest" even on sugar (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean*, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d21). The set that carries this account is 23 documents, chosen from about 4,900 in the editors' own Cuba chapters and about 13,900 that mention Cuba anywhere, among the 553 volumes of the library (see the memo for every exact count).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *The words 'national security' as a description of Cuba's importance before the 1940s.* I searched all 553 volumes for any document mentioning Cuba or Havana together with the phrase. Of the nine documents returned from before 1940, eight print the phrase and none is about Cuba's bearing on American security (they concern a legal code, wartime bond and agriculture acts, the Paris peace conference, arms reduction and armaments, and a Colombian coast guard; the ninth is returned by the index's word matching but does not print the phrase). In the editors' Cuba chapters the phrase is absent in the 1900s, 1920s and 1930s, appears once in the 1910s (a bond act) and in almost none of the 1940s. The earlier thinking has to be read under other names, such as independence, intervention, defence and commerce, which is why the findings below rest on reading documents. Two controls ran in the same pass: a search for 'department of state' returned documents in every decade, and a search for a nonsense string returned none in any decade; both were decade-level controls, not volume by volume. (b) *'National security' and 'prosperity' as measures of Cuba in particular.* The phrase appears in the documents that mention Cuba at about the same rate as in all documents of the decade (about one in three in the 1960s in both), and 'prosperity' (about 450 Cuba documents) runs only a little above its rate in the series as a whole, so neither word shows a change in thinking about Cuba and neither is used as evidence. (c) *Anything after January 1989.* The last two documents that mention Cuba are dated January 1989. (d) *The nineteenth century beyond a handful of documents.* The early volumes are arranged by correspondent, with no Cuba chapters; the 1895–1900 Cuba material sits inside the chapters on Spain, which the editors' Cuba chapters used here do not include, and the findings for the 1870s and 1880s rest on three documents. The shelf that holds this material is microfilmed Record Group 59: Despatches from United States Ministers to Spain, 1792–1906 (134 rolls) and to Cuba, 1902–1906 (18 rolls); Despatches from United States Consuls in Havana, 1783–1906 (133 rolls), with smaller runs for Santiago de Cuba, Matanzas and Cienfuegos; and Notes from the Cuban Legation in the United States, 1844–1906 (2 rolls). The bundled data gives their NARA identifiers but no access status. (e) *Numbers about trade and investment.* This run extracted no trade statistics; it found one investment total, which is the Cuban ambassador's claim rather than an American estimate, and one claims total. (f) *Cuban and Spanish views, Congress and public opinion*, which appear only as American officials describe them.

**Scope.** The findings draw on the library's 553 volumes (about 311,000 documents once front matter, editors' notes and two later editions of earlier volumes are set aside), read in two ways that are never mixed: the editors' own Cuba chapters (about 4,900 documents in 62 volumes, 1900–1980) and every document that mentions Cuba or Havana (about 13,900 in 481 volumes). Twenty-three documents are cited, and nothing before 1874 or after 1981 is used for a finding.

## Decisions this needs from you

1. **What counts as Cuba's 'impact'.** Counting every document that mentions Cuba reaches back to the 1870s but includes conferences held at Havana and passing mentions (about three in five of those documents mention Cuba fewer than five times), whereas the editors' Cuba chapters are cleaner but begin in 1900 and reflect the editors' twentieth-century choices. Meanwhile I took the documents the argument rests on from the chapters where they exist and from word searches where they do not, and nothing else.
2. **Whether 'national security' is a phrase or a concept.** Read as a phrase, it enters the Cuba chapters in the 1950s and is nearly universal by the 1980s, but it tracks the whole series, so it dates the vocabulary of the period and not a change in thinking about Cuba; read as a concept, it has to be found under 'independence', 'intervention', 'defence' and 'commerce', and the earlier periods become matters of judgement. Meanwhile I read the concept and reported the phrase only as a caution.
3. **Whose prosperity.** American prosperity (sugar supply, markets, capital, tariffs) gives one history and Cuban prosperity (the standard of living of the Cuban people, which the 1934 instruction mentions) gives another, and the 1934 instruction is about both, so the choice decides how that document is read. Meanwhile I followed the American side and noted the Cuban side where the officials did.
4. **Who counts as a policymaker.** Presidents, Secretaries of State, envoys, the Joint Chiefs and the intelligence director all speak in this set, so restricting it to the President and Secretary of State would drop several of the sharpest statements (the 1897 despatch, the 1960 sugar plan, the 1962 transcript, which come from envoys, officials and the Defense Secretary), while widening it to Congress or the sugar interests is not possible in FRUS. Meanwhile I counted any American official speaking for the government and treated each speaker's role as part of the evidence.

## Findings

### 1. A rich neighbour that should stay out of European hands, 1874–1886

In the earliest documents Cuba matters because it is close and because it trades. Secretary of State Fish's instructions of 6 February 1874 call the island "the largest insular possession still retained by any European power in America" and say it is "almost contiguous to the United States"; he concludes that "the state of Cuba is the one great cause of perpetual solicitude in the foreign relations of the United States" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 7, 1874*, Document 550 (index number 552), https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552). The aim is independence, not annexation: Cuba "ought to belong to the great family of American republics". In 1881 Blaine, using Cuba as a model for Hawaii, called it "the key to the Gulf of Mexico, and the field for our most extended trade", part of "the American commercial system" though in Spanish hands (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 5, 1881*, Document 405, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d405); and a despatch of 22 October 1886 gives the earliest figure in this collection, that the United States "buys about 95 per cent, of the sugar exported from Cuba" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, Transmitted to Congress, With the Annual Message of the President, December 6, 1886*, Document 414, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1886/d414). On this evidence the language of security is geographic and commercial here, not strategic in the later sense. The caution is the size of the evidence: three documents, and about three in ten of the decade's Cuba mentions come from the single volume of 1874.

### 2. The sugar bowl and the case for war, 1897–1898

The last year before the war with Spain sets out the American stake more fully than any earlier document. Minister Woodford, in a despatch of 5 October 1897, says "the United States does not seek to annex Cuba or to establish a protectorate over Cuba", but describes the island's sugar as essential: "the sugar plantations of Cuba are as necessary to the food supply of the United States as are the cotton fields of India" (with further comparisons to Egypt and Russia in the full sentence), adding that "our native cane-sugar crop, raised mostly in Louisiana and Texas, does not furnish more than one-tenth of the sugar we consume" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 5, 1898*, Document 498, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/d498). The President's message to Congress, dated 12 April 1898 in the library, mixes the two kinds of reasons: "The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace, and entails upon this Government an enormous expense", and says the "right to intervene may be justified by the very serious injury to the commerce, trade, and business of our people, and by the wanton destruction of property and devastation of the island" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 5, 1898*, the President's message on Cuba, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). By the library's count about one document in thirteen mentions Cuba in the 1890s, the highest of any decade before the 1960s, but about two in five of those come from the 1898 volume alone, so the rate marks one episode.

### 3. Security and prosperity written into one treaty system, 1904–1934

After 1898 the two aims are put in the same instruments. The Platt Amendment, as the 1904 proclamation reprints it, has Cuba consent "that the United States may exercise the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence", and provides that "to enable the United States to maintain the independence of Cuba, and to protect the people thereof, as well as for its own defense" Cuba will sell or lease "lands necessary for coaling or naval stations" (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, With the Annual Message of the President Transmitted to Congress December 6, 1904*, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d207). In Cuba-mentioning documents of the 1900s, about one in seven use the words of intervention, protectorate or the Platt Amendment, against about one in twenty-five of all documents of that decade. The trade side runs alongside: in the 1930s nearly one in four Cuba-mentioning documents discuss sugar, tariffs or reciprocity, against about one in thirteen of all documents, and in 1929 the Cuban ambassador told the Secretary of State that nearly 1,500 million dollars of American capital were invested in Cuba, which is Cuba's own figure (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1929, Volume II*, Document 924, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1929v02/d924). Intervention remained a live obligation: Ambassador Welles wrote in August 1933 that "the purpose of my mission here is to avoid the existence of a situation which would give rise to intervention by the United States" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 299, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d299), and Secretary Stimson had told reporters in October 1930 that the Platt Amendment was about preserving independence and not a protectorate over internal affairs (*Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume II*, Document 747, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v02/d747). Then in July 1934 Secretary Hull's instruction pairs the two: the new treaty, "eliminating the permissive right of intervention by this Government, has removed a chronic source of friction", and the sugar concession is "a further indication of the desire of this Government to assist the Cuban people in regaining an adequate and satisfactory standard of living" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V*, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d127).

### 4. Hemisphere defence, bases and sovereignty, 1941–1958

The Second World War makes Cuba a defence problem, and the new language meets a sovereignty argument. In the editors' Cuba chapters the words defence or defense appear in about one document in six in the 1940s, against fewer than one in a hundred in the 1930s. In July 1941 the State Department objected to the Navy's draft for enlarging the Guantánamo station on the ground that the "principle of leased areas over which the United States forces exercise unquestioned jurisdiction could not be applied to other countries of the Hemisphere" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1941, The American Republics, Volume VII*, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v07/d119). In December 1944 the Joint Chiefs wanted a defence facility in western Cuba under exclusive American jurisdiction (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII*, Document 878, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d878), and in January 1945 President Grau refused any lease of the bases, as a matter of sovereignty (*Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX*, Document 741, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d741). Prosperity is now argued as strategy: sugar appears in about two in five of the chapter documents of the 1940s, and in June 1954 the State Department opposed a cut in Cuba's share of the American market because "Cuba has been a reliable expansible source of sugar in both war and peace, a strategic concept which should not be jeopardized" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV*, Document 342, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d342). By June 1958 the State Department and the Joint Chiefs tied internal stability to defence: "If the security of the nation is destroyed or an avenue is opened up for leftist exploitation, this would certainly weaken the nation’s stability and affect hemispheric defense" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI*, Document 70, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d70).

### 5. Communism, missiles and economic denial, 1959–1963

After Castro the threat is defined by communism and weapons, and prosperity is turned from a shared interest into an instrument. In March 1959 the Director of Central Intelligence described Castro as an instigator "actually supporting Communist-dominated revolutionary groups" against other governments in the region, in a document that never uses the word Cuba and was found only by similarity of meaning (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, American Republics, Volume V*, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05/d112). By April 1960 the policy is economic: "every possible means should be undertaken promptly to weaken the economic life of Cuba", and the "principal item in our economic quiver would be flexible authority in the sugar legislation" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI*, Document 499, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d499). Words for communism, the Soviet Union, missiles and subversion appear in about one in twenty of the chapter documents of the 1930s, one in three of the 1950s and nearly two in three of the 1960s; but they were also in about half of all documents of the 1960s, so the lift for Cuba is real but moderate (nearly three in four Cuba-mentioning documents against half). The security case is less settled than the vocabulary suggests. In the first meeting on the missiles, on 16 October 1962, the question "How gravely does this change the strategic balance?" draws a reply from Defense Secretary McNamara: he had asked the Chiefs, and "they said, substantially. My own personal view is, not at all" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath*, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d21). Four days later, as the minutes report, the President held that fifty planes in Cuba did not affect the balance of power but that the missiles were different, with "an entirely different impact throughout Latin America" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath*, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d34). Afterwards the stated aim narrows. Bundy told President Johnson on 15 December 1963: "The bare minimum objective of our policy is a Cuba which poses no threat to its neighbors and which is not a Soviet satellite", pursued by "intensifying Cuba’s already-serious economic difficulties; increasing the cost and unpleasantness to the Soviets of maintaining Cuba" and encouraging internal resistance (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics; Cuba 1961–1962; Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath*, Document 729, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d729).

### 6. A managed problem with modest economic leverage, 1969–1981

The last period treats Cuba as a double problem to be contained. A July 1969 interdepartmental study says "Cuba has been a double-barreled problem for the U.S.: —as a base for Soviet military power, and —as an exporter of revolutionary doctrine and violence to Latin America", and ranks preventing strategic weapons as the only vital interest (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–10, Documents on American Republics, 1969–1972*, Document 205, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d205). The phrase 'national security' is now common in the Cuba chapters (about two in three of the documents of the 1970s and nearly nine in ten of the 1980s), while sugar, the 1940s' strategic concept, appears in only about one in ten of the 1970s and none of the 44 documents of the 1980s. In August 1977 the Treasury advised President Carter on normalisation that the "political significance of both sides of the trade issue far outweighs economic effects", that, even on sugar, "our leverage in economic terms is quite modest", and that claims of about $2 billion might be settled for at least $600 million ("the usual 30%") (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean*, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d21). The 1962 understanding stays the benchmark: a January 1981 memorandum says that the "intent of the 1962 Understanding was to prevent Cuba from becoming a threat to the security of the US—and to countries friendly to us in the Hemisphere" (*Foreign Relations of the United States, 1981–1988, Volume III, Soviet Union, January 1981–January 1983*, Document 5, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v03/d5). Taken with the earlier findings, prosperity moves from a stake officials listed in 1897, through a joint treaty interest in 1904 and 1934, to a weapon in 1960 and a lever the Treasury judged weak in 1977.

## How this was done

I worked from a working copy of the app's search index taken on 25 October 2026 that holds all 553 volumes, setting aside front matter, editors' notes and the two second editions of earlier volumes (about 311,000 documents remain). I defined two sets, every document matching the words Cuba, Cuban, Cubans, Havana or Habana, and the documents under the editors' own Cuba chapter headings, and kept them apart. The index matches on word stems, so a search for 'tariff' also finds 'tariffs'; each group of words used for a comparison was therefore checked against the printed text and, allowing for ordinary inflection, at least 96 in 100 of the documents matched in each of the three groups used actually print one of the words. Comparisons are rates by decade against the same words in all documents of the decade, with the first-ranked results of searches by era read, and a vocabulary count taken over every document of the editors' chapters. Positive and negative control searches ran in every decade. Two documents were found by similarity of meaning and by no word search, and the reprint of Blaine's 1881 despatch in an 1894 appendix volume was set aside as a duplicate. The underlying XML text of the volumes was not consulted, no count here distinguishes a footnote from the body, and the nineteenth-century evidence is thin. All 23 collection documents were retrieved whole and parts of each were read; all 35 passages the run kept were checked word for word against the document text. Document numbers follow the library's index; the one case where the memo records a difference from the printed number is the 1874 instruction. Dates are the index's own. I checked that every quotation in this overview is one the memo verified against its document, and that every document it cites is in the collection.
