# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From 1877 to 1973 the State Department applied three tests to a Latin American regime that took power by force. Did it control the country? Did its people accept it, or at least not resist? Would it honor its international obligations? (1900, Document 530; 1948, Volume IX, Document 7). At times it added a constitutional test (1917, Document 379) or a treaty bar on coup leaders in Central America (1931, Volume II, Document 232). Before recognizing, it asked for particular things. Nicaragua was to sign a loan convention (1911, Document 933), Mexico a property treaty (1921, Volume II, Document 344), Peru to guarantee elections (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 423), and Argentina to cooperate against the Axis (1944, Volume VII, Document 226). From 1948 its planners called non-recognition a poor lever (1948, Volume IX, Document 115). This rests on 56 documents I selected from the editors' recognition chapters (about 2,700 documents) and from about 450 later ones found by search.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as recognition.** The United States sometimes resumed or suspended relations without using the word, and the 1948 Bogotá resolution avoids it (1948, Volume IX, Document 89); counting resumed relations adds cases such as Argentina in 1945 (1945, Volume IX, Document 254), while counting formal recognition alone drops them.
- **What counts as force.** I took anything the editors headed a revolution, coup or overthrow. A stricter rule, such as only the overthrow of an elected government, would shrink the set by an amount I did not measure.
- **What counts as Latin America.** I included Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean republics including Cuba, Haiti, Panama and the Dominican Republic, and South America; dropping Mexico would remove the cases of 1913 to 1923, most of which I did not read beyond the policy statements.
- **Where the story begins.** I started the close reading in 1877; if you want the earlier nineteenth century, the 1861 to 1894 volumes need a reading that I have not done.

Until you decide, my rule is this. A document belongs if it sits under a chapter whose editor-written title names recognition, non-recognition, a revolution, coup or overthrow, or a recognition decision for a Latin American government.

## Findings

### 1. The test was control, assent and obligations, and it was already old in 1877

In 1877 Acting Secretary Seward told the minister in Mexico to wait before recognizing Díaz, and he wanted a guarantee of frontier order as a condition rather than a hope (1877, Document 222). Ten months later Evarts authorized recognition. His instruction records that

> the Government of the United States before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico waited to be assured that his administration was approved by the Mexican people, and was possessed of due stability and animated by a disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties. (1878, Document 344)

The rule is stated more fully in a statement of 1900 written for Colombia: the United States defers recognition until the new executive is "in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority," and can meet "all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state" (1900, Document 530). The phrase "de facto", meaning in fact, appears in about one chapter document in fifteen.

The test sat beside requests. In 1911 the United States had already recognized Estrada's government in Nicaragua as the de facto authority, and the instruction to the new minister listed what that government was to do:

> The Government of Nicaragua is to proceed at the earliest possible date to the signature of a convention with the United States which shall authorize the contemplated bankers’ loan contract, the terms of the latter to be approved thereafter by both Governments, and to make provision, among other things, for the service of the loan secured by a certain percentage of the customs receipts of the Republic collected in a manner satisfactory to both Governments. (1911, Document 933)

### 2. From 1913 the Department added a constitutional test and asked for elections and treaties

Wilson's circular to the Latin American missions said cooperation needed orderly government "based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force" (1913, Document 7). In August 1913 the proposals carried to Mexico included an early free election and Huerta's pledge not to stand in it, and they were refused (1913, Document 1028). In 1917 Lansing told the minister in Costa Rica that

> the desire which this Government has of seeing the will of the people prevail in governmental matters in Costa Rica has forced it to the conclusion that no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition. (1917, Document 379)

After the Bolivian coup of 1920 the Department did not expect satisfactory evidence before the elections announced for December (1920, Volume I, Document 387). In January 1921 it recognized Bolivia "not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic" (1921, Volume I, Document 238). In June 1921 Hughes made recognition of Mexico depend on a treaty:

> In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place. (1921, Volume II, Document 344)

Recognition could also be withheld in form while business went on. In July 1925 Kellogg told Ecuador's chargé that dealings continued but did not amount to recognition of the new regime as other than the de facto authorities (1925, Volume II, Document 54). Full recognition came three years later (1928, Volume II, Document 741).

### 3. A treaty rule for Central America was enforced, then abandoned

In June 1923 Hughes wrote that the United States was "in the most hearty accord" with Article II of the Central American treaty of that year (1923, Volume II, Document 345). The Secretary of State's statement of September 1930 explains the exception for those republics and sets out the rule:

> will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country. (1930, Volume I, Document 375)

In December 1931 the Department applied it: "The United States Government, therefore, will not recognize General Martínez as President of Salvador" (1931, Volume II, Document 232). In January 1934, with El Salvador and Costa Rica having denounced the treaty, Welles proposed a procedure for recognizing El Salvador (1934, Volume V, Document 199). Guatemala, Nicaragua and Honduras recognized Martínez on 25 January. Welles asked to act the next day, so that the United States would be seen to act independently (1934, Volume V, Document 255). Hull then instructed formal recognition (1934, Volume V, Document 257), just over two years after the refusal. In April 1936 the Department decided to stop following the treaty:

> the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America, nor should it endeavor to utilize that Treaty as justification for any other action it may take or fail to take (1936, Volume V, Document 112)

A memorandum of the same month judged that the United States would recognize Somoza if his regime governed effectively and met its obligations, whatever the treaty said, but feared that announcing the change would invite revolt (1936, Volume V, Document 109).

### 4. Cuba in 1934 and the wartime test of Axis influence

In November 1933 the Department named its criteria for a Cuban government: popular support and ability to keep law and order (1933, Volume V, Document 508). In January 1934 the President decided that no person or group could be promised recognition in advance:

> This Government will recognize a Cuban provisional Government which is substantially supported by the Cuban people and is able to maintain law, order and the normal functions of Government. (1934, Volume V, Document 85)

Nine days later the Department instructed formal recognition of the government of Cuba (1934, Volume V, Document 103).

In the war a further question was added. After the Argentine coup of 1944, Hull relayed a British suggestion that Perón be told the road would be open to reconsidering recognition if there were collaboration against Axis activities, new cabinet ministers and a date for elections (1944, Volume VII, Document 226). In January 1944 Hull told the other republics that the evidence of Nazi and Argentine involvement in the Bolivian coup was very strong and that recognition was unlikely (1944, Volume VII, Document 388). On 9 April 1945 the United States announced that it was resuming relations with the Argentine Government (1945, Volume IX, Document 254). In February 1945 a Department officer explained the practice to the ambassador in El Salvador:

> There are certain well established requirements of international law for recognition: (a) Control of the machinery of Government; (b) General popular support without active opposition; and (c) willingness and ability to observe international obligations. Due to the war, the Committee for Political Defense of Montevideo established a fourth—whereby when a Government came into power through force, the countries of the hemisphere would consult to determine whether its assumption to power demonstrated direct or indirect Axis influence. (1945, Volume IX, Document 883)

### 5. In 1948 the Department separated recognition from approval and from leverage

A Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948, approved by the Secretary, set the standard:

> The recognition policy of the Department should be based upon the three following factors, any or all of which would be applied in a given case according to U.S. national interests: (a) de facto control of the territory and the administrative machinery of State, including the maintenance of public order; (b) the ability and willingness of a government to discharge its international obligations; (c) general acquiescence of the people of a country in the government in power. (1948, Volume IX, Document 7)

On Venezuela in December 1948 the Policy Planning memorandum said that "non-recognition is not a suitable weapon for influencing the course of internal events in Venezuela at this time" (1948, Volume IX, Document 115). The Army separately argued that oil supply and the American military mission favored recognition (1948, Volume IX, Document 118). In December 1949 the Department had Panama's government give assurances on treaties, majority support and control of territory before it recognized the regime (1949, Volume II, Document 450). By May 1951 the standard was routine. Acheson told Truman that the Bolivian junta

> has met the criteria which the United States has applied in other recent cases involving the recognition of Latin American governments which have come to power through irregular procedures. (1951, Volume II, Document 688)

The memorandum names the junta's authority with substantial acquiescence, its declared intent to honor obligations, and the absence of external influence.

### 6. From prompt recognition to conditions and back, 1955 to 1973

In the 1950s recognition came quickly: Colombia's junta in May 1957 (1955–1957, Volume VII, Document 465) and Cuba's provisional government on the day it was recommended, 7 January 1959 (1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 217). In 1962 and 1963 the Department set conditions. After the Peruvian coup of 1962 it listed what it wanted:

> 1. Guarantee of continued civil liberties. 2. Guarantee of free and fair elections, preferably before June 9 but at no later date, with full rights to all parties under Constitution, and with guarantee that Armed Forces will respect results those elections. 3. Offer by Junta, as indication of good faith, to invite OAS observers during electoral processes. (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 423)

The same telegram notes that the United States had waited three weeks in the Argentine case. In October 1963 it told the Dominican regime that it would not recognize or deal with it as it stood (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 359). By December it judged its prospects of further influence through non-recognition "virtually nil" (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 363). A footnote dates the non-recognition of Honduras from 3 October to 14 December 1963, ten weeks (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 72). For Bolivia in December 1964, Harriman recommended recognition, and the new leaders had privately pledged to reduce communist influence and not to restore relations with Cuba or Czechoslovakia (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 152).

After the Argentine coup of June 1966 the Department suspended relations (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 132). In July it asked for standby authority to recognize, and it treated the criteria of the Organization of American States as a further test:

> The classical criteria for recognition (i.e., general control of the country and pledge to honor international obligations) have been met. We are still awaiting public affirmation of the OAS criteria: respect for human rights, peaceful settlement of disputes, and eventual return to constitutional government. (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 139)

After the Chilean coup of 1973 the line was that "we recognize governments, not heads of government, and that question of recognition has not arisen"; a State Department official also said that the biggest errors in recent years had come from too hasty recognition of military regimes, Brazil among them (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 348).

## How this was done

I searched an index of the 553 volumes' documents, a searchable copy that sets aside editors' notes and front matter. I used the editors' own chapter headings as the scope. I took every document under a chapter whose title names recognition or non-recognition, a revolution, coup or overthrow, or a recognition decision for a Latin American government, which gave about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes. From 1949 onward I searched for documents that join recognition with a junta, coup, revolutionary or de facto government, and I read the ones that carry a decision. The search treats "recognize" and "recognition" as the same word, so I tested the word against its context. About three of every eight chapter documents contain a word starting "recogn". So do about one in ten of the other documents in the same volumes. The term marks the topic but is not a clean marker. The phrase "international obligations" turns up in about a hundred chapter documents, nearly all in exactly that form. Controls were a phrase every volume should contain, which was found in all 41 chapter volumes, and a nonsense string, which was found nowhere. I read in full 55 of the 56 documents in the collection and part of the other, whose long enclosure I did not read; I checked every quotation in this overview against the text of its document. All figures come from the index, not from the volumes' source files, so I ran no scan for spelling variants.
