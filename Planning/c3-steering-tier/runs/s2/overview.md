# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** FRUS shows corruption entering American policy in two distinct ways. In the first, corruption in a client government became a reason Washington gave for withholding, conditioning or redesigning aid, and a standing subject of pressure on the client's leader: Nationalist China between 1944 and 1948, South Vietnam under Diem in 1960 and again under Thieu and Ky in 1967, and South Korea in 1961. In the second, from 1975, it was American companies' payments abroad, above all Lockheed's, that put foreign governments friendly to the United States at risk. Washington then had to manage the fallout with Saudi Arabia, Japan and West Germany, and it went on to propose international rules and to live with a domestic law against bribery. Between the two lie episodes in which bribery was an instrument: the British complaint in 1956 that Saudi money was buying Jordanian officials, an American admiral's 1959 suggestion that bribery might serve in Iraq, and a 1965 charge in Iran that Washington had bribed the Iranian government. The words for corruption, bribery, graft and embezzlement appear in about one document in a hundred across the 553 volumes of the series. The argument rests on 19 documents retrieved whole and read, among them Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 908 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d908); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 131 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d131); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 202 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d202); and Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 161 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d161).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Series the printed documents came from:* the Department of State's Central Decimal File (Record Group 59, National Archives at College Park; the China telegrams and the 1960 Vietnam telegrams are filed there under country and subject numbers; the bundled finding aids give no series identifier for it); the Marshall Mission Files (Record Group 59, Lot 54 D 270), which the National Archives arranged as ten series under the Office of the Special Envoy to China (creator identifier 10481258; for example Cables Received, identifier 872088, and Records Relating to Political Affairs, identifier 872091; no dates or access status in the bundled data); the Conference Files (Record Group 59, Lot 62 D 181, a probable match by name: series identifier 602875, entry A1 3051B, 1949–1972, unrestricted, College Park); the Transcripts of Secretary of State Kissinger's Staff Meetings (Record Group 59, Entry 5177, formerly Lot 78 D 433; no identifier reached); the Records of Secretary of State Henry Kissinger (Record Group 59, Entry 5403; not resolved); and presidential library files that the bundled data does not resolve to identifiers: Johnson Library National Security File, Vietnam; Kennedy Library President's Office Files and National Security Files, Korea; Ford Library National Security Adviser files, including Presidential Correspondence with Foreign Leaders, Japan; and Carter Library White House Central Files and Brzezinski Material. One CIA document came from CIA files (Job 79-T00472A). *Series the editors' footnotes cite without printing:* the Bureau of Far Eastern Affairs files, Lot 62 D 26 (Record Group 59, *Subject, Personal Name, and Country Files*, identifier 1965953, entry A1 3113, 1960–1963, partly restricted on national-security grounds, College Park); the Saigon Embassy Files, Lot 65 F 115, file 350.1, *Political Parties—Can Lao* (Record Group 84; not resolved to an identifier); the Jidda Embassy Files, Lot 79 F 80, file *POL Northrop and Lockheed* (Record Group 84; not resolved); further Central Decimal File numbers for Vietnam and Korea; and the Eisenhower Library's Herter Papers, Iraq. The first list is the documents' origin and the second is what the footnotes point at. They are different channels, and no count here adds them together.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *After 1980.* A search for the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act by name found four documents, all from 1977–80, and none in the thirteen 1981–88 volumes indexed. Relaxing it to "corrupt practices" found one document in those volumes, and the general corruption words fifty-nine. The post-1980 record of how the law shaped diplomacy is mostly unpublished. (b) *The congressional investigations themselves.* The Church subcommittee is named in 42 documents, but only three join it to bribery or payments; its own records are the Senate's, not the State Department's, and this run did not resolve them to a shelf. (c) *Who took the money.* In the Lockheed telegrams the recipients' names are still withheld as not declassified. The editors cite the Jidda embassy's Lockheed file (Lot 79 F 80) as the shelf, and this run could not resolve it to a series. (d) *Bribery in consular and fee matters* (27 documents in 21 volumes join bribery to consuls and fees), *bribery as an extraditable crime*, and the nineteenth-century record, where the subject is about as dense as in the 1960s, are in the corpus but not followed here. Most of that early material has no shelf in the bundled finding aids: only eleven of the nearly seven hundred series they describe start before 1940. Each search ran with a positive check (the phrase "Department of State", found in 550 of 551 volumes) and an impossible-word check (found nowhere) in the same pass.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the index, minus two second editions whose first editions are present, and minus front matter and editorial notes: about 311,000 documents, of which 3,036 use the words (3,020 once sixteen documents printed twice are counted once). From that set, 19 documents were chosen and read under the rule below; every count is conditional on this index copy (see the memo).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Corruption of whom?** Foreign governments' corruption as an American policy problem (used here), or also American officials' and companies' own conduct. The second widens the set to consular-fee and procurement cases and to the 1970s payments, which I kept only where a foreign government's dealings with Washington turned on them.
- **Allegation or established fact?** I counted an allegation once it entered a U.S. decision or démarche, true or not; Durbrow in 1960 pressed Diem on reports he could not prove. Requiring proof would shrink the set to almost nothing, since FRUS rarely adjudicates.
- **Bribery as an instrument of statecraft.** Kept as one finding (Saudi money, a U.S. admiral's suggestion, a charge against Washington). If you treat covert payments as outside "corruption", that finding drops, and covert-action volumes would need a separate search.
- **Period.** I worked from 1944 to 1980, where the documents show decisions. Extending to the nineteenth century brings in a large but different literature (claims, extradition, Hawaii in 1893) that is listed as unfollowed.

Inclusion rule used meanwhile: a document enters when it records corruption or an allegation of it entering a U.S. decision, a U.S. representation to a foreign government, or a foreign government's dealings with Washington, and I have read it whole.

## Findings

### 1. In China, corruption became Washington's stated reason for reluctance on aid (1944–1948)

The American embassy reported Nationalist corruption as a threat to the regime itself. Marshall told T. V. Soong on leaving China that reform depended on rooting it out. When the Foreign Minister asked for a billion-dollar loan in May 1947, Ambassador Stuart answered with American opinion about corruption. The Chinese government in turn blamed American reporting for its standing in the United States. The 1947 China volume holds more documents using the words (71) than any other volume in the series.
- Gauss, September 1944: "the two departments in which mismanagement and corruption are most flagrant and most dangerous to the continuance of the Government itself are the Ministries of Food and War" — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, China, Volume VI, Document 148 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v06/d148).
- Stuart, May 1947, on the loan request: "I referred to a widespread feeling in United States that the present Government is controlled largely by reactionary elements and that there is much corruption and graft throughout the system." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 908 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d908).
- Marshall to Soong, January 1947, in the minutes of their meeting: an opposition party would let Chiang "wipe out graft, corruption and incompetence in the government and in the Kuomintang." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The Far East: China, Volume X, Document 373 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v10/d373). Also cited: the Chinese information chief's complaint that American reporters "play up the cases, making the US public believe that the entire Chinese Government is corrupt", Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 43 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d43).

### 2. In South Vietnam, the allegations became leverage on aid, and then a priority of the war effort (1960, 1967)

In April 1960 Ambassador Durbrow tied Congress's concern over the Can Lao party's alleged rackets to the next year's aid. Washington warned Diem's minister that rumours, "even if untrue", could hurt if aid funds seemed involved. By November 1967 the senior officials' informal group put an anti-corruption campaign first on its list of priorities for the coming year, partly for its effect on opinion at home.
- Durbrow to Diem: "it might prove difficult obtain adequate aid FY 61 if disturbing reports continue re alleged corrupt practices Can Lao party" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 131 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d131).
- The Vietnam desk officer to Thuan, as recorded in his memorandum of conversation, which the editors quote: "there is a risk that such rumors, even if untrue, might be picked up and exaggerated … particularly if there were any implication that US aid funds were involved, even indirectly" — same volume, Document 135 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d135).
- Katzenbach to Johnson: "There was unanimous agreement that a visible and credible anti-corruption effort would help a great deal in improving the Administration’s position, and in building a more effective GVN." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 395 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d395).

### 3. In South Korea, an aid official's report on corruption reached the President and reopened policy (1961)

Hugh Farley, who had resigned from the aid mission over its stand on corruption in Korea and its operations, wrote that it endangered the whole American position. Within nine days Rostow told Kennedy that everyone agreed Korea policy needed a fresh look. Rostow's memorandum does not use any of the words for corruption. It was found by its similarity to Farley's report, by no word search, and read before it was cited.
- Farley: "they seriously endanger the whole U.S. position in Korea, and threaten to discredit, not only the aid program in Korea, but the Mutual Security program as a whole" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 202 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d202).
- Rostow: "The underlying fact is that all hands agree the situation in Korea is not good; and American policy in Korea requires a fresh look." — same volume, Document 204 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d204).

### 4. Bribery as an instrument, by rivals, proposed in Washington, and charged against it (1956–1965)

The British told Eisenhower's team that Saudi money was buying Jordanian officials and weakening the Baghdad Pact's prospects. In 1959 the Chief of Naval Operations floated bribery as an option against Qasim's Iraq. In 1965 a military credit bill sent to the Iranian parliament just after the status-of-forces bill gave rise to charges that the United States had bought the immunities.
- Lloyd, January 1956: "The fact was, however, that high Government officials were being bribed by the Saudis and the British could not be certain what would happen." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Near East: Jordan-Yemen, Volume XIII, Document 16 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v13/d16).
- Burke to Herter: his note to Allen Dulles raised oil purchases and prices, and "the possibility of raising the prices, if bribery is more useful than reduction of Kassem’s income" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Near East Region; Iraq; Iran; Arabian Peninsula, Volume XII, Document 168 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v12/d168).
- CIA, April 1965: the timing gave "rise to charges that Washington had bribed the Iranian Government to grant widespread immunities to US personnel" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXII, Iran, Document 79 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v22/d79).

### 5. American companies' payments abroad became a crisis of alliance management (1975–1976)

Once Senate hearings exposed Lockheed's payments, the State Department faced allied governments whose own standing was at stake. About two dozen documents in seven volumes, dated 1973–1977, deal with the payments rather than with aircraft sales (see the memo). Saudi Arabia's defence minister demanded that the names never come out. Kissinger, in his staff meeting, doubted that the Saudis wanted to change their practices, and he told Ford privately that the payments to German and Dutch figures should be kept quiet. Japan's Prime Minister asked for disclosure as a matter of Japanese democracy.
- Ambassador Akins, September 1975: "Sultan said the Department or President Ford must do whatever is necessary to make sure the names are never divulged." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 161 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d161). Also Document 152 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d152), where Kissinger says of the Saudis: "They don’t want to change. They are being forced to change by us, and they are going to go to other suppliers."
- Kissinger to Ford, 8 October 1975, as quoted by the editors: "We must do everything possible to keep this quiet." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Documents on Western Europe, 1973–1976, Document 293 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d293).
- Miki to Ford, February 1976: "if the whole issue is kept unsolved with the names of the officials involved remaining in doubt, democracy in Japan may suffer a fatal blow" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 218 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d218).

### 6. From scandal to rules: an international agreement and the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1976–1980)

Ford answered Miki by offering to share the Securities and Exchange Commission's evidence and by asking Japan to back an American proposal for an international agreement. By 1978 the State Department judged that a consensus on such an agreement was forming at the United Nations. By 1980 the Commerce Secretary and the Trade Representative reported that exporters ranked the new law the second most significant disincentive to exporting. The law's name appears in only four documents, all in 1977–80 volumes.
- Ford to Miki: "I hope the Government of Japan can support the United States proposal for an international agreement to curtail such practices" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 219 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d219).
- State Department paper, June 1978: "Consensus for international agreement on illicit payments appears to be developing in UN working group on corrupt practices." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 308 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d308).
- Klutznick and Askew, July 1980: "some exports are lost because foreign companies can bribe foreign officials while U.S. companies cannot" — same volume, Document 248 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d248).

## How this was done

I searched the full text of the series' 553 volumes for the words for corruption, bribery, graft, kickbacks and embezzlement. The index treats "corruption", "corrupt" and "corrupted" as the same word, so I tested a sample of forty matching documents against the literal wording. All forty contained a form of the words, and 37 used them for official or financial wrongdoing. I measured how often the words appear in each decade against the number of dated documents in that decade: about three in every thousand documents in the 1930s and about twenty in the 1960s and 1970s. The editors gave the subject a chapter heading only once, so the threads came from the documents' text, probed volume by volume (China, Vietnam, Korea, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Central America, Africa, Lockheed, the 1977 law). Phrase searches were checked against the literal text of every hit. "Questionable payments" turned out to be unusable, because the index cannot tell "questionable" from "question". I retrieved each cited document whole and read it (in the six longest, the passages around the subject rather than every line) and checked every quotation word for word against its document. For each finding I also compared the document I rested it on with the documents most similar to it across the whole series, and read the closest ones that a word search would miss; one of them, the Rostow memorandum in Finding 3, entered the argument. A check for documents printed twice found sixteen, which are counted once. The archival sources come from the documents' own source notes and footnotes, resolved through the app's bundled finding aids as far as they reach. Footnote and editorial language is part of the text searched, so the decade figures mix the documents' words with the editors'.

Checks made before finishing: every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was verified in the memo against its document's text, word for word (19 of 19), and every document this overview cites is in the collection (19 documents).

## Read next

The collection "Corruption and U.S. foreign policy — FRUS, 1944–1980" holds the 19 documents under the six findings, each with a note and its quoted passage, and ends with the archival sources block. It would gain most from your additions in four places. First, any request to the National Archives for the Saigon embassy's Can Lao file (Lot 65 F 115) and the Jidda embassy's Lockheed file (Lot 79 F 80), which would show what the embassies knew beyond the printed telegrams. Second, a decision on whether American covert payments count, which would open the intelligence-heavy volumes. Third, the threads not followed here, each with documents already identified: Somoza and the Nicaraguan crisis of 1978–79, Mobutu's Zaire in 1977–78, Costa Rica and the Vesco affair in 1976, Iran under Zahedi in 1953–54, and the Philippine election of 1953, which the ambassador feared would be won by corrupt practices. Fourth, the nineteenth-century record (Hawaii in 1893, claims and extradition), where the subject is about as frequent as in the 1960s.
