# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows corruption entering U.S. decisions in four ways that recur across the series. First, a client government's corruption served as a reason to limit aid or attach conditions to it. In November 1948 the Secretary of State gave the "incompetent and corrupt officials" holding high office in the Chinese National Government as one reason against all-out aid (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 506, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d506). The Ambassador had proposed a year earlier to release aid in stages and stop it "whenever the forces of reaction or of corruption assert themselves" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d292). Second, U.S. officials in South Vietnam treated corruption as an obstacle to pacification, but declined to take the control over Saigon's appointments that a quick cure would have needed (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258). Third, when American firms' bribes to foreign officials came out in 1975–76, the Department and the White House handled the disclosures as a threat to friendly governments in West Germany, the Netherlands, Saudi Arabia and Japan (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Documents on Western Europe, 1973–1976, Documents 292 and 293; Volume E–9, Part 2, Document 155; Volume E–12, Documents 218, 222, 224 and 227). By 1977–78 the same disclosures had become a U.S. initiative for an international agreement on illicit payments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 310). Fourth, the scandal over South Korean bribery of members of Congress was put forward in 1978 as a reason to postpone the first troop withdrawal from Korea (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XIII, China, Documents 84, 92 and 94). The argument rests on 21 printed documents. They were chosen from about 3,000 documents, roughly one in a hundred of the series, whose text uses the words corrupt, bribe, graft, embezzle, kickback or "illicit payment"; see the memo for the exact counts.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (i) *The investigations themselves.* "Koreagate" appears in 3 documents, "Tongsun Park" in 4 and "Abscam" in none, across all 553 volumes. A search for Lockheed together with bribe, payoff, scandal or "illicit payment" reaches 27 documents in 7 volumes. The records of the Senate subcommittee on multinational corporations and of the House ethics inquiry would hold the rest. Those are Congress's records, not the Department's; they are held at the National Archives' Center for Legislative Archives, which the bundled reference data do not cover. (ii) *The making of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.* Its full name appears in 4 documents in 2 volumes, all from 1978–1980, against 81 documents that use the looser phrase "corrupt practices" (the index treats "corrupt practices" and "corruption practice" as the same words). (iii) *Anything after 1988.* The indexed series holds 13 volumes for 1981–1988 and one for 1989–1992. Its 177 documents dated in the 1990s include none that use any of the terms. (iv) *The early Republic.* No document dated before the 1840s uses the terms, but the series prints fewer than two hundred dated documents from those decades, so this is thin. The footnotes' record of unprinted sources begins only with documents of December 1910. Each of these searches was run with a check search that should always succeed ("Department of State", found in 552 of the 553 volumes; the exception is a volume of Paris Peace Conference minutes) and one that should always fail (a nonsense string, found in none).

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series are in this library; the search covered every document except front matter and editorial notes, and counted the two second editions in the series only once. The working set is about 3,000 documents in 447 volumes, of which 21 are cited and collected here.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Does "corruption" include U.S. firms' bribery abroad, or only foreign governments' corruption?** Including it, as I have done, adds the 1975–78 Lockheed and illicit-payments thread, the richest in deliberate decision-making. Excluding it leaves a story mostly about aid to client states.
- **Do allegations count where corruption is only a pretext?** Counting them brings in cases like Venezuela's 1875 refusal to pay U.S. claims. Excluding them narrows the question to cases where U.S. officials believed the corruption was real.
- **Must corruption change a decision, or is shaping how diplomats talk enough?** On the strict reading, the core is China 1948, Korea 1978 and the illicit-payments initiative. On the broad reading, the hundreds of embassy reports that describe a government as corrupt also count, and I have not read those one by one.
- **Inclusion rule used meanwhile:** a document is collected when its text shows U.S. officials weighing corruption, or a corruption allegation, in a choice about aid, leverage, disclosure, negotiation or troop deployment.

## Findings

### 1. A client's corruption was a reason to limit or condition aid

In China, officers described Kuomintang corruption as structural from 1943: "Graft and bribery are commonplace." (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, China, Document 202, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943China/d202). By 1947 the Ambassador proposed to use aid as leverage. Released in stages, he wrote, it "would give us the strongest leverage in furnishing the desperately needed aid as at each stage there is evidence of progress or in stopping it whenever the forces of reaction or of corruption assert themselves" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d292). In November 1948 the Secretary made it one of the reasons against all-out aid: "the administrative inefficiency of the National Government and the extent to which incompetent and corrupt officials occupy high positions make extremely questionable its capacity to utilize effectively American aid in anything approaching all-out proportions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 506, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d506). In 1950 the same reasoning appears as conditions on aid to the Philippines. The editors summarise the survey mission's view that the government "should institute a sweeping cleanup of the large number of corrupt and inefficient tax administrators and collectors" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 831, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d831). These words are the editors' summary, not the telegram's own.

### 2. In South Vietnam, corruption was an obstacle Washington would not remove by taking control

In 1960 Ambassador Durbrow wrote that "Diem knowingly tolerated corruption in his family and in high governmental circles though remaining personally free of such taint." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 179, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d179). In 1967 the embassy's political counsellor agreed that corruption "has become a key obstacle to pacification". He then rejected the cure his colleagues had defined, a U.S. veto over the appointment of province chiefs and division commanders: "The assumption of such sweeping prerogatives entails an invasion of the sovereignty of the Republic of Viet-Nam so great that it could and would be argued thereafter that United States is indeed the neo-colonialist power its critics and enemies allege it to be." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258). Six months later a Defense Department memorandum for the record noted that "The Junior Officers of the Army are increasingly restive in that nothing has been done about corruption in the Armed Forces." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume VI, Vietnam, January–August 1968, Document 16, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v06/d16).

### 3. American firms' bribes abroad became a problem of protecting friendly governments

The Senate subcommittee's staff told the Department in 1975 that Lockheed's payments to Franz Josef Strauss "paved the way for the sale of the Starfighter to the FRG" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Documents on Western Europe, 1973–1976, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292). The editors' note to the next document quotes Kissinger telling the President: "We must do everything possible to keep this quiet. If the CSU is destroyed in Germany it will really shake Germany and strengthen the left." (Document 293, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d293). On Saudi Arabia, the Near East bureau warned that "full disclosure of Lockheed’s payments may prove far more damaging to Saudi-US relations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 155, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d155). With Japan the line was different. Prime Minister Miki asked for the names (Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 218, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d218), and Kissinger told the Japanese ambassador: "We have to follow our judicial procedures; it is not a foreign policy matter." (Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d222). After former Prime Minister Tanaka's arrest, the Tokyo embassy expected it to "SHARPLY REDUCE ANY LINGERING SUSPICION THAT THE U.S. AND GOJ WERE SEEKING TO COVER UP THE LOCKHEED SCANDAL" (Document 227, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d227). Japan's special envoy, sent because the Diet insisted, also thanked Washington for its help in a meeting of May 1976 (Document 224, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d224).

### 4. The scandal became a U.S. initiative in economic diplomacy

Preparing the 1976 economic summit, Kissinger cautioned: "Two governments are in political difficulty on this issue. It could help, but we shouldn’t browbeat." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXI, Foreign Economic Policy, 1973–1976, Document 140, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v31/d140). Under Carter, Under Secretary Cooper listed the U.S. "proposal on illicit payments" among the issues on which the United States could take the offensive (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 266, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d266). By July 1978 he could tell the President: "Illicit payments is one area where we have done so." (Document 310, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d310).

### 5. Bribery of the U.S. Congress constrained a decision on Korea

In March 1978 the National Security Council staff argued for "Postponement of the first withdrawals—on grounds that the Congress cannot be expected to address the security dimensions of the problem while the Tongsun Park affair hangs over them" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XIII, China, Document 84, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d84). An interagency options paper carried the same justification (Document 92, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d92). At the White House meeting that followed, Assistant Secretary Holbrooke put it plainly: "The issue is not the withdrawal but the Tongsun Park affair. Because of “Koreagate,” Congressmen fear political retribution at the polls if they vote for any sort of aid to Korea this year." (Document 94, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v13/d94).

### 6. Earlier, an allegation of corruption was turned against U.S. claims

In 1875 the U.S. diplomat Scruggs reported to Secretary of State Fish that Venezuela's president would not pay claims awarded to Americans: "The alleged corruption of the arbitral commission which awarded those claims is Blanco’s pretext for the proposed bad faith of his government." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1875, Volume I, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v01/d199). This one document shows the other direction of the question: an allegation used against the United States. It is a lead, not a pattern.

## How this was done

I searched the full-text index of all 553 volumes of the series for words built on corrupt, bribe, graft, embezzle and kickback, and for the phrase "illicit payment". Front matter and editorial notes were excluded, and the two reprinted second editions were counted once. For each family I checked against the documents' literal text that the search finds the intended words. The index treats words sharing a root as one ("graft" and "grafted" match alike). In samples of 40, the corruption sense held in 38 for "corrupt" and in 36 for "graft"; the misses were mostly "grafted on". I charted the families by decade of document date, against each decade's number of dated documents. Use of the terms runs highest in the 1960s and 1970s, about twenty documents in a thousand, against three in a thousand in the 1930s. I scanned the TEI source files for spelling variants and for the split between document text and footnotes. I read the highest-ranked documents in each thread, and every quotation here was checked word for word against its document's text. Archival series were resolved through the reference files bundled with FRUS Explorer, with documents' origins and the footnotes' unprinted citations kept as separate counts. The memo holds every query, count and denominator.
