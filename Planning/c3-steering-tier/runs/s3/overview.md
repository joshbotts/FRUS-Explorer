# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Across all 553 volumes of the series, about one document in a hundred uses the words corruption, bribery, graft, kickback or embezzlement, and in a sample about three in four of those use them for official or political corruption. The printed record shows corruption shaping U.S. policy in three ways. First, as a lever over a dependent government: in 1922 the President's representative in Cuba made the suppression of graft a formal demand on the Zayas government (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, Document 841). Second, as a measure of whether an aid recipient could survive. Embassy and mission reporting on Nationalist China from 1944 to 1948 treats graft as a cause of military collapse and of lost public support, and reform of it as part of the U.S. programme (1944, Volume VI, Document 292; 1947, Volume VII, Document 583; 1948, Volume VII, Documents 43 and 471). In 1961 Washington and its embassies weighed graft in Korea and the Philippines in the same way (1961–1963, Volume XXII, Document 244; Volume XXIII, Document 356). Third, from 1974, corruption became a diplomatic liability created by American firms' own payments abroad: in Saudi Arabia, in Honduras and in Western Europe (1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents 122 and 155; Volume E–11, Part 1, Document 159; Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292). That thread ends with the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the 1980 argument over what it cost American exports (1977–1980, Volume III, Document 248). The collection holds twelve documents.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* the Department of State's central files, Record Group 59: the Central Decimal File through 1963 (the bundled indexes give no single identifier for it), then the 1964–73 central files and the Central Foreign Policy File. CIA files. The presidential libraries' National Security Files: Kennedy (NARA identifier 1164, 1961–1963), Johnson (567979, 1963–1969) and Nixon (572235, 1969–1974); access status for these is not in the bundled data. Two RG 59 lots. Lot 54D270, the Marshall Mission Files, which NARA split into ten series under the Special Envoy to China (creator identifier 10481258). Lot 63D351, the Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted. The Jidda embassy's post files (RG 84, Lot 79F80), source of the 1974 Saudi airgram, could not be resolved to a series. *What the editors cited without printing:* the same central-file classes, which are by far the largest group. The same presidential-library files, with the Carter Library's National Security Affairs files (not resolved in the bundled data). RG 59 Lots 66D95 and 63D351 (both identifier 2839192, entry A1 1586E). Lot 62D1, the Policy Planning Council's Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council (entries A1 1583E and 1583F, identifier 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted). Lot 62D430, Subject and Special Files (entry A1 1586C, identifier 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted). The two channels are separate counts and are not added together (see the memo).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Nothing after the series ends: I searched the same five word families over the documents dated in the 1990s and none matched, while the 1980s still hold about a hundred. The names behind the 1970s reporting are often withheld: about one matching document in six dated from 1969 on carries a "not declassified" bracket. The shelf for the Saudi case is the Jidda post files named above, not yet resolved to a series. The law itself is thin: the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act is named in only four documents, in two volumes. The congressional and SEC investigations that forced the 1975–76 disclosures appear only as the Department saw them; their own records are not a FRUS surface, and the bundled indexes name no series for them. The corpus has no word for "kleptocracy": no indexed word begins with those letters, while "anti-corruption" appears in 54 documents. Some enclosures were not printed at all, such as Crowder's 1922 memorandum on graft in the Cuban administration; it stays in the State Department's decimal file for Cuba. A check phrase, "Department of State", was found in every volume but one, a volume of conference minutes that is still searchable, and an impossible string was found nowhere, so these absences are not a failure of the search.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series. The scope is the roughly 3,000 documents that use the corruption words (fewer than 3,040 distinct texts once reprints are set aside), with front matter, editorial notes and two second editions excluded; seventeen were read whole and twelve are cited.

I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo verified, word for word, against its document, and that every document cited here is in the collection.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as corruption.** If it means only bribery and graft by officials, the working set is about three-quarters of what the word search returns. If it also takes in electoral fraud, "squeeze", patronage and the 1970s euphemism "improper payments", the set grows and needs new searches. Meanwhile I used the five word families and read for the official or political sense.
- **Whose corruption.** Counting foreign governments' corruption gives the China, Korea and Philippines threads. Counting American payments abroad gives the 1970s threads. Counting U.S. covert payments to foreign politicians gives a third set, which the corpus holds but this run did not read. Meanwhile I included the first two.
- **Allegation or fact.** Several documents carry an allegation made by a third party, such as a Chinese official's speech or a Senate staffer's account. Treating these as evidence of corruption or as evidence of how allegations moved policy changes which documents carry weight. Meanwhile I read them as evidence of the allegation and of the U.S. response.

## Findings

### 1. Reform of graft as a demand on a dependent government (Cuba, 1922)
The earliest sustained case in the set makes corruption itself the object of U.S. pressure. Crowder sent the Department a memorandum on graft in the Cuban administration, asked it to approve the memorandum and to warn the Zayas government, and admitted he could not document specific cases: "I have not been able to present much in the way of specific instances of corruption. There is here almost a conspiracy to conceal facts" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, Document 841, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d841). Around this document sit about twenty Crowder telegrams of 1921–23 that do not use the words; they are the next reading.

### 2. Corruption as the measure of an ally's survival (China, 1944–1948)
U.S. reporting turned Nationalist corruption into an explanation of military failure and a condition of reform. From Fukien the embassy relayed that "graft, bribery and corruption are standard practices to be found in every section of the province" (1944, Volume VI, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v06/d292). A memorandum prepared for General Wedemeyer's mission urged that "A thorough investigation should be made into the charges of corruption among army officers" (1947, Volume VII, Document 583, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d583). By 1948 the allegation itself had become a diplomatic issue. The embassy transmitted the government information director's complaint that "many US reporters, when they come across any corruption or graft, play up the cases, making the US public believe that the entire Chinese Government is corrupt" (1948, Volume VII, Document 43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d43). From Peiping the consul general relayed a Nationalist commander's verdict on Manchuria: "Nationalist failures in suppression rebellion entirely due to Nationalist corruption and impotence" (1948, Volume VII, Document 471, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d471).

### 3. Graft and aid in Asia, 1961
After the Korean coup, the embassy judged that the "Military government’s efforts to deal with wholesale graft, bribery and corruption in government and business" were genuine and producing results, while it watched for graft reappearing (1961–1963, Volume XXII, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d244). In Washington a former ambassador to the Philippines told the desk he saw "the graft and corruption in the Garcia Administration" as "the principal cause for the low state of Garcia’s public image". He proposed outside advisers in key ministries to check it, and Garcia had signalled he would accept them (1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 356, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d356).

### 4. American payments abroad as a diplomatic liability (1974–1975)
In the mid-1970s the corruption at issue was paid by American firms. The embassy in Jidda wrote that "As we have watched corruption mount we have asked ourselves what we might do to counter it—not from any moralistic concern, but as a means of guarding U.S. interests." It recommended that the United States refuse to be party to corrupt contracts, since "Our own U.S. government reputation for incorruptability may one day prove to be our most valued asset in Saudi Arabia" (1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Document 122, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122). Eight months later the Secretary's briefing warned that "Problems arising from publicity over the enormous commissions being paid by American firms to promote sales of defense articles and services to the Saudi Government threaten to strain US-Saudi relations" (same volume, Document 155, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d155). That briefing was found by its similarity to the Jidda airgram, by no word search, and read before it was cited. On Honduras, the Assistant Secretary told Kissinger of United Brands' payment: "The Department just learned of the bribe. It refused to help cover it up." (1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Document 159, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d159). On Western Europe, the European bureau passed on a Senate staffer's account that "At that point apparently, Lockheed bribed Prince Bernard to weigh in with the Germans." (1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292).

### 5. From scandal to statute: the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (1980)
By 1980 the question had become whether the law cost exports. The Commerce Secretary and the Trade Representative ranked the act as exporters' second-largest disincentive, while holding that "As a nation we have decided that bribery in international trade is reprehensible and that American companies must forego exports that can be attained only by illicit payments." They recommended amendments to clarify the law without loopholes (1977–1980, Volume III, Document 248, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d248). The act appears by name in only four documents, so this finding rests on a thin record.

Across the series, the words appear most often from the 1950s on: about one document in fifty in the 1960s and 1970s, against about one in three hundred in the 1930s (see the memo).

## How this was done

I searched the full-text index of all 553 FRUS volumes for five word families (corruption, bribery, graft, kickback, embezzlement), excluding front matter, editorial notes and two duplicate second editions. The index treats "bribe", "bribes" and "bribed" as the same word. I tested the search on an evenly spaced sample of forty matching documents: all forty contained one of the words as printed, and about three in four used it in the political sense. I ran a check phrase that should be everywhere and an impossible string that should be nowhere, in the same pass. I measured the words' frequency against the number of dated documents in each decade. I traced where the matching documents came from and what their footnotes cite, through the archival indexes that ship with FRUS Explorer, keeping the two apart. I compared document texts to find and set aside about eighteen documents printed twice. I used document similarity to look for related documents that use none of the words, and found one. I read seventeen documents in full and quoted twelve, checking each quotation word for word against its document.

## Read next

The collection, *Corruption and U.S. foreign policy: scope run*, holds the twelve documents under the five findings above, with the excerpts quoted here. The historian's additions would unlock several threads the run did not read. The South Vietnam documents of 1954–68 and the Iran documents of 1951–54, both heavy in the matching set. U.S. covert payments to foreign politicians (the Congo, 1964–68). The 1976 OECD guidelines on bribery and Japan's Lockheed affair. The twenty or so Crowder telegrams around the Cuba document. A decision on whether "improper payments" and electoral fraud belong in scope would also widen the searches.
