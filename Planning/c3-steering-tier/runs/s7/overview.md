# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows corruption entering American decisions in five recurring roles. It was a pretext a debtor government used against a settled award, and one the Secretary of State refused to accept (Venezuela, 1875: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1875, Volume I, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v01/d199; and Volume II, Document 393, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v02/d393). It was a diagnosis that bore on how far Washington would back a client (Nationalist China: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 582, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d582; South Korea: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 202, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d202). In South Vietnam it was an obstacle to the war aim that American officials chose not to override, either for fear of a coup or because overriding it would have meant taking over Vietnamese sovereignty (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume IV, Vietnam, 1966, Document 215, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v04/d215; Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258). From 1975, American companies' bribes abroad became business between governments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Document 218, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d218; Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292), and the 1977 anti-bribery law became a cost weighed in trade policy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d152). About 2,700 documents in about 435 of the series' 553 volumes use the words corruption or bribery. That is roughly one document in 115. The rate climbs steeply after 1945.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* (a) the State Department's Central Decimal File, Record Group 59, which supplies about two in five of the documents; most of them are files on China's internal politics, 1910–1949, then Indochina, Iran and South Korea. The bundled indexes give no entry or identifier for the decimal file itself. (b) Johnson Library, National Security Files (NAID 567979; Asia and the Pacific Country Files, 1963–1969, NAID 587358). (c) Kennedy Library, President's Office Files (NAID 1166; Country Files, 1961–1963, NAID 193654). (d) Ford Library, Presidential Correspondence with Foreign Leaders (NAID 572571; series NAID 572583, 1974–1977). The bundled catalogue gives no access status for (b) to (d). (e) Lot 63D351: RG 59, Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, Entry A1 1586E, NAID 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted under national-security exemption (b)(1). (f) Lot 62D430: RG 59, Subject and Special Files, Entry A1 1586C, NAID 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted. Three named sources remain unresolved: the Center of Military History's CORDS/MACV file "GVN Corruption 1967", the Kissinger records in RG 59 (Entry 5403), and two lots, 54D270 and 59D95. *What the editors cited without printing:* (a) decimal files on South Korean internal political affairs, about 20 documents dated 1952–1962, and on Indochina, about 15 documents dated 1952–1954. (b) Lot 66D95, which resolves to the same NSC policy-papers series (NAID 2839192), or possibly to RG 59 Subject Files, Entry A1 1586F, NAID 2839193, 1947–1965. (c) Lot 62D1: RG 59, Policy Planning Council, Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council, Entry A1 1583E/F, NAID 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted. (d) The Carter Library's National Security Affairs files and the Johnson National Security Files, which the editors cite most often. These are two separate channels and their counts are never added together.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** *Enforcement of the 1977 Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.* A search for that name found four documents in two volumes of 1977–1980, all about export policy, so for prosecutions the shelf is the Justice Department's and the Securities and Exchange Commission's own records, which the bundled indexes do not resolve. *The 1975–79 negotiation on an international agreement against "illicit payments".* Sixteen documents in four volumes: enough to show the negotiation existed, too few to write its history. *A count of the period's own phrase, "questionable payments".* It cannot be counted, because the index treats "questionable" and "question" as the same word, and only 1 of the 9 documents it returns contains the phrase. *The later anti-corruption regime.* The series thins after 1980, with about 6,000 dated documents in the 1980s against about 76,000 in the 1940s. The modern word "kleptocracy" never appears; a search known to match nothing returned nothing and the phrase "Department of State" matched 552 volumes, so the search itself worked. Its absence shows only that contemporaries said "corruption". *The Venezuelan commission scandal of 1867–68.* It is printed only as Venezuela's charge and the American answer, with no source notes, and the bundled archival indexes barely reach before 1940, so they have no shelf for it.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series are in the index used. Every document using corruption or bribery was counted, with front matter, editorial notes and the two duplicate second editions excluded. The argument rests on eleven documents in ten volumes, read in full.

## Decisions this needs from you
- **Does "corruption" include American bribery of foreigners, or only corruption in foreign governments?** Including it adds the 1975–80 Lockheed and trade-policy material (Finding 5). Excluding it confines the study to clients and partners. Meanwhile I used both.
- **Do allegations count, or only corruption U.S. officials treated as established?** The 1875 and 1975 documents are allegations: Venezuela's against the commission, and a Senate staff member's against Lockheed. Counting only established cases would drop both. Meanwhile I included allegations and labelled them.
- **Does the word used as a political epithet count** (adversaries' propaganda calling a regime "corrupt")? Including it inflates the post-1945 counts. Meanwhile it is in the counts, but no finding rests on it.
- **Does a document qualify only if it uses the word?** One document here (Finding 3) was found by its similarity to another, not by its wording. Requiring the word would drop it. Meanwhile I used this rule: *U.S. officials discuss corruption or bribery as a factor in a decision or a negotiation*.

## Findings

### 1. Allegations of corruption as a lever in claims diplomacy (1875)
Venezuela refused to pay awards of the 1860s mixed claims commission by alleging the commission was corrupt. The American minister at Bogotá reported it plainly: "The alleged corruption of the arbitral commission which awarded those claims is Blanco’s pretext for the proposed bad faith of his government" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1875, Volume I, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v01/d199). Secretary Fish answered that the United States had waited seven years "and have been met by vague promises, and still more vague allegations of corruption on the part of individuals", and he warned of a break in relations. The Venezuelan envoy named the former American minister among those accused (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1875, Volume II, Document 393, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1875v02/d393).

### 2. Nationalist China, 1947–1948: diagnosis, and a contest over American opinion
China accounts for most of the 1940s documents: the 1947 China volume alone supplies about one in six of the decade's uses of the word. Wedemeyer's 1947 mission reported that Shanghai "serves to reaffirm earlier certainty of corrupt and inept practices, both political and economic". He added that Formosans "fear that the Central Government contemplates bleeding their island to support the tottering and corrupt Nanking machine and I think their fears well founded" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 582, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d582). The Nationalist government saw the danger in Washington. A spokesman complained, as Ambassador Stuart reported, that "many US reporters, when they come across any corruption or graft, play up the cases, making the US public believe that the entire Chinese Government is corrupt" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d43).

### 3. South Korea, 1961: corruption as a threat to the aid program
Hugh Farley, an aid official in Seoul who resigned over his stand on corruption, wrote that graft and fraud "seriously endanger the whole U.S. position in Korea, and threaten to discredit, not only the aid program in Korea, but the Mutual Security program as a whole" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 202, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d202). Rostow carried the report to Kennedy. He noted that State and the embassy read the danger as less urgent, but that "The underlying fact is that all hands agree the situation in Korea is not good" (same volume, Document 204, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d204). That memorandum never uses the word corruption; it was found by its similarity to Farley's report.

### 4. South Vietnam, 1966–1967: an obstacle to pacification, and the limits of leverage
By the 1960s the words appear about sixteen times per thousand documents, against about two per thousand in the 1920s and 1930s. Vietnam supplies the largest single block. When Prime Minister Ky raised two corrupt generals, Ambassador Lodge disclosed that "Earlier in the day he had decided not to raise this subject with General Ky because he did not want to urge on Ky a step which might precipitate a coup". The President's staff summary added that "There is no doubt in his mind that there is widespread disgust with corruption and that it shakes confidence in the government and in the integrity of the elections" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume IV, Vietnam, 1966, Document 215, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v04/d215). A year later the embassy's political counselor agreed that corruption "has become a key obstacle to pacification". But real reduction would require an American veto over Vietnamese appointments, and he concluded: "I do not think it would be wise for us to seek such control" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258).

### 5. American bribery abroad, 1975–1978: a matter between governments, then a cost in trade policy
After the Senate's multinational-corporations hearings, the State Department collected the subcommittee staff's allegations. One was that Lockheed's payments to Franz Josef Strauss "coming when Strauss was Defense Minister paved the way for the sale of the Starfighter to the FRG" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292). This is an allegation as relayed, not a finding. Japan's Prime Minister asked President Ford to help disclose names, warning that "if the whole issue is kept unsolved with the names of the officials involved remaining in doubt, democracy in Japan may suffer a fatal blow" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Document 218, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d218). By 1978 the White House weighed export promotion against the new law. Among the steps the domestic policy staff judged unwise was to "terminate or severely curtail our arms sales and human rights efforts and repeal the foreign bribery and Arab boycott laws"; the staff recommended guidelines instead (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d152).

## How this was done
I searched a full-text index of all 553 volumes for the corruption and bribery word families, excluding front matter, editorial notes and duplicate second editions. The index treats "corruption", "corrupt" and "corrupted" as the same word. For each family I checked, on an evenly spaced sample of forty documents, that the words actually appear and mean political or moral corruption. The corruption family passed on all forty, with no garbled-telegram "corrupt" among them. Each search was run beside one known to match nothing and one known to match nearly every volume. I measured rates per thousand dated documents in each decade. I traced the documents' archival origins and the files their footnotes cite through the app's bundled archival indexes, keeping the two channels apart. I used the app's semantic similarity data, which finds documents written alike, to look for related documents that do not use the word; that is how the Rostow memorandum was found. I read eighteen documents in full and quote eleven of them. The counts include the editors' footnotes, which this index cannot separate from the documents. Exact figures, queries and denominators are in the memo; see the memo.

I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was verified word for word against its document in the memo (14 of 14), and that every document cited here is in the collection (11 of 11).
