# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The index I searched holds all 553 volumes of the series. About 2,200 of its roughly 311,000 ordinary documents (about one in 140, spread over about 400 volumes) use the word "corrupt" or a form of it. Most of those are passing remarks: in a sample of forty, six were not about official corruption at all. Where the documents show corruption bearing on what the United States did or said, it works in five ways, and almost never as the single cause of a decision. It is the reason Washington gave when it pressed an aided government for reform: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 832 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d832). It is a problem for the Vietnam war that American officials chose to press but refused to take over: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258). It is a threat to the stability of a friendly regime, argued at length by the embassy at Jidda: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122). It is a question of disclosure and protection when American companies had paid the bribes: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Documents on Mexico; Central America; and the Caribbean, 1973–1976, Document 159 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d159). And it is a ground for trusting or distrusting a government: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 249 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d249). The argument rests on 29 documents from 20 volumes.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two different things are listed here and they are not added together. First, the files the printed documents came from, taken from the source note under each of the 29 documents: (a) the State Department's central decimal files, Record Group 59 (the classes for China in 1943–44, the Philippines in 1950 and 1961, Vietnam in 1960 and South Korea in 1961), with no series-level identifier, entry or access status in the finding aids I had; (b) the Department's Subject-Numeric Files for 1967–69 and 1970–73, and its Central Foreign Policy File for 1973–79, again with no series-level identifier in those aids; (c) the Jidda embassy's files, Record Group 84, lot 79F80 (the folders on agents' fees, Northrop and Lockheed), which the aids name but do not tie to a National Archives series; (d) the Lyndon Johnson Library's National Security Files (National Archives identifier 567979), the Nixon Presidential Materials' National Security Council files, the Ford Library's National Security Adviser files, and the Carter Library's White House Central Files, Subject File (identifier 165203, 1977–1981); I could not confirm access terms for any of these; and (e) one Army collection that is not at the National Archives, the Center for Military History's files of the deputy for pacification (CORDS), folder on corruption in the Vietnamese government for 1967. Second, the unprinted files the editors' footnotes cite from documents that use corruption vocabulary: (a) the Far Eastern Bureau's Subject, Personal Name, and Country Files, Record Group 59, lot 62 D 26, National Archives identifier 1965953, entry A1 3113, 1960–1963, partly restricted (cited under the April 1960 Vietnam cable, Document 135 of the 1958–1960 Vietnam volume); (b) the Saigon Embassy files, lot 65 F 115, folder on the Can Lao party, which the aids do not tie to a catalogue number; (c) the files of Ambassador at Large Harriman for Southeast Asia, lot 71 D 461, identifier 603208, entry A1 5005, 1967–68, open; and (d) the Near Eastern Bureau's Iran files, lot 76 D 169, identifier 2602428, entry A1 5644, 1965–1975, partly restricted. The finding aids reach back poorly: only about one series in thirty-five among the 698 series their facts file describes begins before 1940.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) A count of how often corruption changed a decision. I searched the root word and a family of related words (bribe, graft, embezzle, venal, kickback, malfeasance, nepotism) in the index, then spelled-out variants in the volumes' own text, with a word that cannot occur as a control and a common phrase as a check. The hits are mostly descriptions of foreign governments; the cases in which an American decision is tied to the charge are the 29 cited here, and the volumes were not read exhaustively. (b) Whether the allegations were true. The documents report rumour, informants and press stories as much as findings, and a sample check shows the word often describes a regime's reputation. (c) The Senate hearings that exposed the American payments, and the history and enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act: that exact phrase appears in only four documents in the whole series, all from 1977–1980. The hearings are published by Congress, and I did not search them. (d) A settled account before 1940. The vocabulary is common in the earlier volumes, but the uses I read (an 1893 inquiry into Hawaii's monarchy, a bribery case in the Ottoman Empire, treaty clauses listing bribery as a crime that permits extradition) are not decisions about corruption, and I did not run a full search of period vocabulary there. (e) Anything the shelf above does not hold: the unprinted files named in the second list are where an investigator would look next.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes in the index were searched, with front matter and editors' notes set aside and the second edition of the 1951–54 Iran volume left out in favour of the first, which gave 311,429 ordinary documents. The 29 documents in the collection are those I read whole and judged to link a charge or fact of corruption to something the United States did, considered, refused or said.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as corruption.** I included corruption in foreign governments and bribery by American firms abroad, and left out the word's other uses (corruption of minors, a corrupted telegram text, rhetorical charges against American political machines). Counting only foreign officials would drop the Lockheed and United Brands cases, which are the clearest case of American conduct of diplomacy; counting every use would raise the set to the full 2,200 and bury the cases.
- **What counts as affected.** I required a document in which an American official ties corruption to something done, proposed, refused or said. If you also want documents in which corruption is only the diagnosis (as in the wartime China memoranda), the set grows, but the causal claim weakens.
- **Allegation or fact.** I treated the charge as a historical fact about the Americans' belief, not about the other government. If you want to separate proven cases from rumour, the documents will not do it alone.
- **Period.** The cases fall between 1943 and 1980, where the documents are most numerous and most candid. If your interest includes the 19th century, the question needs a different search.

## Findings

### Corruption was the reason Washington gave when it pressed an aided government for reform

The survey mission sent to Manila in 1950 met the charge in every office it visited, as its chief told President Quirino, and its report to the President listed inefficiency and even corruption as widespread while recommending fiscal, tax and administrative measures (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 841, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d841). In 1961 a former ambassador reported telling President Garcia bluntly that graft and corruption explained his low standing (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Southeast Asia, Document 356, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d356), and the Deputy Under Secretary for Political Affairs told a Philippine visitor that high American officials were concerned about corruption under President Garcia, and the two discussed international advisers placed in ministries, not Americans. In 1978 two departing officers in Haiti described an American aid proposal as aimed squarely at corruption and doubted it would succeed. The pattern is consistent: corruption is named as a political liability, and the remedy offered is budgetary or administrative. Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 832 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d832): "We said that as we started through the Government departments and as we visited the provinces, we could not very well ignore the question of graft and corruption." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Southeast Asia, Document 358 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d358): "Mr. Johnson opened the discussion by expressing the concern of high-level U.S. officials with respect to corruption in the present Philippine administration. He pointed out that the Philippines could not assume a role of leadership in Asia until there was a change for the better." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 252 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d252): "it is the toughest, most ambitious U.S. aid package ever presented to the GOH, and one which focuses squarely on one of the most important factors in Haiti’s development equation today: corruption."

### In Vietnam corruption was pressed, politicised and, by choice, left in Saigon's hands

From Ambassador Durbrow's report of 1960, in which Diem's own secretary and ministers worried about corruption in the ruling party (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 111, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d111), in April 1960 the Secretary of State's cable records an Assistant Secretary raising corruption with Diem's finance secretary (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 135, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d135), and in 1968 Bunker credited the Thieu and Huong government with cutting it back though it remained a deep-rooted cancer (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume VII, Vietnam, September 1968–January 1969, Document 91, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v07/d91), to Ambassador Bunker's talk with President Thieu in 1970, American officials treated corruption as a reason the war was not working and as a danger with Congress. In June 1967 the President's special counsel reported Thieu saying people could live with some of it. In July 1967 the Embassy's senior political officer agreed it was a key obstacle but rejected the American vetoes over appointments that would be needed to end it. In November the Under Secretary of State told the President that a visible, credible anti-corruption effort would help the Administration's own position (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 395, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d395). By 1970 Bunker told Thieu that the inability to act on corruption was hurting American ability to help, because of Congress. The public line, sent to every post in 1968, was that corruption was no monopoly of Southeast Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 177, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d177). Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258): "As the Committee points out, in order to effect rapid reduction in corruption the United States would have to acquire, and be willing to exercise, at least the power to veto appointments of Province Chiefs and ARVN officers of divisional commander rank and responsibility." and "I do not think it would be wise for us to seek such control." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 197 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d197): "“Most of the generals are corrupt. Most of the senior officials in the provinces are corrupt. But getting at corruption takes time. And you must remember that corruption exists everywhere, and people can live with some of it. You live with it in Chicago and New York.”" Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume VI, Vietnam, January 1969–July 1970, Document 175 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d175): "The inability of the GVN to do anything about high level corruption is sharply affecting my ability—the American ability—to help you."

### For friendly regimes, corruption was argued as a risk to stability and to American interests, not as a moral wrong

The Jidda embassy's long assessment of December 1974 concluded that corruption was rising, that the dynasty would be more vulnerable if identified with it, and that the Embassy would act only "as a means of guarding U.S. interests." After the Moroccan coup attempt of 1971 the embassy in Rabat weighed corruption against the King's way of living as an explanation, and called it a popular issue (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–5, Part 2, Documents on North Africa, 1969–1972, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve05p2/d119). In Tehran in 1973 the Ambassador urged the Shah on in his own campaign. In 1980 the Ambassador to Saudi Arabia listed corruption among four threats to the royal family and said he knew of no way for the United States to correct it. The common thread is a limit: the Americans thought it mattered, and thought they could do little. Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122): "As we have watched corruption mount we have asked ourselves what we might do to counter it—not from any moralistic concern, but as a means of guarding U.S. interests." and "If, with the passage of time, the Sa’uds become widely identified with corruption, they will become more vulnerable politically." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXVII, Iran; Iraq, 1973–1976, Document 45 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d45): "It was quite clear from this discussion that the Shah indeed is convinced that his program and the country’s future will go right downhill if he is not able to deal with the corruption issue." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XVIII, Middle East Region; Arabian Peninsula, Document 206 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v18/d206): "The problem of corruption is a difficult one and I do not know of any truly effective means that we can use to help correct the situation."

### When the bribes were American, the question became disclosure, protection and a new rule

In 1975 the State Department learned that United Brands had paid a bribe to Honduran officials, probably the chief of state, and refused the company's requests to intercede. The same year the ambassador in Jidda reassured a Saudi prince that the Department and Lockheed were resisting disclosure of names to the Senate, and the prince demanded they never be revealed. A Senate staff investigator's claims about Lockheed payments in Western Europe were relayed up the Department the same autumn (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Documents on Western Europe, 1973–1976, Document 292, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d292). In 1976 Japan's prime minister asked the President for the names in the Lockheed record (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 218, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d218), and Ford offered to share the Securities and Exchange Commission's material under the American legal limits, while Kissinger told Japan's envoy that the Secretary of State could not interfere in an investigation (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 224, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d224). Ford then asked the summit leaders to support an international agreement against bribery (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXI, Foreign Economic Policy, 1973–1976, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v31/d149). By 1980 Commerce reported exporters rated the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act the second greatest export disincentive and the President was asked for clarifying amendments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume III, Foreign Economic Policy, Document 248, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v03/d248). Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Documents on Mexico; Central America; and the Caribbean, 1973–1976, Document 159 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d159): "United Brands asked us to intercede with the SEC to keep the information confidential. We refused. United Brands also asked us to approach the GOH. We refused this too." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 161 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d161): "Sultan said the names cannot rpt not ever be revealed." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 219 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d219): "We are prepared to make arrangements to share with your government the information developed by the SEC, in the course of its inquiry."

### A charge of corruption was also a reason to trust or distrust a government

In 1943 a diplomat in Chongqing described the Nationalist government as riddled with graft, and recommended sending observers into Communist territory; early in 1944 the ambassador reported a plot by young officers to remove officials they thought corrupt (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, China, Volume VI, Document 269, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v06/d269). In 1961 the American embassy in Seoul treated the new military government's campaign against graft as genuine, and the ambassador told the Secretary of State that rumours of corruption had almost stopped. These documents show the charge doing work in judgments about whom to rely on, though they do not show it deciding the matter. Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, China, Document 202 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943China/d202): "Nepotism and favoritism are rife. Graft and bribery are commonplace." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 244 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d244): "Military government’s efforts to deal with wholesale graft, bribery and corruption in government and business, smuggling, large-scale diversion of military supplies, hoodlum terror, and police and press blackmail of individuals are genuine and are producing results." Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 249 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d249): "They have produced convincing evidence that they will not tolerate corruption, graft, bribes, smuggling, tax evasion, or political blackmail in government, military or civilian life."

## How this was done

I counted the word and its relatives in the full-text index of all 553 volumes, leaving out front matter and editors' notes, and checked the count's wording against the documents' own text on an evenly spaced sample of forty; I then repeated the count in the volumes' original markup for spelled variants (corruption, corrupted, corruptly, bribery, graft and the like), with a nonsense word as a control that came back at zero and a common phrase as a control that appeared in every volume. I dated documents by their own dates, not by the volume's year, and compared how often the word occurs in each decade among the dated documents of that decade, since the 1940s hold far more documents than the 1870s. I read 63 documents whole to find the cases, kept 29 of them, and quoted 17 passages, each checked letter for letter against its document. I also asked the similarity index for documents like each anchor and read what came back; none of the cited documents depended on it, and one, the 1950 mission's report, I first read because it came up this way. I checked that the 29 documents and the quotations are not printed twice in the series under a different volume.
