# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** In the printed record corruption works on U.S. policy in three ways, and FRUS
documents each of them. First, it serves as a reason to withhold or condition aid. In 1947 Secretary Marshall argued that
arming Chiang's government early would tell its "reactionaries" that American support came regardless of "party
corruption" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 628). In 1961 the
Kennedy White House made Korean aid "conditional upon Korean performance", with anti-corruption measures among the
conditions (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 221). Second, it is a
political liability at home that Washington tries to manage through its allies. In 1967 Lyndon Johnson's advisers agreed
that a visible Saigon anti-corruption drive would improve "the Administration’s position" (Foreign Relations of the United
States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 395). Third, in 1975–76 the corruption was American companies'. When
the Senate exposed Lockheed's payments abroad, diplomats had to manage friendly governments that were implicated in them.
Kissinger told Ford "We must do everything possible to keep this quiet" about the German and Dutch payments (Foreign
Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 293). In Japan, by contrast, Ford offered to share
the evidence (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Document 219). The argument rests on 28
documents from 12 volumes, dated 1947 to 1978, chosen from roughly 2,200 documents across the whole 553-volume series that
mention corruption.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* eight of the 28 came from the Department of
State's Central Decimal File (Record Group 59, National Archives at College Park; the app's bundled reference data names
this series but gives no entry number or catalogue identifier). The others came from the Department's later
Subject-Numeric Central Files and the Central Foreign Policy File (both Record Group 59, likewise unresolved). They also came
from the Jidda Embassy files, Lot 79F80 (Record Group 84, not in the bundled lot index), and from the Records of Deputy
Secretary Warren Christopher, Lot 81D113 (Record Group 59, entry P 14, National Archives identifier 1487627, 1975–1981,
partly restricted on national-security grounds). Four presidential libraries and the Library of Congress (the Kissinger Papers) supplied the rest: Kennedy's President's
Office Files and National Security Files (identifiers 1166 and 1164, 1961–1963); Johnson's National Security File, Vietnam
Country Files (series 591194, 1963–1969) and Tom Johnson's meeting notes (series 23657995); Ford's Presidential
Correspondence with Foreign Leaders (series 572583) and Presidential Country Files for the Middle East and South Asia
(series 568224), both 1974–1977; and Carter's National Security Affairs staff files. The bundled data does not give the
access status of any library series. *What the editors cited without printing:* the Bureau of Far Eastern Affairs' Subject,
Personal Name, and Country Files, Lot 62D26 (Record Group 59, entry A1 3113, identifier 1965953, 1960–1963, partly
restricted), cited for the 1960 démarches to Diem's government; the Executive Secretariat's Records Relating to National
Security Council Policy Papers, Lots 63D351 and 66D95 (Record Group 59, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979,
partly restricted), the lot that footnotes in corruption documents cite most often; the Policy Planning Council's
Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council, Lot 62D1 (A1 1583E/F, identifier 2838992, 1948–1961,
partly restricted); the Jidda Embassy's "POL Northrop and Lockheed" file in Lot 79F80 (Record Group 84, unresolved); and the
Ford Library's National Security Adviser Memoranda of Conversations, which hold Kissinger's October 1975 exchange with Ford.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *The investigations themselves.* A search for the Church subcommittee
under its three printed names found 24 documents in 15 volumes. A search for Lockheed with bribes, payoffs or payments found
43 documents in 20 volumes, 1957–1983. These are the diplomats' side only. The subcommittee's, the Securities and Exchange
Commission's and the Justice Department's own files are not FRUS documents, and the bundled reference data has no shelf for
them. (b) *How the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 was made.* The Act's name appears in just four documents, in two
volumes of 1978–1980, all after its passage. (c) *Before December 1910, where the editors' footnotes point.* The citation
data begins then. About 300 nineteenth-century and early-twentieth-century documents that mention corruption therefore
have no archival pointers here, and the offline archival guides barely reach before 1940. That is a limit of the tools, not
evidence that no records exist. Every search ran over all 553 volumes. The positive control ("Department of State") was
found in 552 of them; the one exception, a Paris Peace Conference volume of Council of Four minutes, does contain "United
States". The negative control (a string that cannot occur) returned nothing.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series. The scoping set is the 2,186 documents, out of 311,429, that use any form of the
word "corruption", once editorial notes, front matter and the two duplicate second editions are set aside. The argument
rests on 28 documents read in full, from five episodes: China 1947–48, South Vietnam 1960–68, South Korea 1961–63, the
foreign-payments affair of 1974–76, and Nicaragua 1978.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Whose corruption counts?** Counting only foreign governments' corruption keeps the four aid-and-alliance episodes.
  Counting U.S. companies' payments adds the 1975–76 affair, which is the one case where corruption changed U.S. law and
  treaty proposals rather than aid levels. *Meanwhile:* both are included.
- **Does an allegation used as a weapon count?** If yes, the Korean junta's purge of "corrupt" politicians and Pérez's
  attack on Somoza belong in the argument. Washington treated both charges with scepticism, so including them shows
  corruption charges shaping diplomacy without being believed. If no, they drop out. *Meanwhile:* included.
- **Must the word appear?** Requiring the word "corruption" (about 2,200 documents) misses euphemisms such as
  "commissions", "agents' fees" and "squeeze". Admitting them by hand-reading would widen the set without a fixed count.
  *Meanwhile:* the word must appear, or the document must belong to the Lockheed-payments search; one document about agents'
  fees was admitted that way.

## Findings

### 1. In China, corruption was the State Department's argument for holding back military aid (1947–48)

The China volumes of 1947 and 1948 hold more corruption documents than any other volumes. In them the Department turns the
Nationalist government's venality into a policy argument. Vincent's February 1947 memorandum would vet each aid project
for "corrupt management". Marshall's March letter to Patterson warns that premature military aid would tell the
"reactionaries" that corruption carried no price. Wedemeyer's August report from Nanking finds Shanghai confirming "earlier
certainty of corrupt and inept practices". Chiang turned the charge around: the Americans called his government corrupt
yet would not help it reform.

- "those reactionaries in the Chinese Government who have been counting on substantial American support regardless of
  their actions and party corruption would have cause to conclude that they were right" — Foreign Relations of the United
  States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 628, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d628
- "The information received in Shanghai serves to reaffirm earlier certainty of corrupt and inept practices, both political
  and economic." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 582,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d582
- "It is precisely because he knows the Government to be corrupt and inefficient, he says, that he has repeatedly asked for
  American aid" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 322,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d322

### 2. In South Vietnam, corruption moved from a private démarche to a public-relations requirement (1960–68)

In April 1960 Washington raised corruption quietly with Diem's adviser Thuan (Document 135 of the 1958–1960 Vietnam volume).
By December, Diem was telling Ambassador Durbrow that no Asian government prosecuted corruption more diligently, and that
the press stories were propaganda. Under Johnson the issue became one of domestic politics. Ky told Lodge in 1966 that two
corps commanders were corrupt. In November 1967 the President's senior advisers agreed that a credible drive would help
"the Administration’s position". In March 1968 the embassy urged Saigon to publicize the disciplining of 74 officials to
"meet criticism in the U.S." Johnson himself shrugged: "We cannot have perfection."

- "no country Asia more diligent in prosecuting corruption than GVN" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960,
  Vietnam, Volume I, Document 267, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d267
- "There was unanimous agreement that a visible and credible anti-corruption effort would help a great deal in improving the
  Administration’s position, and in building a more effective GVN." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968,
  Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 395, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d395
- "We cannot have perfection. We have corruption here." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume VI,
  Vietnam, January–August 1968, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v06/d31

### 3. In South Korea, aid was made conditional on reform, and the junta's own corruption charges were distrusted (1961–63)

In March 1961 a report by an aid official warned that "graft, corruption and fraud" endangered the whole U.S. position
in Korea. In May the White House staff proposed making all aid above a maintenance level conditional on performance,
corruption measures included. Two years later, when the military junta offered to restore an agreement if "corrupt"
politicians withdrew, the Department asked who was to decide. An allegation of corruption had become a tool of the junta,
and Washington resisted it.

- "The extent and depth of graft, corruption and fraud in the major institutions of Korean society today" — Foreign
  Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 202,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d202
- "Make it absolutely clear that U.S. assistance in the future, above a minimum maintenance level, will be conditional upon
  Korean performance" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 221,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d221
- "Who is to decide who is corrupt?" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia,
  Document 297, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d297

### 4. American firms' payments abroad: managing allies, and a proposal for international rules (1974–76)

The Jidda embassy reported in December 1974 that corruption was spreading and could embarrass both the Saudi government and
American firms. By August 1975 the Northrop agents' fees had stalled the Saudi air-force program. At the Secretary's staff
meeting that month, Mr. Maw argued that fees should be paid only with the purchasing government's knowledge. Kissinger's
briefing for his visit to Saudi Arabia listed the Church subcommittee's revelations about Adnan Khashoggi among the strains
on the relationship. When those revelations reached allies, Washington's responses diverged. On the German and Dutch cases,
an editors' note quotes Kissinger telling Ford to keep the matter quiet, for fear for the CSU. To Japan, Ford offered the
evidence and proposed "an international agreement to curtail such practices". The Saudis were told the matter was beyond
the executive branch's control. Qadhafi publicly accused one of his own officials of taking Lockheed's money (Foreign
Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 1, Document 50). In July 1976 the Tokyo embassy called Tanaka's arrest the
biggest political scandal for Japan's ruling party since its founding.

- "At the same time we have concluded that corruption has been on the increase." — Foreign Relations of the United States,
  1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Document 122, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d122
- "We must do everything possible to keep this quiet." (Kissinger to Ford, 8 October 1975, quoted in the editors' note) —
  Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 293,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve15p2/d293
- "I hope the Government of Japan can support the United States proposal for an international agreement to curtail such
  practices" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Document 219,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d219

Also cited above: the Strauss and Starfighter memorandum (Volume E–15, Part 2, Document 292), the staff meeting (Volume
E–9, Part 2, Document 152), the Atherton briefing (Document 155), the Akins–Sultan telegram (Document 161), Miki's letter
(Volume E–12, Document 218) and the Tanaka telegram (Document 227); all are in the collection.

### 5. In Nicaragua, corruption was a charge Washington heard and set aside (1978)

In February 1978 Venezuela's President Pérez called Somoza's government a "corrupt dictatorship" in a letter to Carter, and
the State Department listed government corruption among the problems that remained. By August the opposition's program
put the "Eradication of the corruption" of the Somoza years near its head. Yet the Central America office director argued that corruption was not
the cause of Somoza's trouble; regime fatigue was. On that reading, corruption figured in U.S. calculations as something
to purge from the National Guard, not as the reason for policy.

- "corrupt dictatorship that has systemically violated human rights" (Pérez, quoted in the editors' note) — Foreign
  Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XV, Central America, Document 68,
  https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v15/d68
- "Eradication of the corruption which has characterized the Somocist dictatorship" — Foreign Relations of the United
  States, 1977–1980, Volume XV, Central America, Document 80, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v15/d80
- "It’s not primarily his corruption, his human rights record, or anything else he has done." — Foreign Relations of the
  United States, 1977–1980, Volume XV, Central America, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v15/d83

### The long view

Corruption is far more present in the record of the 1960s and 1970s than earlier. In the 1920s and 1930s about two
documents in a thousand mention it. In the 1960s and 1970s about sixteen in a thousand do. No single volume accounts for
the rise. The 1890s figure, by contrast, is mostly one volume, the Hawaii appendix of 1894. The rates include the editors'
footnotes. See the memo.

## How this was done

A read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer search index (all 553 volumes) was searched for every form of "corruption" and
"bribery". The index treats "corruption", "corrupt" and "corrupted" as the same word, so every hit was checked against the
printed text. All 2,186 hits contain the word itself. A hand-coded evenly spaced sample of 40 found 36 that use it in the
sense of official venality; the rest are a legal phrase or a bare insult. Only 9 of the 2,186 use it for garbled telegram
text. The editors' document and chapter headings were scanned in the TEI source files; no volume has a chapter on
corruption. Five episodes were chosen from the volumes where the word is densest. In those volumes it is about nine times
as frequent as elsewhere, while an ordinary control phrase is about equally frequent everywhere. The 32 documents
retrieved in full were read around each passage. Archival origins and the editors' unprinted citations were resolved
through the app's bundled finding-aid data, and the two were kept apart. The app's semantic vectors were used to look for
reprints and for documents similar to two anchor documents; they suggested one document, which was read before it was
admitted. Every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was checked character for character
against its document, and every document cited here is in the collection; both checks were made before finishing.

## Read next

The collection **"Corruption and U.S. diplomacy, 1947–1978"** holds the 28 documents under the five findings above, with the
quoted passages marked. A historian's additions would open what it leaves out: corruption among American aid and
military personnel; the 1950s Indochina and Iran volumes, which rank high but were not read; the 1973–76 Latin American
documents; the Philippines and Indonesia in 1964–68; and the nineteenth-century claims cases. Above all, the investigative
files of the Church subcommittee and the SEC, which FRUS does not print, would show the domestic side of the payments
affair.
