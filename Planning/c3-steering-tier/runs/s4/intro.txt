What FRUS answers directly: The printed record shows a recurring pattern, not one story. Between 1892 and 1980 American officials wrote that a foreign government, or an American company, was corrupt, and that belief appears in five kinds of decision: it was given as a reason for someone to act against a government (Hawaii 1892–93, Korea 1894); it became a condition on loans and aid (Cuba 1922, Greece 1947, Korea 1961); it made officials hesitate over regimes they went on supporting (China 1947–49, Vietnam 1959–72); it reached policy through Congress (the Congo 1963, Colombia 1977, Haiti 1979–80); and in 1975–76 the bribes of American companies became a diplomatic problem in their own right (United Brands in Honduras, Lockheed in Saudi Arabia, West Germany, the Netherlands and Japan). This collection holds the 35 documents, from 20 of the 553 volumes indexed, on which that rests. Where FRUS points for the rest: the documents came mostly from the State Department's central decimal files (Record Group 59), the Kissinger-era records of the Secretary (Record Group 59, NARA identifier 2131131, 1973–77, partly restricted), and the Johnson, Kennedy, Carter and Ford presidential libraries; the footnotes of the same volumes point to unprinted Carter, Johnson and Kennedy library files and to State Department lot files such as the Deputy Secretary's records (identifier 1487627, 1975–81, partly restricted); the closing block lists them. What this request cannot get from FRUS: how much corruption weighed against the other reasons given in the same documents; the Senate subcommittee, SEC and foreign-government files on the Lockheed affair; domestic American corruption as a cause of diplomatic decisions (searches found only 2 documents on Teapot Dome, 9 on the spoils system and none on Abscam in all 553 volumes); and anything the editors did not print.
