# How has corruption, or allegations of corruption, affected U.S. foreign policy decisions or the conduct of diplomacy?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record answers this as a recurring pattern, not as one story. Between 1892 and 1980, American officials repeatedly wrote that a foreign government, or an American company abroad, was corrupt, and that belief turns up in five kinds of decision. It was offered as a reason for someone to act against a government (Hawaii in 1892–93, Korea in 1894). It became a condition on money (a loan to Cuba in 1922, aid for Greece in 1947, aid to Korea in 1961). It made officials hesitate over regimes they went on supporting (China in 1947–49, Vietnam from 1959 to 1972). It reached policy through Congress (the Congo in 1963, Colombia in 1977, Haiti in 1979–80). And in 1975–76 the bribes paid by American companies themselves became a diplomatic problem for Washington (United Brands in Honduras; Lockheed in Saudi Arabia, West Germany, the Netherlands and Japan). The finding rests on 35 documents drawn from 20 of the 553 volumes of the series that are indexed here, which is the whole series; among them "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258" (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258), "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, General Wedemeyer to the Secretary of State, August 17, 1947" (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d582), "Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, the Acting Chief of the Division of Latin American Affairs (White) to the Secretary of State, October 10, 1922" (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d864) and "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 161" (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d161). About one document in a hundred among the roughly 311,000 in the series uses a word from the corruption family (3,123 documents carry one), and a sample suggests about three in four of those are really about corrupt dealing, so the set the question concerns is nearer 2,300. These documents show what officials believed and proposed; they seldom show a decision turning on corruption alone, and each finding below says which.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels, never to be added together. *Channel one, where the printed documents came from* (read from the source notes of the 35 documents; a Record Group is the National Archives' number for one agency's records, a lot is a batch of files the State Department transferred together, and an identifier is the National Archives catalogue number). Sixteen came from the State Department's central files (Record Group 59: decimal files such as the 893 China, 837 Cuba and 751K Vietnam classes, the post-1973 central policy files, and a subject-numeric file on the Congo); the bundled indexes do not resolve a series identifier or an access status for these, except that the 1910–1929 decimal files are the Numerical File (identifier 654171). One came from the Department's intelligence-bureau estimate files (the 1960 Vietnam estimate), not resolved further. Two came from the Records of Henry Kissinger as Secretary of State (Record Group 59, lot 91D414, entry A1 5403, identifier 2131131, 1973–1977, 11 linear feet 5 inches, creator the Office of the Secretary, access listed as partly restricted, under the national-security exemption). One came from the Jidda Embassy Files (Record Group 84, lot 79F80), which the indexes do not resolve further. Nine came from presidential libraries: the Johnson Library's National Security Files (identifier 567979, 1963–1969), the Kennedy Library's National Security Files (identifier 1164, 1961–1963), the Carter Library's Office of the National Security Advisor (identifier 560131, 1977–1981) and the Ford Library's National Security Adviser files; the bundled catalogue gives no access status for the libraries. One came from the Kissinger Papers at the Library of Congress and one from the Army's Center for Military History (the Saigon embassy's corruption file for 1967), neither resolved further. Four, the 1892–94 Hawaii and Korea papers, carry no source note; the nearest bundled shelf by place and date is the State Department's series of despatches from U.S. ministers to Hawaii, 1843–1900 (Record Group 59; file unit identifier 177380751, the roll for September 1890 to July 1893 identifier 189267012), offered as a lead and not as the documents' proven source. *Channel two, what the editors' footnotes cite without printing* (the 14 of the 20 volumes that the citation index covers: 1,218 references to 115 targets). The largest are the Carter Library's National Security Affairs files (393 references), the Johnson Library's National Security Files (152), the Kennedy Library's National Security Files (75) and the Nixon-era National Security Council files (55). Among State Department lot files: the Records of Deputy Secretary Warren Christopher (Record Group 59, lot 81D113, entry P 14, identifier 1487627, 1975–1981, partly restricted, 32 references); the East Asian and Pacific Affairs Assistant Secretary's subject files (lot 85D240, entry A1 5408, identifier 2131132, 1961–1974, unrestricted, 19 references); the Secretary's and Under Secretary's memoranda of conversation (lot 65D330, entry A1 1566, identifier 602231, 1953–1964, unrestricted, 17 references); and the Human Rights bureau's subject files (lot 80D177, entry P 534, identifier 111879278, 1976–1979, partly restricted, 10 references). That channel records only lot, library and decimal-file anchors, stores the fragment of the footnote rather than the sentence, and begins with the 1911 volume. The offline indexes barely reach before 1940 (11 of the 698 series they describe start earlier, about one in sixty), so the thin shelf guidance for 1892–1922 is a limit of the tools, not an absence in the archive.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) The weight of corruption against the other reasons given in the same documents: FRUS prints a selection, and no document in the collection says that a decision turned on corruption alone. (b) The other side of the bribery cases. The Senate subcommittee's hearings, the Securities and Exchange Commission's and Justice Department's files, and the Japanese, German and Dutch investigations are cited or alluded to but not printed, and the offline indexes resolve no shelf for them; the documents themselves name the Senate subcommittee on multinational corporations and the Securities and Exchange Commission's Lockheed consent order as the places to look. (c) Corruption inside the United States as a cause of diplomatic decisions. I searched all 553 volumes for "Teapot Dome" (2 documents), "spoils system" (9) and "Abscam" (none), with the phrase "Department of State" as the positive control (94,049 documents) and a nonsense string as the negative control (none); two documents on a scandal and nine on a patronage system are too few to build a finding, and the historian should treat domestic corruption as unexamined, not as absent. (d) A chapter on corruption: of the editors' chapter headings in all 553 volumes only one names it (the 1958–1960 Vietnam volume's chapter on the Can Lao Party, corruption and the use of American aid); elsewhere the subject has to be found by words inside documents, and a word search cannot reach a case described only as irregularities or favoritism. (e) Anything after 1988: only 177 documents in the library are dated 1990 or later, and just one of them carries a corruption word.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes indexed here (321,749 documents, with 1,103 front-matter and 8,481 editorial-note entries set aside and the second editions of two volumes left out as duplicates of their first editions), searched for words from the corruption family; 35 documents from 20 volumes carry the argument. Every count is conditional on this library, which holds all 553 volumes of the series.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "corruption".** I used bribery, embezzlement, graft, kickbacks, nepotism and venality of officials, charged or proved; I left out smuggling by itself, political scandals that involve no money (Watergate appears in 183 documents and is not counted), and lists of crimes in extradition treaties. If you want nepotism and patronage broadened (appointing men for personal loyalty rather than competence, which runs through the Nationalist China material), the China and Vietnam findings grow and the question becomes one about governance.
- **Allegation or fact.** I counted a charge and a proven case alike, because the question says "allegations". If you want only established cases, Hawaii in 1893 drops out, since the printed record contains the counter-charge.
- **Decisions or conduct.** I counted a case where the United States chose to do nothing (declining a veto over Vietnamese appointments) as corruption affecting a decision. If you want only changes of action, the Vietnam finding shrinks to the reform of the import program in 1966.
- **Foreign or American corruption.** I included the bribes of American firms abroad as corruption affecting diplomacy. If you read the question as about foreign governments' corruption only, the last finding is a separate project.
- **Period.** 1861–1988 throughout. If you want the Cold War only, the first two findings drop to background.

## Findings

### 1. Corruption was alleged as a reason to act against a government (Hawaii, 1892–93; Korea, 1894)

In 1892 and 1893 the American minister in Honolulu, John Stevens, told Washington that the Hawaiian palace had for twenty years been "the center of corruption and scandal", and that opponents of annexation were men who "thrived on the palace corruptions". The Queen's own statement to the American commissioner who investigated the overthrow turns the charge round: it says native legislators sold their votes, a practice "taught them by the Thurston party". The documents do not show whether the charge changed any decision in Washington, but they show it travelling in a despatch that also asked for a United States vessel. In 1894 the same device came from another government: Japan's minister told Secretary Gresham the Korean rebellion was caused by "official corruption" and that Japan would not withdraw its troops until reforms were made.

- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1894, Appendix II, Mr. Stevens to Mr. Blaine, April 2, 1892": "For twenty years the palace has been the center of corruption and scandal, and is likely to remain so as long as the Hawaiian native monarchy exists." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1894app2/d172
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1894, Appendix II, Statement of Liliuokalani": "It was a practice among some of the native members to sell their votes for a consideration. This was taught them by the Thurston party." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1894app2/d316
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1894, Appendix I, Mr. Gresham to Mr. Bayard, July 20, 1894": the Japanese minister said "the rebellion in Korea was caused by maladministration and official corruption". https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1894app1/d27

Also cited in this finding: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1894, Appendix II, Mr. Stevens to Mr. Foster, February 1, 1893 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1894app2/d199).

### 2. Money was given strings: loans and aid conditioned on clean government (Cuba, 1922; Greece, 1947; Korea, 1961)

The clearest case of corruption shaping a decision is Cuba in 1922. General Enoch Crowder, the President's representative, rewrote the Cuban lottery decree himself and reported that President Zayas signed it "in the precise form in which I rewrote it". In October he told the State Department by telephone, as the Division chief relayed it, that an amnesty bill would free the men indicted in the lottery scandals and that the Department "should not consent to a loan unless this amnesty bill is definitely killed". The Department's own reply is more cautious: it raised no objection to the loan once Cuba gave a revenue assurance, and said only orally that it viewed the amnesty "with great concern". The editors add that the amnesty became law in 1924, which is the limit of the lever. The same logic appears in Acheson's memorandum on Greece in February 1947, which says aid cannot be used well while a corrupt civil service administers it, and in a 1961 staff memorandum on Korea making further aid "conditional upon Korean performance", including action against corruption.

- "Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, White to the Secretary of State, October 10, 1922": "General Crowder feels that the Department should not consent to a loan unless this amnesty bill is definitely killed." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d864
- "Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, the Acting Secretary of State to the Cuban Chargé, November 4, 1922": the Government of the United States "viewed with great concern the pending amnesty bill". This document contains no corruption word; it was found by its similarity to the preceding one and read before it was cited. https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d872
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXII, Northeast Asia, Document 221": future assistance "will be conditional upon Korean performance". https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v22/d221

Also cited in this finding: Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1922, Volume I, the Representative on Special Mission in Cuba (Crowder) to the Secretary of State, June 30, 1922 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v01/d852); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Near East and Africa, Volume V, Memorandum by the Under Secretary of State (Acheson) to the Secretary of State, February 21, 1947 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v05/d23).

### 3. China, 1947–1949: officials weighed supporting a government they called corrupt

American reporting on the Nationalist government used corruption as a settled description. The Wedemeyer mission found "corrupt and inept practices, both political and economic". Chinese officials themselves proposed supervised aid: a State Council member said "U.S. aid to China must be accompanied by close supervision", and Vice President Sun Fo said aid should be matched by reform through the elimination of corruption. Chiang Kai-shek, in Ambassador Stuart's report of August 1948, complained that Americans charged his government with corruption yet did little to help, and said only with American advisers could the reforms be made "which alone would justify material assistance". In February 1949 Stuart wrote that the Kuomintang system "had become so corrupt and inefficient" that orderly reform from within was unlikely, and by August 1949 the Nationalist leaders in Canton were putting the dilemma to the Americans in terms of the same word. What the documents show is a position, not a single decision: corruption was the argument both for conditioning aid and for explaining why aid would be wasted, and no document in this set records a withholding decision resting on it alone.

- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, General Wedemeyer to the Secretary of State, August 17, 1947": "The information received in Shanghai serves to reaffirm earlier certainty of corrupt and inept practices, both political and economic." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d582
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, the Ambassador in China (Stuart) to the Secretary of State, August 16, 1948": "Only with such American help can the reforms be undertaken which alone would justify material assistance." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d322
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East: China, Volume VIII, the Chargé in China (Clark) to the Secretary of State, August 23, 1949": the Nationalist leaders argue that "unless we make the best of a bad bargain and come to aid of existing government, corrupt as it is, Communist domination of all China is inevitable". https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v08/d576

Also cited in this finding: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Memorandum of Conversation by Mr. Philip D. ### 4. Vietnam, 1959–1972: corruption was known, tackled at the edges, and not taken in hand

In 1959 Ambassador Durbrow reported that President Diem wanted American aid put into the military budget because "there are less controls on GVN use of military funds". In April 1960 he told Diem that "it might prove difficult obtain adequate aid FY 61 if disturbing reports continue re alleged corrupt practices Can Lao party", the party of Diem's brother, and cited congressional concern; Diem denied the charge, and in August the intelligence estimate said Diem had failed to take any major steps against corruption by the Can Lao organization. The editors gave this part of the volume a chapter heading that names corruption. In May 1966 the White House aide Robert Komer told President Johnson that the Commercial Import Program was being reformed to meet "the rising tide of criticism over loose administration, windfall profits, corruption and the like", adding that the only credible stick was to withhold economic aid. In 1967 the embassy's own analysis rejected the one measure that might have worked: a veto over provincial and divisional appointments would be "an invasion of the sovereignty of the Republic of Viet-Nam". The senior group around Under Secretary Katzenbach, meeting in November 1967, valued a visible anti-corruption effort for what it would do for "the Administration’s position". In May 1967 Prime Minister Ky told Rostow's party that corruption "is real; it is serious; it is major source of discontent". In 1972 a Senate staff report, summarised in the Washington Special Actions Group, said that corruption was countrywide and that American officials winked at it.

- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 131": Durbrow told Diem "it might prove difficult obtain adequate aid FY 61 if disturbing reports continue re alleged corrupt practices Can Lao party". https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d131
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 258": it "would be argued thereafter that United States is indeed the neo-colonialist power its critics and enemies allege it to be." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d258
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume V, Vietnam, 1967, Document 395": "There was unanimous agreement that a visible and credible anti-corruption effort would help a great deal in improving the Administration’s position, and in building a more effective GVN." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v05/d395

### 5. Congress and the press carried corruption into aid decisions (1963–1980)

In May 1963 Secretary Rusk asked how to control corruption in Léopoldville because, he said, there would be "considerable trouble with Congress" over aid to the Congo if corruption were widespread. In 1977 the State Department was holding back helicopters promised to Colombia in 1975 for the narcotics program "because of reported narcotics-related corruption in the Colombian Government". In 1979–80 the Senate Foreign Relations Committee proposed cancelling aid to Haiti for failing to end corruption, and the House Appropriations Committee cut Haiti's aid to "demonstrate its concern for brutality and corruption"; Haiti's ambassador told the State Department the symbolic effect was "devastating". In these cases the effect is on the size and timing of aid, and in most of them the documents show the legislature, not the diplomats, as its source.

- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XX, Congo Crisis, Document 422": "The Secretary asked what might be done to control corruption in Léopoldville and remarked that we would have considerable trouble with Congress over giving aid to the Congo if there was widespread corruption." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v20/d422
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, South America; Latin America Region, Document 14": helicopters "which we are hesitant to deliver because of reported narcotics-related corruption in the Colombian Government." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d14
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 264": the House committee approved a $2 million cut to "demonstrate its concern for brutality and corruption." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d264

Also cited in this finding: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 257 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d257).

### 6. American companies' bribes became a problem of diplomacy (1975–1980)

In March 1975 the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs warned Kissinger that the Securities and Exchange Commission had found a promised bribe by United Brands to the President of Honduras, and the editors add that by late April the Honduran military had removed him (they do not say the bribe was the cause). Lockheed's payments then involved four governments. The embassy in Jidda warned that publicity about bribery of Saudi officials "could be extremely damaging", and in September 1975 Prince Sultan told Ambassador Akins that the names must "never" be divulged or the special relationship would end. Senate staff evidence about payments to a West German party and to Prince Bernhard of the Netherlands led the Assistant Secretary for Europe to write that he saw nothing useful the Department could do; the editors' footnote records that Kissinger told President Ford, "We must do everything possible to keep this quiet." In Japan, Prime Minister Miki asked President Ford in February 1976 for the names of Japanese officials, and when Japan's envoy came in May, Kissinger said the Secretary of State could not involve himself in a criminal investigation and that the Department hoped "to avoid any foreign policy embarrassment". The consequence in law, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of December 1977, appears in the record chiefly as a cost: a 1980 memorandum to President Carter says the task force's estimate of lost exports, $5–10 billion a year, "cannot be supported by firm evidence".

- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–9, Part 2, Documents on the Middle East Region, 1973–1976, Document 161": "Sultan said the Department or President Ford must do whatever is necessary to make sure the names are never divulged." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve09p2/d161
- "Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 224": "We look forward to seeing justice done, but it would not be appropriate for the Department to be involved in a criminal investigation." https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d224

## How this was done

I searched the full text of all 553 indexed volumes for the corruption family of words (corruption, bribery, kickbacks, payoffs, venality, embezzlement, malfeasance, misappropriation, nepotism), set aside front matter and editorial notes, and left out the second editions of the Iran 1951–54 and Western Europe 1973–76 volumes. Because the index treats related word forms as one word, I checked a sample of 40 evenly spaced hits against the actual wording: all 40 contain a form of the word, 36 in its plain forms, but only about 30 of 40 use it for corrupt dealing and not, for example, for lists of extraditable crimes or for "payoff" meaning a result. To find where corruption touched a decision I then searched for the words near each other in the same passage as aid, support, conditions, Congress and reform (595 documents), read the strongest hits in each period, and retrieved 69 documents whole. For six anchor documents I also asked the library's similarity index for the nearest 25 documents, joined them back to the text, and read those that held no corruption word; one, the November 1922 note to the Cuban chargé, bore on the question and was added. I checked the collection's documents against the similarity index for pairs printed twice; none of the 35 is one. Every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo checked, word for word, against the document it came from, and every document cited in this overview is in the collection; I ran both checks before finishing.
