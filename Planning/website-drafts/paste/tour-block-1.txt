        <p class="lead">FRUS Explorer brings the <i>Foreign Relations of the United States</i> series to your iPhone, iPad or Mac along with powerful research tools that work offline. State Department historians edited the series with great care, and the app is built on their work. This tour starts with an overview of the editorial annotation they added to the documents published in FRUS. Then it follows one research question through a typical research process, from your first search to sharing reproducible findings.</p>

        <p class="sc">Before Part I</p>
        <h2 id="p0">What FRUS historians built</h2>
        <p class="intro">FRUS is an edited documentary history series produced by the State Department's Office of the Historian. The historians who compile the volumes select documents for publication after extensive research in classified records from across the foreign affairs interagency and then add <i>editorial annotation</i> to help readers place each document in its contemporary and archival context. They give each document a heading, a source note describing its original provenance and current archival location, and also add other footnotes as needed to help readers understand the text and locate other directly related records. The digital editions published by the State Department include all of that work as open TEI data. The tools highlighted in this tour are extensions of their foundational work.</p>
        <p class="intro">Here is one document, with its editorial annotation labeled: the record of Secretary of State George Marshall's meeting with Stalin at the Kremlin on April 15, 1947, five weeks into a foreign ministers' conference in Moscow that ended without agreement on Germany or Austria.</p>
      </div>
    </div>

      <p class="sc">How this tour labels sources</p>
      <p>In the app, <i>provenance</i> means where something comes from. A document's archival provenance is the citation you need to locate and access the underlying original record. A number or a name has a provenance too: the source the app took it from. Where the app shows that source, it uses small labels such as <i>FRUS text</i>, <i>FRUS + NARA catalog</i> and <i>This app's model</i>. Each step of the tour below labels its sources as well:</p>
      <p class="prov"><span class="pk ed">From the editors</span><span>The documents and their editorial annotation. In the app: <i>FRUS text</i>.</span></p>
      <p class="prov"><span class="pk jn">From other records</span><span>The series augmented with another source, such as the National Archives catalog or the Office's subject tags. In the app: <i>FRUS + NARA catalog</i>, <i>FRUS + OH subjects</i> and the like. The correlation and reconciliation decisions required to combine information from different data sources reflect judgments made during the development process and incorporated into the app.</span></p>
      <p class="prov"><span class="pk cp">Made by the app</span><span>Worked out by the app, with a model or a scoring rule. In the app: <i>This app's model</i> or <i>FRUS + this app's word lists</i>. These models and rules also reflect judgments made during the development process.</span></p>
      <p class="prov"><span class="pk cp">Yours</span><span>Your own annotations. In the app: <i>Your reading</i>.</span></p>

            <p class="where">Research · Mac</p>
            <h3><span class="n">7.</span>Track everything you've marked up</h3>
          </div>
          <div class="ebody">
            <p><b class="ui">Research</b> gathers every document you've worked on, with your highlights and notes shown. Sort it by collection, tag or highlight color. <b class="ui">History</b> keeps a record of what you opened, searched, and exported.</p>
            <p>Use projects to keep one paper or course apart from the rest. Its research question appears at the top of the screen. <b class="ui">Suggested Next</b>, on Project Home, uses the same similarity detectors as the per-document Related feature to suggest leads to other documents from tags or collections you've associated with the project.</p>
            <p><b class="ui">Plan a Visit</b>, also on Project Home, starts an Archives Visit plan from the project's documents' source notes and footnotes, with your research question at the top.</p>
            <p class="prov"><span class="pk cp">Yours</span><span>The app stores your notes, tags and highlights separately from the published text.</span></p>

        <div class="etext">
          <p class="where">Browse ▸ Working Corpora · iPad</p>
          <h3><span class="n">8.</span>Save a set of results to come back to</h3>
          <div class="ebody">
            <p>Save a search's results, or a group of documents you lasso (draw a loop around) on the semantic map, as a working corpus. A <i>corpus</i> is a body of texts that you study. A working corpus is a fixed set of documents, a snapshot of one set of results. Later you can search inside it, and you can name it as the underlying sources an argument rests on. Saved searches keep your search query and its filters, and show what's new since you last ran them as you download additional volumes or new volumes are published.</p>
            <p class="limit">Each working corpus says how much of the result set it holds when a query exceeds device limits.</p>

        <div class="etext">
          <div class="ehead">
            <p class="where">Source Explorer and Archives Visit · iPad</p>
            <h3><span class="n">9.</span>Follow footnotes to archives</h3>
          </div>
          <div class="ebody">
            <p>Most documents have a source note from the editors that provides its archival provenance. <b class="ui">Source Explorer</b> reads that note and looks it up in reference data built into the app. For many documents, the app shows you the record group, lot file, entry, and/or filing manual that you need to access the original record copy at the applicable repository. <b class="ui">Archival Neighbors</b> lists the other documents, in any volume you've indexed, that came from the same source, such as the same lot file or the same decimal class. <b class="ui">Unprinted Material</b> lists the files a document's footnotes cite that FRUS didn't print. A free NARA Catalog API key from the National Archives enables live lookups.</p>
            <p class="nara">FRUS Explorer is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the National Archives and Records Administration. NARA Catalog data is provided by the National Archives and is subject to its terms of use.</p>
            <p>An <b class="ui">Archives Visit</b> turns documents you've selected into a roadmap for a research trip. It compiles information from source notes and footnotes to identify relevant archival collections or central file classes, lets you triage your list of files to examine, and even provides draft text for an inquiry email so you can initiate a dialogue with the archivists who know the records best.</p>
            <p class="prov"><span class="pk ed">From the editors</span><span>It all starts from the editors' source note. The app reads the note and matches it to reference data built from the volumes' own lists of sources.</span></p>
            <p class="prov"><span class="pk jn">From other records</span><span>Catalog records, access rules and entry numbers come from the National Archives catalog. The app joined and reconciled source note citations with NARA Catalog data in a bundled index that works offline.</span></p>

        <div class="etext">
          <p class="where">Cross-Reference Graph · iPad</p>
          <h3><span class="n">12.</span>Follow the editors' cross-references</h3>
          <div class="ebody">
            <p>The <b class="ui">Graph</b> tile in a document's research rail visualizes both its outgoing and incoming cross-references, up to three hops away, as a network or a timeline. A hop is one cross-reference: one hop reaches the documents linked to the selected starting point, and two hops extends the graph to the documents linked to those. Files the editors cited but didn't print show up as their own points and provide information about the archival collection that holds them.</p>
            <p><b class="ui">Cross-Reference Analytics</b> ranks the most-cited documents in your library.</p>
            <p class="prov"><span class="pk ed">From the editors</span><span>Every line in the graph represents one of the editors' footnotes: a cross-reference to a printed document, or a footnote citation to a record they didn't print.</span></p>
            <p class="prov"><span class="pk cp">Made by the app</span><span>Rankings such as Landmark Documents are worked out from cross-references, and measure how the editors linked documents, not how important a document was.</span></p>
