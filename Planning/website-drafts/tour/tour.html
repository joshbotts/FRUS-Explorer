A walk through the app

Take one question through 553 volumes1

FRUS Explorer puts the Foreign Relations of the United States series on your iPhone, iPad or Mac. It makes the series searchable on your device, so it works offline. Historians edited the series with great care, and the app is built on their work. This walk starts with what the editors made. Then it follows one research question through five kinds of work, from the first search to the checks that let others trust what you found.

Working on

How did Washington come to see the Soviet Union as its principal adversary, 1945–1950?

Most screens below come from a project built around this question. A few show other questions. The note under each screen says what it shows.

FRUS Explorer is an independent, private-capacity research tool. It is not an official product of the Office of the Historian or the U.S. Department of State.

Before Part I

What the editors made

FRUS is an edited series. Its editors chose each document from the government's records and put the documents in order. They gave each one a heading, noted where the original is kept, and added notes that explain it. The app calls all of this editorial annotation. The Office of the Historian publishes the series, with that work, as open TEI data, and every tool in this walk starts from it. Here is one document, with its editorial annotation labeled.

264,598source notes the editors attached to documents, in 502 of the 553 volumes2
8,637cross-references from a document in one volume to a document in another3
51volumes with no source notes at all. Where the editorial annotation is thinner, the tools that read it have less to work with.

2Source notes matched to a document, counted in the app's own data, October 2026. ↩

3Links between volumes, counted in the app's own data, October 2026. ↩

How this walk labels sources

In the app, provenance means where something comes from. A document's archival provenance is the archive and file that hold the original. A number or a name has a provenance too: the source the app took it from. Where the app shows that source, it uses small labels such as FRUS text, FRUS + NARA catalog and This app's model. Each step below sorts its sources into the same groups.

From the editorsThe documents and their editorial annotation. In the app: FRUS text.

From other recordsThe series matched to another source, such as the National Archives catalog or the Office's lists of people and subjects. In the app: FRUS + NARA catalog, FRUS + OH subjects and the like. The match is the app's.

Made by the appWorked out by the app, with a model or a scoring rule. In the app: This app's model or FRUS + this app's word lists.

YoursYour own annotations. In the app: Your reading.

I

Part I

Finding what you need

Search everything you've downloaded, ask in your own words, or explore the series the way its editors built it. Every path leads to a document, and every document leads to others.

Search · Mac

1.Search everything on your device at once

Keyword search works offline across every volume you've downloaded. Put a phrase in quotation marks. Use NEAR() to find words close together, and =word to match only that exact word. Facets show how the whole set of results breaks down by year, volume, person, document type, archival provenance and subject. Most of them also work as filters.

The Query Inspector shows exactly how the app read your search, so you can describe your method. You can read the same results four ways: List, Timeline, Concordance (every match lined up on your word) and Collocates (the words that often appear near it).

Checklist mode turns a long list of results into a to-do list. A result drops off the list once you open it or mark it reviewed, and Mark Page Reviewed clears a page at a time.

From the editorsThe person, document type and archival provenance facets use the editorial annotation: the people the editors tagged, whether an item is a document or an editorial note, and the source note under it.

From other recordsThe subject facet uses the Office's subject lists, matched by name. The app says these are not the editors' own headings.

Search ▸ Meaning · iPad Experimental

2.Ask a question in your own words

Switch Search from Keywords to Meaning and type a question. The app finds the 100 closest matches by what your question means, so a document can match even if it doesn't use your words. Each result shows a Semantic match score. Semantic means “having to do with meaning,” so the score tells you how close the meaning is. It is not a probability.

Search by meaning uses an optional language model. You download it once (310 MB), and it runs on your device. Nothing you type leaves it.

It can't search front matter or chapter headings. How well it works on 19th-century documents hasn't been tested yet.

Made by the appThe ranking comes from the app's model reading each document. It finds documents to look at. The editors' text is what you read and cite.

Browse · iPad

3.Browse the way the series is built

Explore the series by subseries, presidency, editor or archive, or open the lists of people and topics. Your own volume scopes (sets of volumes you choose) and working corpora (sets of documents you save, see 8) sit beside them.

Administrations groups volumes by the dates of their documents, not by when they were published. A volume that covers two presidencies shows up under both.

From the editorsSubseries, volumes and editors are how the series itself is organized. Archives sorts the series by the sources the editors cited.

Related Documents · iPad

4.Follow the threads from one document

The Related tile ranks other documents by what they share with the one you're reading. It weighs eight measures: archival provenance, cross-references, closeness in date, corpus proximity (how near each other the editors placed two documents), shared people, shared topics, semantic similarity and similar wording. Look in this volume, this subseries or all volumes. You can change how much each measure counts.

Two of the measures, semantic similarity and similar wording, are experimental. Each result says why it was picked.

From the editorsFive of the measures come from the editorial annotation: archival provenance, cross-references, the dates the editors gave, corpus proximity and the people they tagged.

From other recordsShared topics come from the Office's subject lists.

Made by the appSemantic similarity and similar wording are worked out by the app, and marked experimental.

Browse ▸ People · iPad

5.Follow a person across the series

The People list brings together everyone named in the volumes you've indexed. One person often appears under several spellings, such as “Kissinger, Henry A.” and “Kissinger, Henry.” The app joins them into one entry, with the role the volumes give and the years the person appears in them.

Open a person to see their career, the subjects of the volumes that name them, and Find all mentions. Person Analytics charts who is mentioned when, compares up to five people over time, and draws a network of the people most often named in the same documents.

If the app joins two different people by mistake, you can separate them.

From the editorsThe people the editors tagged in the text, and the roles their lists of persons give.

From other recordsLife years and posts come from the Office's register of people, with gaps filled from its register of Principal Officers and Chiefs of Mission. The match is the app's.

II

Part II

Managing your research

Highlights, notes, tags, projects and collections stay attached to the documents they're about. They sync through your own iCloud account. Nothing you write goes to a server the developer runs.

Reader and Research rail · iPad

6.Read it as printed, and mark it as you go

Documents appear as the Office of the Historian published them, with working footnotes, cross-references and links to people. Highlight in four colors, write notes, and add tags as you read. The app calls these your annotations, and keeps them apart from the editors'.

The Research rail next to the text holds Cite, Word Cloud, Sources, Graph, Related, On the Map and Share. Below them are the document's topics, summary, notes, tags and collections. Summaries come from Apple Intelligence on your device.

To sum up many documents at once, start a batch run over a volume, a tag, a saved search or a range of years. A large run takes hours. Any summary can then become the start of a note, which you correct and finish in your own words.

From the editorsHeadings, footnotes, cross-references and links to people are the editorial annotation, kept where the editors put it and made clickable.

Made by the appApple Intelligence writes the summary. In the app's words, a summary is always labeled as generated, never passed off as part of the record.

Research · Mac

7.Find everything you've marked

Research gathers every document you've worked on, with your highlights and notes shown. Sort it by collection, tag or highlight color. History keeps a record of what you opened, searched and exported.

A project keeps one paper or course apart from the rest. Its research question appears at the top of the screen. Suggested Next, on Project Home, points to other documents that may belong with yours.

Plan a Visit, also on Project Home, starts an Archives Visit plan from the project's documents, with your research question at the top.

YoursYour notes, tags and highlights are your own. The app never mixes them into the published text.

Browse ▸ Working Corpora · iPad

8.Save a set of results to come back to

Save a search's results, or a group of documents you lasso (draw a loop around) on the semantic map, as a working corpus. A corpus is a body of texts that you study. A working corpus is a fixed set of documents, a snapshot of one set of results. Later you can search inside it, and you can name it as the set your argument rests on. Saved searches keep your search and its filters, and show what's new since you last ran them.

Each working corpus says how much of the result set it holds.

Source Explorer and Archives Visit · iPad

9.Go from footnote to archive shelf

Most documents have a source note from the editors that records their archival provenance. Source Explorer reads that note and looks it up in the reference data built into the app. It finds the record group, lot file, entry and the archive that holds it, with no internet and no key. A free key from the National Archives adds live lookups.

An Archives Visit turns the files you've gathered into a plan for your trip. It says what to look for in each file, drafts a letter to each archive, and gives you a list to check before you go. It keeps the files the editors printed from apart from the files they only mention.

Archival Neighbors lists the other documents, in any volume you've indexed, that came from the same source, such as the same lot file, so you can read a document beside the rest of its box. Unprinted Material lists the files a document's footnotes cite that FRUS didn't print.

FRUS Explorer is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or sponsored by the National Archives and Records Administration. NARA Catalog data is provided by the National Archives and is subject to its terms of use.

From the editorsIt all starts from the editors' source note. The app reads the note and matches it to reference data built from the volumes' own lists of sources. The plan's split between printed and mentioned files comes from the editors' footnotes.

From other recordsCatalog records, access rules and entry numbers come from the National Archives catalog. The match is the app's. If the app can't match a record, the record is missing, not wrong.

III

Part III

Visualizing results

Every chart reads the volumes on your device, opens up to the documents behind it, and tells you what it counts.

Corpus Analytics · iPad

10.Chart a word across the years

Corpus Analytics works across your corpus: every volume on your device. Chart one word or several by year, as a raw count or as a share of all documents. The share shows whether a topic grew or the series just got bigger. Open the documents behind the chart, or send a search here with Visualize in Corpus Analytics.

From the editorsThe counts measure what the editors chose to print, which they picked to record major decisions. Read a spike as attention in the record.

Made by the appThe app works out the counts and shares from the volumes on your device.

Chronology · iPad

11.Lay out a crisis day by day

Pick a date range, and Chronology charts every dated document in it from every volume on your device, colored by the volume that printed it. The list below the chart goes day by day. Search in this range narrows the window to a word.

The list shows up to 5,000 documents. The chart covers the whole range.

From the editorsEach document sits on the date the editors gave it, as exactly as they gave it. Documents that cover a span of dates, mostly editorial notes, are listed separately.

Production noteThis capture shows the retired line "714 editorial notes span this whole period" (#1422). Re-shoot it on build 51.

Cross-Reference Graph · iPad

12.Follow what the editors cited

The Graph tile draws the documents that cite this one and the ones it cites, up to three hops away, as a network or a timeline. A hop is one cross-reference: one hop reaches the documents linked to this one, and two hops reach the documents linked to those. Files the editors cited but didn't print show up as their own points and open the collection that holds them.

Cross-Reference Analytics ranks the most-cited documents in your library.

From the editorsEvery line in the graph is one of the editors' footnotes: a link to a printed document, or a mention of a file they didn't print.

Made by the appRankings such as Landmark Documents are worked out from those links. In the app's words, they measure how the editors linked documents, not how important a document was.

Production noteThis capture predates two fixes to the graph (#1438, #1481). Re-shoot it on build 51.

Semantic Analytics · iPad Experimental

13.See the whole series at once

The semantic map places 314,616 documents4 by meaning, so documents whose language is alike sit close together. It names each area by the words that set it apart. Tap a document to open it, or lasso a group and save it as a working corpus.

Use the map to come up with questions, not to answer them.

Made by the appThe map is the app's model reading the language. The app says on screen that it is not an editorial fact.

Experimental. The map keeps similar documents close together. Distances between areas that are far apart don't mean anything.

The model hasn't been tested on 19th-century writing.

4Documents placed on the map, counted in the app's own data. ↩

Research Guide ▸ About the Series · iPad

14.Learn how the series itself is made

Four charts about the series itself work before you download a single volume: Production & Timeliness, Geographic Emphasis, Archival Sourcing and Administration Profiles.

Publication lag compares each volume with the deadline in force when it came out. The deadline was 15 years under a 1961 rule, 20 from 1972, and 30 from 1985. A 1991 law made the 30-year deadline official.

From the editorsThese charts read the series' record of itself: when each volume came out, the years its documents cover, and the sources its editors cite.

Part IV

Sharing findings

Citations, readers, trip plans and charts leave the app in formats other people already use.

Cite and Share · iPhone, iPad and Mac

15.Cite as you write

The Cite tile copies a document's citation in History at State style, or in Chicago or Turabian style. It can also copy it as BibTeX or RIS. Share sends the document to your Zotero library with your tags and notes, or sends the citation and its history.state.gov link to anyone.

From the editorsEach citation names the volume's editors and the document's number, and links to the page where the Office publishes it.

Collections · iPad

16.Build a reader for next week

Paste the citations from a syllabus into a collection, and the app finds the documents. Add section headings, your own notes, exact quotations, a bibliography, a timeline and a list of people. Presets such as Teaching reader and Briefing packet set the usual choices in one step.

Export to PDF, Word or HTML for students who don't use the app. For those who do, a .fruscollection file opens in their own copy as a collection they can change. Your research notes stay out of it unless you add them.

From the editorsThe Teaching reader keeps each document's heading and source note. Its list of people uses the people the editors tagged, and its Sources & Archives section lists the collections their source notes name.

Export · Mac

17.Share a chart and how you made it

Nearly every chart exports its data as a spreadsheet file (CSV) that starts with a full description of the method. Most also export as an image, with the chart's warnings printed under it. Readers of your work can check what was counted.

An Archives Visit plan can be shared as plain text or as a PDF.

The Person Network graph and Chronology's chart don't export.

Made by the appThe method statement says what was counted, and in which volumes.

V

Part V

Standing behind your work

Research has to hold up when someone checks it. These tools keep a record of how you found things, test the citations you read and write, and keep your notes true to the record when the record itself is corrected.

History and Data & Recovery · iPhone, iPad and Mac

18.Show how you got your results

A claim about the record, such as “mentions of this word fell after 1963,” has to say what was counted. The app keeps that record as you work.

Save a working corpus first, so the set of documents stays fixed. A count inside it means the same thing next month, whatever you download later. Chart a share of all documents, not just a raw count. Nearly every chart exports a spreadsheet that begins with its method.

History lists every document you opened, every search you ran and every collection you exported, filed by project. The method appendix turns your searches into a table for a methods section: when each one ran, what it found, how many volumes were indexed, and what it searched within.

The appendix keeps the searches that found nothing, because “I found nothing” is evidence only with a date and a scope. A count that hit the app's limit is written as a floor, such as “at least 1,000,” never as a total.

Made by the appThe appendix says what each count was drawn from: the volumes' text, or the app's model for a search by meaning.

Search ▸ ••• ▸ Find by citation · iPhone, iPad and Mac

19.Check a citation

Paste a citation from a book's footnote, and the app opens the document it names, even in a volume you haven't browsed. It reads the forms historians write: a Chicago footnote, a short form such as FRUS 1955–57, vol. XIV, doc. 23, a history.state.gov link, or a citation that gives only a page.

Batch checks a whole chapter's footnotes at once. Each note gets a row that says it was resolved, how many documents it could be, or that nothing matched. A student's FRUS footnote either leads to a real document or it doesn't, so Batch is also a way to grade.

Look up an abbreviation searches the lists of abbreviations in every volume you've indexed. It shows each meaning with the number of volumes that use it.

A page alone can't say which of several short documents a citation means, so the app lists them all and picks none. Lookup covers the volumes in the app's catalog, not volumes you've added yourself.

From the editorsLookup reads the volume titles, document numbers and page breaks of the Office's editions. In the pre-1955 volumes, document numbers were added to the digital editions, and the app says so when it uses one.

Production noteNew in this draft. No capture exists. Shoot Batch on build 51 with the footnotes of a real chapter, so the counts on screen are true ones.

Reader and Research · iPhone, iPad and Mac

20.Keep your notes in step with corrections

The Office of the Historian corrects volumes after it publishes them. When a corrected volume arrives, the app compares it with the copy it indexed before. If a document you've marked has changed, a banner says whether the text changed or only the footnotes, source note or heading. Highlights made on the old text turn amber, and Research lists the document under Changed by an update until you review it.

Review… opens a sheet where you decide each case. Confirm keeps a highlight where it is. Move Here appears only when the app finds your exact words in one new place, and it shows you the passage first. The app never moves a highlight on its own.

Each quotation you saved from the document is checked again. The same check runs on every quotation in a collection before it exports. It forgives line breaks and curly quotes, but not changed words, so a paraphrase doesn't pass.

The check warns but never blocks. When it can't check a quotation, it says why instead of calling it wrong. A document that a correction removed from its volume keeps everything you attached to it.

From the editorsThe corrections are the Office's. The app shows what changed and leaves every decision to you.
