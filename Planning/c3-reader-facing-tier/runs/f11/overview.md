# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly** — The series never uses the phrase ‘soft power’: it appears in none of the 30,092 documents of the 69 East Asian and Pacific volumes searched. The officials wrote instead of the ‘information program’, ‘psychological’ strategy, ‘prestige’ and the ‘image’ of the United States, and a set of 25 documents from 1949 to 1978 shows how they judged it. Their messages were built on contrast with communism, American strength and, after 1975, reassurance, rather than on the attraction of American society: Ambassador at Large Philip Jessup asked in 1949 for a larger information program in non-Communist Asia to show sympathy with nationalism and expose Soviet imperialism (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). Their beliefs about the audience ranged from respect for Asian nationalism to stereotype: a field reviewer in 1964 thought Vietnamese peasants would be persuaded “by other Vietnamese” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32), and the Ambassador to Japan in 1965 read Japanese opposition to the bombing of North Vietnam as the product of “ostrich-like pacifism” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46). Their own judgments of effect were mixed and, over time, more doubtful: in 1955 the National Security Council’s report on the information program reported placements, while in 1964 the Director of the U.S. Information Agency told the President that “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197). The documents also show that, by the late 1960s, the officials judged the regional image of the United States to move with the alliance and with American domestic order, and the documents I read credit the information program with little of the movement.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest** — Two channels, never to be added together. The first is where the printed documents came from. Of 42 documents in the information and public diplomacy volumes whose headings name an Asian or Pacific country, post or office (a loose test that also catches some South Asian offices), 27 came from the U.S. Information Agency’s own records at the National Archives, Record Group 306: its Director’s Subject Files, 1968–1972 (Entry A1–42), the Director’s files of 1963–1969 (Entry UD WW 257, accession 72 A 5121), the Office of Policy and Plans (Entry P–207) and, for 1977–1978, the Executive Secretariat’s correspondence files, 1973–1980 (Entry P–104); the rest came from State Department files and presidential libraries. The bundled finding aids match the accession to many National Archives series rather than to one, and none of them records those entry numbers, so I cannot name a single catalogue identifier for the boxes cited. The nearest bundled series, by creator only and not verified against the boxes, are the Director’s subject files of the Agency’s Executive Secretariat (National Archives identifier 40967285, 103 linear feet, 1956–1972, partly restricted) and the Office of Policy and Plans subject files (identifier 1257679, 47 linear feet, 1953–1971, partly restricted). The second channel is what the editors’ footnotes cite without printing. Among the 1,176 regional documents that use the information vocabulary, 322 carry at least one footnote citation to unprinted files: mainly State Department central files and lot files, with some Johnson Library National Security File, Nixon White House National Security Council files and Kennedy Library National Security Files. The lot files most cited are the Executive Secretariat’s records on National Security Council policy papers (identifier 2839192, 26 linear feet, 1947–1979, partly restricted), its subject and special files (identifier 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted) and the Policy Planning Council’s records of its National Security Council work (identifier 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS** — First, any use of the term itself: ‘soft power’, ‘cultural diplomacy’ and ‘Radio Free Asia’ each returned no document in the 69 regional volumes (with the phrase ‘public diplomacy’ appearing in just 2), and a control search for a phrase that cannot occur returned none either, while a common phrase, ‘Department of State’, returned 13,470, so the search works. Second, the audience’s own voice: the opinion data I met are the government’s own summaries, chiefly one Tokyo poll (1965) and one Saigon survey (637 adults, 1970); the Agency’s research reports themselves are in the National Archives, and the nearest bundled series is its Office of Research and Intelligence production division files for 1954–1958 (identifier 1254251, 9 linear feet, partly restricted), a match by creator that I have not checked. Third, any controlled measure of effect: officials counted placements, film audiences and survey answers, and none of them tested what a message changed. Fourth, the non-government channels: the Asia Foundation appears in 12 of the 30,092 regional documents and the Peace Corps in 59, and the phrase ‘cultural exchange’ in 92, always from the government’s side. Fifth, the period before 1948, which I did not search for this question, where the bundled archival finding aids are also thin: only 11 of the 698 series they describe start before 1940, about one in sixty-three.

**Scope** — The search covered 69 volumes on China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and the region as a whole from 1948 to 1980, with 30,092 documents, and 7 volumes on foreign information and public diplomacy, with 1,582 documents, all inside the full library of 553 volumes; front matter and editors’ notes were left out, as were the South Asian and Near Eastern volumes, the China volumes of 1942 to 1947 and the second editions.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** The term postdates the documents, so I used American information, psychological, cultural and exchange activity aimed at foreign publics, plus officials’ statements about American prestige and image; a narrower rule (cultural and exchange programs only) would drop most of this set, and a wider one (aid, troops, trade) would multiply it several times over.
- **What counts as East Asia and the Pacific.** I used the China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and region-wide volumes; adding South Asia would bring in India’s neutralism, which the officials treated as the main rival to American appeal, and adding Australia and New Zealand would bring in the allies’ own views of American staying power.
- **Where the period starts.** I began in 1948; starting earlier would add the wartime China volumes and, outside the series’ strengths, the missionary and educational links that some historians count as the first American soft power in the region.
- **Whose judgment of effect counts.** I counted what officials wrote about effect, including the Agency’s own claims; if you want only independent measures, only the 1965 Tokyo poll and the 1970 Saigon survey remain, and both were taken by or for the government.

## Findings

### 1. The message was contrast, strength and reassurance, not attraction

The officials described three jobs for the message. The first was to set the United States against communism: Jessup in 1949 wrote that “A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). The second was to hold wavering audiences: after the 1955 Geneva summit the Acting Director of the Agency, Abbott Washburn, told posts “We can anticipate, too, an upsurge of neutralism. Our information program must energetically combat these tendencies.” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 189, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d189). The third was to project strength calmly: in July 1963 the Agency told every post how to blunt the effect of a Chinese nuclear test, by stressing American strength “avoiding a preoccupation with the negative side” and by leaving that negative case to others because, as an overt American agency, “our credibility on this negative aspect is relatively low” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 135, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d135). Two other documents show the same habit: a Psychological Strategy Board plan for Thailand put information work beside aid and guerrilla training and ran it through local leaders (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403), and in 1969 the Agency stated its aims for the Nixon–Thieu meeting at Midway as a list of public images to strengthen (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 24, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d24).

### 2. Officials believed the audience was nationalist, poor, wary of whites and best persuaded by its own people

The belief that Asian audiences had to be understood, not taught, appears early. In December 1950 a joint State and Defense survey mission to Southeast Asia reported of the American information service that “At present we are talking over the heads of Southeast Asia, and doing so inadequately.” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d89). A National Security Council paper of July 1960 told officials to hold or reduce the number of Americans in each country, to head off an adverse reaction to Americans in “relatively privileged positions” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, East Asia-Pacific Region; Cambodia; Laos, Volume XVI, Document 65, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v16/d65). The same documents carry stereotype beside respect: in October 1964 an Agency reviewer of the Saigon post wrote “Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese” and described the civil population as “family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). The 1950 report also warned that white troops would always be associated with colonialism, which is why it wanted local armies. Read together, these documents show a policy of working through local voices resting on both a regard for local nationalism and a low estimate of the people addressed.

### 3. Their own judgments of effect moved from counting output to doubting that messages could outrun events

The 1955 National Security Council report on the foreign information program reported that in Japan “the national press remained generally unreceptive to explanatory material on U.S. policy and Communism” while “a remarkable placement of material on the peaceful uses of atomic energy was achieved” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190); the 1957 report is again a list of activity, with a note that a favourable public attitude in Japan sat beside rising dissatisfaction (same volume, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207). By June 1964 the Director, Carl Rowan, wrote that “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories, or GVN lassitude appear to be fiery enthusiasm”, and cited as the available measure that Vietnamese audiences at film showings the year before had totalled an estimated 28 million (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197). In August 1966 the Agency’s survey of how the world saw Vietnam listed first among the principal negative points “The image of a huge white nation attempting to suppress an Asian struggle for freedom.”, and asked for more action by Asians themselves (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 100, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d100). Its survey in Saigon in May 1970 found that only 26 percent of the general public thought the United States a dependable ally, the lowest of thirteen surveys (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). A January 1966 summary of the Far East posts describes their programs as aimed at elites and too short of money for mass work (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73).

### 4. Officials concluded that the image followed policy and America at home

In May 1965 the Ambassador in Tokyo reported a poll in which, since January, those favouring alignment with the free world had fallen from 49 to 40 percent and those favouring neutralism had risen from 22 to 32 percent, with the bombing of North Vietnam as the cause, and judged that “the basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46). In June 1968 the Embassy added that “Racial violence and social unrest in America have roused concern over basic stability of American society” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123). At the Agency’s conference of East Asia and Pacific public affairs officers in Manila in May 1969, the summary records that “Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers” and that the image of the United States “usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations” than on developments within it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). A November 1971 report on the overseas Chinese of Southeast Asia read an audience pulled toward China by feeling and not by ideology (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149).

### 5. After Vietnam the message became reassurance, and the audience was reported to doubt it

In May 1975 the Agency asked its East Asian posts to assess the effect of the fall of Indochina; the replies said that “The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese since the US withdrawal two years ago”, while the Hong Kong post reported that the territory viewed Washington as “very clearly in default” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). Two documents that came from the search by meaning, and not from the word-search results I read, give the policy context: a staff memorandum of 1974 judging that Asian countries were adjusting to the Nixon Doctrine (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 10, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d10) and a 1977 telegram from Hong Kong saying the Southeast Asian countries and Japan had read Washington’s refusal to reassess as a sign that it might lose interest in the region altogether (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXII, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Document 104, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v22/d104). In July 1977 the Agency was told that the Administration believed “shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power” was necessary (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79); the Embassy in Jakarta replied, in a telegram answering the Assistant Secretary’s request for views (Document 81, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d81), that it still faced a “deeply implanted and persistent feeling” that the United States was withdrawing (Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83). A telegram from Beijing in October 1978 describes exchanges, film and book programs for the People’s Republic, the one place in these documents where the aim was to open an audience and not to reassure it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d152).

## How this was done

I searched the full-text index of the 553-volume library (the full series), which matches word stems and so treats ‘information program’ and ‘information programs’ as one phrase, for the vocabulary of American information work (the Agency’s name and its acronyms, ‘Voice of America’, ‘information program’, ‘psychological warfare’, ‘psychological strategy’) in 69 East Asian and Pacific volumes, ranked the results, retrieved 52 documents whole, read the relevant passages of most of them, and quoted from 25. Every phrase family was checked against the text itself: ‘information program’ matched literally in 134 of 135 documents once inflections and punctuation were allowed (80 of 135 with single spaces only), ‘psychological warfare’ in 252 of 252 and ‘psychological strategy’ in 15 of 15. The vocabulary is not specific to the region: the Agency’s acronyms were found in about 26 documents per thousand in the regional volumes and about 18 per thousand in the other volumes of the same years, so I read documents rather than counting them, and the word ‘audience’ was rarer in the regional volumes (about 8 per thousand) than elsewhere (about 12), so I did not use it as a marker. By decade the information vocabulary ran at about 53 per thousand regional documents in the 1960s, 33 in the 1950s and 19 in the 1970s, but single volumes carry much of each figure. I also compared numeric fingerprints of each document’s meaning, shipped with the application, taking the nearest neighbours of four documents, the nearest volumes by average fingerprint, and the application’s map of document clusters; those are suggestions to read, not evidence of a link, and two of the 25 documents came to me that way and not from the word-search results I read. I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo verified against its document, and that every document the overview cites is in the collection.
