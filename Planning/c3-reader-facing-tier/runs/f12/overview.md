# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Twenty-six printed documents from 1950 to 1977 show how officials of the State Department and the U.S. Information Agency judged America's appeal in East Asia and the Pacific, though not under the name "soft power". They wanted to project, in their own words, "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). They aimed it mostly at elites, worked through local voices and governments, and kept American visibility low. They measured success with crude signals: material placed in the local press, film audiences, the queue for English classes, the reception of a visiting astronaut and, from about 1970, opinion surveys. They believed Asian audiences judged the United States less by its domestic life than by how it behaved toward their own countries and whether it would stay. Their 1969 regional conference recorded that "the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations … than on developments within the US" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). After the fall of Saigon, post-by-post assessments treated credibility as the whole message (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two separate channels, never added together:

    - Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972, Entry A1–42 (five documents);
    - Director's subject files, 1963–1969, Entry UD WW 257 (one) and Entry UD WW 193 (one);
    - Office of Policy and Plans, Plans and Operational Policy Staff, Entry P–207 (one);

  - State Department central files, Record Group 59: decimal files 511.90 (1950, informational relations with Asia), 511.51G (1952, informational relations with Indochina), 790.5 and 792.5 (1953, defence affairs of Asia and of Siam) and 611.98 (1962, relations with Indonesia), with the Central Foreign Policy File for 1977 (two telegrams).
  - The Operations Coordinating Board files, Lot 61 D 385, in Record Group 59.
  - The Kennedy Library (Robert F. Kennedy's correspondence), the Johnson Library (National Security File, Country Files for Indonesia and Thailand, and the Marks Papers) and the Nixon presidential materials (National Security Council files).
- *What the editors cited without printing.* Only five such citations exist for these documents:
  - the Office of Southeast Asian Affairs' subject files for 1955–1956, Lot 58 D 726 in Record Group 59, National Archives identifier 2544398, five linear inches, access possibly restricted (the National Archives' wording is "Restricted - Possibly");

  - Kissinger's office files in the Nixon materials.

The app's bundled archival indexes recognise the USIA series by name, but they give no National Archives identifier, dates or access status for any USIA entry above. Those details must come from the National Archives catalogue.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.**
- *The words "soft power".* They occur nowhere in the 21 volumes scanned: the seven public-diplomacy and information volumes and fourteen East Asia volumes, 1950–1980. In the same scan the check phrase "Department of State" was found in every volume and a made-up string in none. The officials' own vocabulary was "image of the United States", "psychological" objectives and, from the late 1960s, "public diplomacy". Those terms are what this run searched.
- *The opinion surveys.* They are summarised in a few documents, such as a 1970 Saigon survey and a 1971 note on Japanese attitudes. The surveys themselves are not printed. The 1971 survey material is filed under "Public Opinion Research" in USIA's Director's Subject Files (Record Group 306, Entry A1–42).
- *How audiences actually reacted.* FRUS prints American records only.
- *1981 onward.* None of the corpus's fourteen volumes from 1981 onward covers East Asia or public diplomacy.

**Scope.** I read the 33 documents in the seven public-diplomacy and information volumes whose headings name an East Asian or Pacific country, or USIA's Far East or East Asia office. I also read documents from fourteen East Asia volumes, 1950–1980, found by heading and ranked word searches and, in one case, by a semantic-neighbour search. Twenty-six documents form the argument. Front matter and editorial notes were excluded, along with South Asian and Near Eastern material that the word "Asia" also returns. Every count is conditional on this index, which holds all 553 volumes of the series.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I read it as the officials' own idea of attraction and persuasion: the information program, exchanges, image, psychological objectives and public diplomacy. Reading it more narrowly, as cultural attraction alone, would drop the counterinsurgency information work of Finding 3. Reading it more broadly, to cover aid and alliance behaviour, would bring in most of the regional volumes, and the evidence would no longer be specifically about persuasion.
- **Whether Vietnam belongs in a regional question.** Vietnam material outnumbers everything else: about three in four of the area-volume documents whose headings name the information program come from the Vietnam volumes. I kept a small Vietnam thread (Finding 3) and gave most weight to region-wide assessments. If Vietnam becomes the centre of the study, the four Vietnam volumes of 1961–1963 alone hold about thirty documents whose headings name USIA or public affairs.
- **Whether "the region" includes South Asia.** I excluded it. Including it would add USIA's Near East and South Asia office, which the same searches return.
- **What period to cover.** I used 1950 to 1977. The corpus has nothing on this subject after 1980, and I found little between 1956 and 1961; a targeted search of the 1958–1960 volumes might fill that gap.

## Findings

### 1. The first strategy was psychological warfare by local proxy (1950–1957)
Early planning treated information as one arm of an anti-communist psychological strategy, carried out through Asian governments and local outlets. The Psychological Strategy Board's 1953 plan for Thailand asked officials to use "every suitable operation to prepare the minds and emotions of the people to collaborate in the effort". It also cautioned that "each nation is an entity basically different from the others and requires special treatment" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). Officials assessed effect by indirect signs. In 1950 the Bureau of Far Eastern Affairs reported "increased usage of USIE news and feature material in the local press throughout the area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31). In 1952 the Saigon embassy read the waiting list for English classes as proof of "intense interest in US by educated groups". It admitted that the rural majority was "not a primary target group" and that films could be called effective only "though difficult to determine degree". It also wanted the program to "soft-pedal" anything that might stir anti-French feeling or "unbridled nationalism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). In Malaya in 1955 USIA itself resisted expanding its program, partly from "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447).

### 2. The image to project was strength, reliability and friendship, aimed at elites
By the 1960s the regional office stated its aim plainly: "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives", and "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups" (Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, above). Officials judged success case by case, often by the warmth of personal contact. Ambassador Reischauer called John Glenn's 1963 visit "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint". He added that Japanese scientists "seemed, for first time, to appreciate openness of the U.S. space program as compared to Russian" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). For a neutral audience the advice was restraint. Murrow wrote of Indonesia that policy "must be pegged to a real understanding of and a demonstrated respect for Indonesia’s neutral stance" and that "we should refrain from glamorous, immediate-impact actions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Southeast Asia, Document 289, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d289). The 1969 conference of public affairs officers in Manila held the clearest set of beliefs about the audience. It recorded that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", that their image of the United States followed bilateral relations, and that in regional seminars "US visibility should be low" (Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, above).

### 3. In wartime the program became a surrogate voice that was meant to become local
Where there was insurgency, USIS acted as the host government's information service. Officials judged the program by whether local services could take it over. A 1964 field review of the Vietnam program concluded that "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese". The same review described the civil population as "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times", a judgement that shaped its call to place Americans throughout the government (Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). The same reasoning applied in Thailand. USIA's director wanted the Thai to build their own program, complaining that the government "has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386). By 1969 USIA said it had "from the beginning" regarded its psychological operations in Vietnam "as short-term in nature" (Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58). A May 1970 Saigon survey supplied the bleakest measure in the set: the rating of the United States "as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88).

### 4. Officials recorded where audiences pushed back and where persuasion had limits
In Indonesia in 1965 the problem was hostility, not indifference. USIA's director called the attacks on USIS posts "a situation that I can describe only as intolerable"; the editors' note records that a White House aide thought his proposed retaliation an over-reaction (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106). In 1971 USIA's director reported that Japanese "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Japan, 1969–1972, Document 67, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p2/d67). During the opening to China, policy restrained the messaging. The East Asia bureau asked USIA to hold back a film on Tibet because "While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking." (Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 143, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d143). Meanwhile USIA's regional office proposed sending an astronaut with the President to Peking, since the Chinese interest in space "could generate favorable opinion toward the U.S." (Document 146 of the same volume, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146). Its survey of public affairs officers found that "Although the overseas Chinese appear proud of the achievements of Peking, they are reluctant to accept communist political and economic models" (Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149).

### 5. After Saigon, credibility became the message
When South Vietnam fell, USIA asked each East Asian post to reassess. The replies read the region as doubting American staying power more than American values. Tokyo found "The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese". Bangkok warned that until Washington defined its goals, "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations", while noting that "Thais still see us as friends and admire much in the American system" (Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, above). In 1977 USIA's new East Asia director described the administration's aim as "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79). Assistant Secretary Holbrooke asked chiefs of mission for their views on public diplomacy in Asia (Document 81, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d81). The embassy in Jakarta answered that the effort had been effective, yet "We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia.". It added that the human-rights message was heard as one that "seeks to implant American models on other societies" (Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83). In Japan the effort turned to editors and economists, dealing with "gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (Document 2, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d2).

## How this was done

I worked from a local copy of the FRUS Explorer search index, which holds all 553 volumes. I identified the public-diplomacy and information volumes from their titles. In those volumes I took every document whose heading names an East Asian or Pacific country or USIA's Far East or East Asia office: forty-two documents, of which nine concerned South Asia or the Near East and were set aside. In fourteen East Asia area volumes I added documents found by searching headings for information-program words and by ranking documents on those words. I retrieved forty documents whole, read most of them in full, and built the argument on twenty-six. To test the vocabulary I scanned the original edition files of twenty-one volumes, with tags stripped, using a check phrase that must appear and a made-up string that must not. I traced the archival sources through the documents' source notes and the editors' citations, and identified them as far as possible from the app's bundled archival reference files. One semantic-neighbour search, which finds documents with similar overall content, added the 1952 Saigon assessment that word searches had not surfaced; I read it before using it. Every quotation here was checked word for word against its document, and every document cited here is in the collection; I checked both before finishing. Exact counts, queries and the trace from each statement to its source are in the memo.
