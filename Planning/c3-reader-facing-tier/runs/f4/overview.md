# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The phrase "soft power" appears in no document of the series, so I read the question as one about what officials called information programs, psychological operations, and educational and cultural exchange. On that reading FRUS answers for 1949 to 1978. Officials treated persuasion as a companion to aid and arms, aimed at peoples they thought poorly informed about America (1950, Volume VI, Document 50). From the 1950s they preferred to work through local governments and keep Americans out of sight. Their own verdicts were sober: the Information Agency's director called information and psychological work "the weakest part" of the war effort in Vietnam (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122), an adviser argued that deeds outweighed broadcasts (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 85), and by 1975 a field post judged security programs to lack credibility (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). About two thousand documents in the East Asia and Pacific volumes use this vocabulary; those that carry the argument are cited below.

The offline index lists a number of series under that accession and cannot say which holds this entry. **3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** FRUS prints officials' judgments of their programs, not audiences' reactions. Apart from a few polls quoted in cables, I found no measurement of what people in the region made of the messages. The Agency's own research and poll reports are not located by the offline index; Record Group 306 is where to ask. FRUS does not hold the broadcasts, leaflets and books themselves, so a study of message content needs the Voice of America and Agency holdings in that record group. I tested "soft power" across the whole series and found it in no document, while a control phrase, "Department of State," appeared in every one of the seventy-six regional volumes and an impossible string appeared in none. Before 1945 I tested the contemporaries' vocabulary of Boxer indemnity remission and cultural relations and found a precedent (1924, Volume I, Document 540) but no assessment of its effect; the offline archival indexes barely reach before 1940, so no shelf is named. I did not read the educational-exchange record for Japan, Korea or the Philippines systematically, so those countries are thinly represented.

**Scope.** I searched all 553 volumes indexed, drawing on seventy-six East Asia and Pacific volumes from 1945 to 1980 (about thirty-seven thousand documents) and the area memoranda in the public diplomacy and information volumes (about sixty). I left out front matter, editors' notes and South Asia.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power."** I used information programs, psychological operations and exchanges. If you also want aid, the Peace Corps and military civic action, the set grows and the argument becomes one about development policy; I left those out.
- **Whose assessments count as "policymakers."** I included the Information Agency's officers and its directors as well as the State Department and White House. If you want only cabinet and White House voices, the Agency's frank self-assessments, which carry most of the evidence on effectiveness, drop out.
- **Whether messages aimed at enemies belong.** I included Vietnam psychological operations because the same officers ran both and the audiences overlapped. If you want only friendly and neutral audiences, the Vietnam material shrinks to the parts about South Vietnamese and nearby publics.
- **How far the region extends.** I took China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and the Pacific from 1945 on. If you want the period before 1945 or South Asia, the opening finding changes, because the earlier record uses other words.

## Findings

### 1. Persuasion was meant to ride with aid and arms

In 1949 the Under Secretary of State reported that Voice of America broadcasts to China had been expanded as Communist authorities ordered Information Service posts closed in Shanghai, Hankow, Nanking, Peiping and Tientsin, and that one aim was "to foster possible rifts between Chinese Communists and the USSR" (1949, Volume IX, Document 558). In 1950 he asked the President for money to explain American military and economic aid in Southeast and South Asia, citing "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (1950, Volume VI, Document 50). A 1953 psychological strategy for Thailand, written by the Psychological Strategy Board, set the method that later programs repeated: "In projecting U.S. leadership, however, we must work with and through indigenous leadership wherever possible" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403).

### 2. The strategy became to speak through local voices and lower the American profile

The Information Agency's field officers repeatedly argued that foreigners would believe their own people. A 1964 review of the Vietnam program stated, "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32). The same logic produced a plan to hand the war's information work to the South Vietnamese; a summary of 1969 states that the Agency "has, from the beginning, considered its psychological operations (PSYOP) support program in Viet-Nam as short-term in nature" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58). In 1970 the Agency's own program guidance generalised the lesson: "The President’s concept of partnership suggests that the U.S. will have to lower its voice" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 99). The same guidance told the Agency to disengage as rapidly as feasible from information support to governments in Southeast Asia. The habit was older. In 1955 the State Department understood the Agency to resist enlarging its Malayan program partly from fear of seeming to back colonialism (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 447). In 1962 the Agency's director proposed American studies chairs and youth work in Indonesia, with "patient persistence" in place of showy action (1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 289). In 1968 he urged the President to press Thailand to run its own national information program (1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Document 386).

### 3. The officials' own verdicts on effectiveness were mostly sober

The Agency's director told President Johnson in April 1964 that information and psychological work was "the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam" (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122). In 1965 he reported only partial progress in building South Vietnamese unity, with some headway in the Hop Tac pacification areas (1964–1968, Volume II, Document 160). In 1966 an officer of the Agency wrote to its director that the effect of the American presence "will, needless to say, be effective primarily through what Americans, both here and there, do rather than through what JUSPAO says or prints," JUSPAO being the joint American public affairs office in Saigon (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 85). In Japan the American embassy reported in 1965 that the number naming the United States among their three favorite countries "has dropped from 52 percent to 38 percent since last December," even though the embassy and its information service had done their best (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). In Indonesia the director judged in 1965 that his service would soon have nothing worthwhile left, yet opposed leaving voluntarily because it would be read as retreat (1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Document 106). The Agency's public affairs officers for the region were also unenthusiastic about its formal planning and budgeting system, and in 1969 they agreed that the whole exercise "has not been worth the effort" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31).

### 4. Beliefs about the audience cast it as uninformed, watchful and in need of local persuaders

Officials thought the regional public knew little about America (1950, Volume VI, Document 50), and that in Vietnam the civil population was "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times," a view that supported the plan to put Americans down to the lowest level of government (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32). The public affairs officers who met in Manila in 1969 were summarized as holding that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers," who read America's domestic troubles for what they implied about American policy in Asia (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). Another belief concerned steadfastness. An embassy cable of 1955 held that "the U.S. position in Asia rests in large part upon Asian appreciation that U.S. policy is based on principles, including observance of the pledged word" (1955–1957, Volume XXIII, Part 2, Document 50). These are officials' beliefs. Apart from a few polls, the documents rarely test them against evidence from the audience.

### 5. After Vietnam the task changed from persuading to reassuring, and China became a new audience

By 1975 the Agency's posts reported that the Vietnam collapse had raised doubts about American commitments, and the post in Thailand warned that while Washington had yet to settle its policy, "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). In 1977 the Agency's East Asia chief told his superiors there could be no serious doubt that the Administration thought shoring up East Asians' confidence in America's staying power "real and necessary" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79). China had become a new audience in 1971, when the Agency's East Asia chief wrote that the President's projected visit offered opportunities to "build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 146). The Agency also polled its posts on how overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia saw the thaw and found them mainly apolitical and concerned with trade, though enthusiastic about the thaw where Peking had no embassy (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149). By 1978 the Agency's East Asia chief, writing from Peking, reported that "The enlarged scope for ICA -funded activities is real and important to the future of US – PRC relations and to perceptions of each other" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 152), ICA being the International Communication Agency, the name the Agency then bore.

## How this was done

I searched the full text of seventy-six East Asia and Pacific volumes, 1945 to 1980, for the vocabulary the officials used: information programs, the Information Service and Agency, psychological operations, exchanges and the Voice of America, and I added the area memoranda from the public diplomacy volumes. I then read the strongest documents whole, keeping the text of each, and I checked a sample of forty documents from the full-text hits to confirm that the phrases actually occur and not only words that the search treats as the same. I also found one document, the Agency's 1970 program guidance, by its similarity to another document and by no word search, and I read it before citing it. Because the search treats "clearing agreement" and "clear agreement" as the same words, I tested the phrases by reading before I trusted a count. Every quotation in this overview is one I verified against its document, and every document the overview cites is in the collection.
