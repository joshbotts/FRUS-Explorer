# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series never uses the phrase "soft power" (a search of the 79 volumes I read, about 35,900 documents, found it nowhere), but it answers the question under three older names: the information program, psychological strategy and, from the 1960s, public diplomacy. What it holds is mainly the officials' own judgments, and I rest the account on 33 documents chosen from that set. Their messaging strategy was country-by-country and group-by-group material in local languages (press, radio, films, libraries and exchanges), later joined by single events, television, support for host governments' own information services in Vietnam, Thailand and Laos, and, after 1969, a message of partnership and Asian initiative. Their verdicts on effectiveness were mixed and rarely flattering: in 1950 Ambassador at Large Philip Jessup, reporting on a fourteen-country tour, found the United States "weak on propaganda" and a conference of chiefs of mission found worldwide material "not satisfactory" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Documents 32 and 15, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d15); in 1968 the Tokyo embassy thought American institutions and mass culture still kept the United States "a strongly positive symbol" in Japan, though Tet and American racial violence had worn the image (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123); in 1975 the agency's posts reported that programs on American security commitments in Asia "lack credibility" until Washington settled its policy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). The beliefs about the audience that officials brought to the work were that Asians follow the winner and respect resolve, read aid as softness or as a sign of selfish designs, carry a colonial memory, and (in the leaders' own words) that Americans do not understand "the Oriental mind". Of the thirty-two documents in which officials use that wording, seventeen apply it to how American policy or aid would be received; the other fifteen are uses such as an Asian leader's own claim or a remark about a negotiator.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Series the printed documents came from (came-from channel, for the 33 documents).* Fifteen came from the State Department's central files (Record Group 59: the numbered subject-classification files, the central decimal files, of 1950–1957, the Central Files of 1967–1969 and 1970–1973 and the Central Foreign Policy File of 1977); eleven came from the Records of the U.S. Information Agency (ten in Record Group 306, chiefly the Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972, entry A1-42, with Office of Policy and Plans files, entries UD-264 and P-207, and Executive Secretariat correspondence files, 1973–1980, entry P-104; and one in Record Group 305, the Office of the Director's Subject Files, 1975, entry UD-UP 7). The bundled finding aids name these USIA series but give no National Archives identifier, dates or access status for them, so I state none. Three came from State Department office files ("lots", accessions of an office's retired papers): the Policy Planning Staff's "S/P-NSC files, lot 61 D 167", which the bundled index matches by control number to the Policy Planning Council's Alphabetical Files, 1948–1961 (National Archives identifier 2198167, partly restricted; the Archives' title differs from FRUS's label). The other two, the Operations Coordinating Board files (lot 61 D 385) and the Saigon embassy files (lot 65 F 98, Record Group 84), are not resolved in the bundled index. Four came from presidential libraries: the Kennedy Library (Robert F. Kennedy's personal papers), the Johnson Library (National Security File and the Marks Papers) and the Nixon Presidential Materials (National Security Council files). *Series the editors' footnotes cite but do not print (pointed-at channel, whole volumes, not summed with the first).* In the five public diplomacy volumes the footnotes cite 202 unprinted items in 143 documents; the most cited State lots are 84 D 204 (not resolved), 78 D 295 (Administrative Correspondence and Policy and Procedural Files of the Deputy Under Secretary for Management, 1968–1975, identifier 27022973, partly restricted) and 78 D 184 (Subject Files of Assistant Secretary John Richardson, Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs, 1968–1976, identifier 5038052, partly restricted), plus National Security Council files at the Nixon, Carter and Ford libraries. In the 74 East Asia volumes the most cited unprinted State series are the Executive Secretariat's records of National Security Council policy papers (lots 66 D 95 and 63 D 351, which the bundled index resolves to one series, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted). This second channel stores the citation fragment, not the sentence, and holds lot, library and decimal anchors only.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *Independent measurement of how audiences received the messages.* FRUS prints a few of the agency's own opinion surveys and the embassies' impressions; the phrases "public opinion poll" and "opinion survey" occur in only about thirty documents among the roughly 35,000 in the 77 volumes searched for them (a search that returned nothing for a nonsense control term and returned documents in every one of the 77 volumes for "Department of State"). The shelf that holds the research is the agency's records (Record Group 306), for instance the public-opinion research file the editors cite, and a series of "Program Files" for 1954–1958 (National Archives identifier 1254251, partly restricted) that the bundled index attributes to the agency's research division, an attribution I have not verified for this topic. (b) *The content of the messages themselves,* the broadcasts, films and publications: not in FRUS; the bundled finding aids list "Records Relating to the Voice of America", identifier 7542975, 1948–1959. (c) *The wartime and Japan-occupation information work.* I did not read the nine 1941–1945 Far East volumes (fifty of their documents mention the Office of War Information, so this is a gap in my reading, not in the series), nor the occupation of Japan as such. (d) *The Asian side:* what editors, officials and publics in the region said among themselves; FRUS has only what U.S. embassies reported. (e) *Anything the term "soft power" would find:* zero hits, and the words "prestige", "image" and "propaganda" occur as often in other postwar volumes as in these, so they measure the series, not the question. Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific islands are thin in the volumes read.

**Scope.** I read through search 79 volumes (the series' East Asia and Pacific, Southeast Asia, China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam volumes from 1946 to 1980, plus five public diplomacy volumes covering 1961–1980), about 35,900 documents after leaving out front matter and editors' notes, and 33 documents from them carry the argument. I did not use the second editions where a first edition exists (none was involved), and the index holds all 553 volumes of the series.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power."** Narrowly (attraction through culture, exchanges, institutions and image) or broadly (the whole persuasion apparatus, including psychological warfare and support for friendly governments' own propaganda)? I used the broad reading; the Vietnam material is kept in one finding so it can be dropped without touching the rest, and a narrow reading would leave mainly the Japan, exchange and image evidence.
- **Which region.** I took the State Department's own East Asia and Pacific grouping (Japan, Korea, China, Taiwan, Southeast Asia and the offshore Pacific); South Asia is out. Adding South Asia would bring in India and Pakistan, where neutralism, not Communism, was the audience problem.
- **Where to start.** I started in 1946. Starting in 1941 adds nine volumes, about 8,700 documents, and the wartime Office of War Information.
- **Whose assessments are "policymakers'."** I counted any U.S. official, which makes the account lean on field posts and the information agency rather than presidents. Restricting to Washington principals would cut the evidence to a handful of memoranda.
- **What "effective" means.** I report what officials thought. If you want effectiveness itself, the missing evidence is the agency's research files (see above).

## Findings

### 1. In 1950 the program was large, local and, by its own officials' account, badly aimed

In March 1950 the State Department's Far Eastern public affairs office reported that the information program in Southeast Asia had been "stepped up to the limit of presently available funds," with a regional production center planned at Manila and a first sign of effect in more use of its material in local newspapers (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31). Ambassador at Large Jessup, who toured fourteen countries that winter, judged the United States "weak on propaganda" and asked whom it was trying to reach and with what themes (Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32). The chiefs of mission meeting in Bangkok agreed that "USIE materials designed for world-wide or even area-wide distribution" were "not satisfactory" and wanted material for specific groups in simple language, and tied American prestige in Asia to showing the American way of life working in Japan, Korea and the Philippines (Document 15, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d15). By April 1954 the Philippine program was a list of four centers (thirteen American and 111 local staff), press material to 122 newspapers and magazines, and 18 radio stations, with the film program to be halved for lack of money (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d359).

### 2. Officials believed in an "Oriental mind" that follows the winner and reads resolve

Jessup's report adds that French and British officials believed Asians "are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning." The Secretary of State's circular of August 1950 argued that "it is in the pattern of the Oriental psychology to respect and follow aggressive, resolute and dynamic leadership" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 266, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d266). General Ridgway in 1952 wanted the United States to overcome "any feeling in the Oriental mind that our interest in Asia is casual, temporary or overshadowed" elsewhere (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, China and Japan, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 551, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v14p2/d551). The chargé in Saigon in 1954 thought give-away programs "mistaken in the oriental mind" for a soft head (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p2/d992). The formula also ran the other way: the ambassador reported of Diem: "Not being Asians, Americans do not understand the Oriental mind and point of view." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 26, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d26). That is a belief about the audience, held by officials and echoed by the audience's leaders, that fits a strategy of showing resolve; none of the documents I read tests it.

### 3. Colonial memory limited what the agency would do and how the message was read

In May 1953 the consul general in Singapore warned that Southeast Asian anti-colonialism might blur the meaning of the Laos invasion and that a tendency to credit Viet Minh aims with nationalism "will make more difficult characterization of invasion as" aggression; he asked for a coordinated psychological offensive (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 1, Document 103, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p1/d103). In December 1955 the Far Eastern bureau recorded that the information agency had objected to expanding its Malaya program, and understood one of its reasons to be "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism"; a later footnote says the Operations Coordinating Board took State's side (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447). In 1957 the Deputy Under Secretary weighed Dutch New Guinea by the same test: support for Dutch trusteeship would be seen by "Asian minds" as American colonialism (Document 271, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d271).

### 4. What drew Asian audiences, and what wore it down, in officials' own accounts

Officials credit institutions, culture and single events rather than messages. Ambassador Reischauer rated John Glenn's 1963 tour of Japan "the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). The Tokyo embassy's 1968 stocktaking is the plainest statement of what we would now call soft power: "popular respect for US political institutions, infatuation with many aspects of American mass culture, genuine respect for our intellectual attainments, and visible attractions of the American way of life, have kept America a strongly positive symbol", while "Racial violence and social unrest in America" "made American image a rather less positive political symbol" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123). The agency's East Asia and Pacific public affairs officers meeting at Manila in 1969 concluded that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers" and that the American image "usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). The limit case is Indonesia, where in 1965 the agency's director reported that "We shall soon have nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106).

### 5. Vietnam turned the information agency into a wartime agency, and its own planners objected

A 1967 agency report found that in Vietnam, Thailand and Laos the agency was doing tasks that "appear to lie outside the Agency's appropriate sphere of operations," and that the three countries absorbed "nearly fifteen percent of the Agency's resources available for overseas programming" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 147, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d147). By 1969 the plan was to hand the Vietnamese government its own television, radio, printing and province staff (Volume VIII, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58). The agency's own Saigon survey of May 1970 found the rating of the United States as a dependable ally at "the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys," with 26 percent of the general public rating it very or rather dependable (Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). In 1971 Senator Church charged that the agency "is now engaged in a massive campaign" to sell the Thieu government to the Vietnamese people, and the agency's internal answer was that it was phasing the work out on a set timetable (Document 129, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d129). Across the 79 volumes, the agency's own names (the agency, its overseas service, the Voice of America, the information program) turn up in about one dated document in twelve for the 1960s against about one in thirty-six for the 1950s; the 1970s rate is nearly as high as the 1960s, but nearly half of it comes from one public diplomacy volume, and the public diplomacy volumes are themselves part of the set.

### 6. After Vietnam and the China opening, reassurance became the message, and audiences were slow to credit it

In August 1969 the agency's guidance to its media after the Nixon party's Asian stop was that peace in Asia "cannot result primarily from American conceptions but has to involve Asian initiatives" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 30, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d30). The agency proposed to turn the President's trip to Peking into "a long-range" asset with moon rocks and books, and treated the overseas Chinese as "a USIS audience" who were proud of Peking but "reluctant to accept communist political and economic models" (Documents 146 and 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). In May 1975, after the fall of South Vietnam, posts reported the Thais "still see us as friends and admire much in the American system," Hong Kong viewed Washington as "very clearly in default," and security programs lacked credibility until policy was settled (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). In 1977 Jakarta wrote that the new administration's public diplomacy "has been effective" yet there was still a "deeply implanted and persistent feeling" that the United States was "withdrawing" from East Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83).

## How this was done

The index holds all 553 volumes of the series. I chose the 79 volumes from the series' own titles (East Asia, China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, Vietnam, and the public diplomacy volumes) and counted documents in them, leaving out front matter and editors' notes. Because the index reduces words to their roots, a phrase search can return a different word with the same root, so every phrase I rely on was checked against the documents' actual wording: all thirty-two documents that the search returned for the "Oriental mind" family contain a form of it, twenty-five of thirty-two in the exact form, and I read each to sort the seventeen that apply it to how U.S. policy would be received from the fifteen that do not. I tested the question's own terms against a control (the other postwar volumes) and found "prestige," "image" and "propaganda" occur at about the rate of the rest of the series, while the agency's overseas-post abbreviation (USIS) occurs nearly four times as often, so I scoped by the agency's records, not by those words. I also used the series' semantic vector files (a numerical fingerprint of each document's meaning) as a finding aid: the documents nearest in meaning to four anchor documents, ranked by a coarse pass and then a finer one, led me to the 1975 reassessment and so to the later public diplomacy volumes, but closeness is a lead, not evidence, and I read every document I rely on. I wrote a quotation into this overview only if it appears word for word in a document in the collection, and every document cited here is in the collection; the memo records both checks and every number's source.
