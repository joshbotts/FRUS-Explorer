# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record answers this question well for 1950 to 1977, though in the vocabulary of the time: officials spoke of the "information program", "psychological strategy", "image" and "credibility", never of "soft power" or "messaging". From the start they treated information work in Asia as the companion of aid and alliances. In 1950 the State Department asked Truman for an intensified program because of "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 50, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d50). By the mid-1960s the U.S. Information Agency (USIA, the government's overseas information service, whose posts abroad were called USIS) defined its regional aim as "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" and pitched it at elites (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). The officials themselves were seldom confident that it worked. They assumed film was effective without being able to measure it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). Their Saigon surveys showed trust in the United States as an ally at its lowest in thirteen readings (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). After 1975 their posts reported that programs on American security commitments lacked credibility (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). The argument rests on 22 documents, each retrieved in full, out of about 2,700 documents in the corpus that mention the information program together with the region.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** These are two separate channels, and their counts are not added together. *Where the printed documents came from:* mostly USIA's own records in National Archives Record Group 306:
- the Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972 (Entry A1–42; the bundled finding aids give no catalogue identifier, dates or access status);
- the director's subject files of 1963–1969 (Entry UD WW 257);
- the Director's Office files of 1966 (Entry UD WW 193);
- the Office of Policy and Plans' planning-officer subject files (Entry P–207);
- the Executive Secretariat's correspondence files, 1973–1980 (Entry P–104);
- two Washington National Records Center accessions of USIA files (FRC 67 A 222 and 68 A 4933).

Beyond Record Group 306 they came from:
- the Kennedy, Johnson and Nixon national security files;
- the State Department's Central Decimal File of the 1950s;
- the State Department Executive Secretariat's Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (Record Group 59, Entry A1 1586E, National Archives identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted). This is the series behind Lots 63 D 351 and 66 D 95. Across the wider set of about 2,700 documents it is the State Department lot-file series that appears most often in both channels.

Two more series sit behind the wider set:
- the Psychological Strategy Board Working Files (Record Group 59, Entry A1 1462, identifier 2124692, 1951–1953, access possibly restricted);
- the Subject Files of the Vietnam Working Group (Record Group 59, Entry A1 5305/A1 5414, identifier 631403, 1963–1974, unrestricted).

*What the editors cited without printing:*
- the Central Decimal File's class 511 files (511.90 and 511.00, "International Informational and Educational Relations" of the United States with Asia and with the world, as the 1950 filing manual glosses them; the bundled indexes carry no series identifier for the 1950s decimal file);
- the Operations Coordinating Board files (Lot 61 D 385, which the bundled indexes cannot place on a shelf);
- the Southeast Asia files of the officer in charge of Malaya affairs (Lot 58 D 726: Record Group 59, Entry UD 53, identifier 2544398, 1955–1956, access possibly restricted).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.**
- *The term itself.* I searched the whole corpus for "soft power" and found nothing. The check that the search works found "Department of State" in 552 of 553 volumes, and a nonsense string found nothing. "Soft power" is a term of 1990.
- *"Messaging".* The literal word occurs in three documents in the whole series. The search engine treats "messaging" and "message" as the same word, so searching on it measures something else.
- *Measured effect.* USIA's own audience surveys and research reports are summarised in a few printed documents but are not printed. They sit in USIA's Record Group 306 files named above, including the Director's Subject Files' public opinion research folders.
- *The audience's own voice.* No Asian audience speaks for itself here. Reception is always reported by an American officer. That record lies in Asian archives and press, outside this series.
- *Gaps in the indexed library.* Among the 553 volumes indexed, the series has no public-diplomacy volume for 1945–1960 or for the 1980s. The 1950s are seen through regional volumes and the 1955–1957 Foreign Information Program volume.

**Scope.** I searched every one of the 553 indexed volumes for documents dated 1945 through 1980 that name the information program (USIA, USIS, the Voice of America, psychological warfare or strategy, cultural exchange, public diplomacy, the American image) together with a place in East Asia or the Pacific. That gave about 2,700 documents in 273 volumes, after excluding front matter, editorial notes, second editions and 161 false matches on the abbreviation "USI", which documents of 1946–1950 on Indonesia use and which the search engine treats as the same word as USIS. The argument rests on 22 of them, chosen by reading, and every count is conditional on this 553-volume library (see the memo).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I read it as the government's deliberate information, cultural and image work, together with its judgments of America's standing. If you mean attraction exerted *without* government programs (films, universities, consumer goods), FRUS holds only scattered remarks, such as a 1952 aside about American films, and the answer shifts to other archives. Meanwhile I included any document in which officials assess or plan the American image, information or psychological programs in the region.
- **Psychological operations in wartime.** Leaflets and propaganda in Vietnam are "messaging", but aimed at enemies as much as at audiences to be attracted. Counting them makes Vietnam dominate the record. Excluding them leaves a thinner, more diplomatic record. Meanwhile I kept them where officials discussed their effect on the American image, not tactical operations.
- **Region.** "East Asia and Pacific" in USIA's own usage changed from "Far East" to "East Asia and Pacific", and it sometimes included Burma and excluded South Asia. Meanwhile I followed the agency's area offices and excluded South Asia.
- **Period.** The question has no dates. Meanwhile I used 1945–1980, because the printed record of Asian information programs effectively begins in 1950 and the indexed public-diplomacy volumes end in 1980.

## Findings

### 1. Information was first conceived as the explanatory arm of aid and containment (1950–1955)

The 1950 request to Truman framed the problem as ignorance: Asians did not know the United States, and communists would misrepresent its aid, so information had to travel with the money. It argued from "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 50, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d50). In 1953 the Psychological Strategy Board's plan for Thailand set out to use "every suitable operation to prepare the minds and emotions of the people to collaborate in the effort". The text reads "utilizing every suitable operation…" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). The same planners already saw race and colonialism as the weak points. From Saigon the ambassador warned of "Commie propaganda which exploits nationalist and racial feeling" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). USIA's 1955 status report described dealing in the Far East with "mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples" and with propaganda "designed to stir up race hatred" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190). In Malaya, USIA itself held back partly from "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447).

### 2. In Vietnam, information officers warned that U.S. conduct was costing the American image in Asia (1961–1963)

In 1961 USIA set out deliberately: "We are in the process of cranking up a major propaganda campaign on Viet Cong insurgency in VietNam" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 49, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d49). Its regional chief also judged that some military choices could not be explained away. On defoliants he wrote: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (same volume, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265). By 1963 USIA's man in Saigon told the President's advisers that "the U.S. image was being hurt". His proposed tactics rested on an explicit belief about the audience: "With Orientals in general and notably with the Ngo Dinhs, capitulation is virtually impossible if they are painted into a corner" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 140, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d140). The same memorandum is printed again in the Vietnam volume for August–December 1963.

### 3. The audience was imagined as elite, watchful and judging by deeds

USIA's 1966 summary of its Far East country plans named the core message: "In all cases, however, the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives". It named the target too: "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). Officers expected words to be discounted. Planning a reply to Indonesian-led anti-American propaganda in 1963, the agency's acting director wrote that without American action "much of our audience would tend to regard it as mere words without deeds" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 126, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126). The 1969 conference of the region's public affairs officers concluded that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", and that the American image "usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on events inside the United States (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). A 1971 study of the overseas Chinese of Southeast Asia found that "The predominant mood is apolitical, seeking to be neutral" (same volume, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). In Vietnam in 1966 an agency officer acknowledged "an unfortunately large amount of anti-Americanism under the surface in Viet-Nam". He traced it partly to Vietnamese experience of being colonized (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 85, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d85).

### 4. Officials measured effect unevenly and often distrusted the measures

The effect of information work was seldom shown, and officials knew it. The 1952 Saigon report could only reason that "must be assumed that films is effective program though difficult to determine degree" (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). Field survey teams were prized: "The teams have given us more of an insight into rural attitudes than any other device that has been used" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). Yet the 1969 conference dismissed the agency's budget-based evaluation system (the Planning-Programming-Budgeting System), saying it "has not been worth the effort" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). When surveys did speak, they were discouraging. In May 1970 the "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). In 1971 USIA's director told Kissinger that "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Japan, 1969–1972, Document 67, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p2/d67).

### 5. From the late 1960s the strategy shifted to handing the message to Asian governments, then to reassurance

In 1968 USIA's director complained that "The Thai Government has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386). In Vietnam the agency said it had "from the beginning, considered its psychological operations (PSYOP) support program in Viet-Nam as short-term in nature" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58). The 1970 program memorandum resolved that "it will disengage as rapidly as feasible from information support to governments there". It also warned against "taking for granted a favorable psychological climate in Japan" (same volume, Document 99, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d99). After Saigon fell, posts reported that "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). In 1977 the new administration's USIA held that "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79).

## How this was done

I worked from a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer search index of all 553 volumes and from the volumes' published contents. I first read the editors' chapter headings in the public-diplomacy and regional volumes, then searched for the period's own vocabulary for information work combined with East Asian and Pacific place names. I checked by exact-text matching that the search found what it claimed: about 94 in every 100 matches held the words literally. The rest were almost all the abbreviation "USI" in documents of 1946–1950 on Indonesia, which the search engine treats as the same word as USIS, and I removed them. I also ran control searches that must and must not succeed. From the matching documents I retrieved 48 in full, chosen by relevance ranking and by headings naming USIA's Far East and East Asia offices. I read the shorter ones entire and the longer ones at their passages on the region, and built the argument on 22. I used the app's document-similarity data, a numerical measure of how alike two documents' texts are, to find neighbours of a key document. That added two documents to the reading, the 1955 USIA status report and the 1970 program memorandum. It also found three pairs of documents printed twice. Similarity was treated only as a pointer to reading. Every quotation above and every excerpt in the collection was checked character for character against the stored text of its document. Every document cited here is in the collection, and I checked both before finishing. Exact counts, queries and verification are in the memo.
