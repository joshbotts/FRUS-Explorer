# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** FRUS shows American officials from the 1950s to the late 1970s judging their standing in Asia by press comment, opinion surveys and the state of each alliance, and finding it fragile whenever policy was unpopular. Their consistent method was to speak through Asians and keep the American hand low: work "through indigenous leadership" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403), let Vietnamese persuade Vietnamese (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32), and keep "US visibility" low (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). They doubted information alone could move opinion. The ambassador in Tokyo judged Japan's response to the bombing of North Vietnam "probably unchangeable over the short run" (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). After Saigon fell, posts reported that programs on American security commitments "lack credibility" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). The beliefs behind this were of an audience sensitive to race, war memory and Western dominance. The answer rests on fifteen documents.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** No document uses "soft power"; I searched every one of the 553 volumes for the phrase and found none, while in the same search a control phrase was found in every one of the 109 volumes I scoped and an impossible one in none, so I searched instead in the officials' own terms ("information program," "psychological strategy," the agency's name, "image of the United States"). FRUS prints the agency's own survey results only as summaries in memoranda; nearly two in three of the documents naming the agency in my scope sit in the five Public Diplomacy volumes, which select for policy, not for evidence of reception. The surveys themselves are on the shelf: the agency's Vietnamese Public Opinion Surveys (Record Group 306, NARA identifier 1127750) and its International Survey Research Reports (identifier 721251). Audience views in Asian languages, and anything after 1980, are beyond these volumes.

**Scope.** I searched the 109 volumes whose titles name East Asia, the Pacific or Public Diplomacy, about 62,000 documents once front matter and editorial notes are excluded. The collection holds the fifteen documents the argument rests on, dated from 1953 to 1977.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** If it means only the information and cultural programs, the Vietnam psychological-warfare documents fall away and the story becomes the information agency's alone; if it includes the example set by American policy, the Tokyo embassy's reports on the war become central. Meanwhile I included both, and kept wartime psychological operations only where they show a belief about the audience.
- **Where the region ends.** Including South Asia would bring in India's press and the Bandung conference; excluding Australia and New Zealand would drop the alliance-credibility reporting of 1975. Meanwhile I used the editors' East Asia and Pacific, Australia and New Zealand included, South Asia excluded.
- **Whether reception evidence must be Asian.** Requiring Asian-language or survey evidence would leave FRUS as a pointer only, because what it prints is American officials' reading of reception. Meanwhile I treated that reading as the object of study.

## Findings

### Speak through Asians, and keep the American voice out of sight (1950s)

The earliest strategy papers in scope assume that an Asian audience would resist an openly American message. The Psychological Strategy Board's plan for Thailand aimed at "utilizing every suitable operation to prepare the minds and emotions of the people to collaborate in the effort," and added: "In projecting U.S. leadership, however, we must work with and through indigenous leadership wherever possible." (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). The same caution cut the other way. When Philippine leaders took up "Asia for Asians," the Department refused to amplify it because it "would carry unsavory recollections of Japanese wartime slogan and could easily be misinterpreted by public at large to be anti-Western in intent" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 352). The information agency's own report for mid-1955 named the audience's sensitivities directly:

> In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred. (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190)

The same report credited a touring American orchestra with "Unparalleled enthusiasm, admiration and good will" across Japan. I found it by its similarity to a 1969 regional conference report, by no word search, and read its Far East section before citing it.

### Vietnam: a surrogate voice, and doubts about the audience (1963–1969)

In Vietnam the agency tried to work behind the South Vietnamese government. Asked to stress Hanoi's dependence on Peking, the agency's director warned that this "may only serve to increase the dimensions of the threat that looms from the North," and that "with over one million Chinese residents in South Viet-Nam who will also hear these broadcasts, we must exercise caution" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 128). A 1964 field review put the method plainly: "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese." The same review called the civil population "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32). By 1969 the agency described its psychological work as functions that "properly belong to and must ultimately be performed by the Government of Viet-Nam (GVN)" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58).

### Effectiveness was measured by reaction, and policy outweighed persuasion (1963–1970)

Officials judged success by press comment, surveys and the reception of visitors. John Glenn's 1963 tour of Japan was hailed as "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). Yet when policy was unpopular, no program could compensate. In August 1963 the director reported that Asian "Editorialists are almost unanimously critical of the Diem Government actions and generally blame the U.S. for continuing it in power." (1961–1963, Volume III, Document 308). In 1965 the ambassador in Tokyo reported that the share of Japanese naming the United States among their favourite countries had fallen from 52 to 38 percent. He saw the Japanese drawing "their ready identification of the American position with that of the Japanese armies in China," and concluded that "Only a rather clear-cut success for American policies is likely to reverse the trend." (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). By 1970 the Saigon surveys found only 26 percent of the public rating the United States a dependable ally, the "lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88).

### Image follows the alliance: modesty, low visibility and segmented audiences (1969–1971)

The agency's regional officers concluded that image depended less on American life than on bilateral ties. At their 1969 Manila conference, "PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on events in the United States, and they set a rule for seminars: "One caveat: US visibility should be low." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). The agency's director told the White House that "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" in the alliance (1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Document 67). Audiences were also read by community. Officers judged the overseas Chinese mood "apolitical, seeking to be neutral," and resolved "to take a very close look at the overseas Chinese as a USIS audience" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149).

### After 1975: credibility of commitment, and a turn to elites (1975–1977)

After Saigon fell, the posts' reports made credibility the problem. Bangkok warned that "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations." Singapore proposed programming in the hope "that we have all learned the lesson that America is not omnipotent" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). By 1977 the agency was brokering exchanges among Japanese, European and American economic editors, saying "We are dealing here with gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange." (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 2).

## How this was done

I worked from a full-text index of the whole series, in which the search treats "information program" and "informational program" as the same words. I drew the scope from volume titles, then found candidate documents by searching the officials' own vocabulary and by scanning document headings, and ranked the public diplomacy volumes' documents by how often they name East Asian places. Each search was run beside a phrase expected everywhere and an impossible one, and I read samples of what each search found to check that it found the words meant. I read each cited document whole or in the relevant section before citing it, and checked every quotation word for word against its document. One document was found by its similarity to another, by no word search, and read before it was cited. I traced the documents' archival sources from their source notes and resolved series through the reference files shipped with the application, labelling the likely matches as such.
