# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** FRUS documents a consistent official judgment from 1949 to 1980: in East Asia, America's standing depended less on American culture than on whether Asians believed American commitments. Jessup's 1949 outline for the President put "the basic attitude of the peoples of Asia towards the United States" above any short-term political swing (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). The agency's public affairs officers, meeting at Manila in 1969, agreed that the image of the United States "usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on events at home (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). After Saigon fell, posts from Tokyo to Rangoon reported doubts about American staying power (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). The messaging these officials designed aimed mostly at elites, sometimes at villages. They judged its success by proxies: press placement, attendance, a few polls. And they built it on explicit beliefs about an "Asian" or "Oriental" audience. The argument rests on 25 documents, from about 2,000 in the scope that use the information programme's vocabulary.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels, kept separate. *Where the printed documents came from*: the U.S. Information Agency's own records, Record Group 306 at the National Archives. These are the Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972 (Entry A1–42), the General Records Relating to the Joint U.S. Public Affairs Office in Saigon, 1966–1975 (Entry A1–31), the Director's Subject Files of 1963–69 (Entry UD WW 257) and the Office of Policy and Plans subject files (Entry P–207). The app's bundled archival data gives none of them a National Archives identifier, so their access status is not stated here. From the State Department, Record Group 59, the documents come from the Executive Secretariat's Subject and Special Files, 1953–1961, which hold the Operations Coordinating Board papers (Lot 62 D 430, Entry A1 1586C, National Archives identifier 2839191, partly restricted). They also come from the Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, 1947–1979 (Lot 63 D 351, Entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, partly restricted), which hold the NSC's semiannual status reports on the information programme. And from the Subject Files of the Vietnam Working Group, 1963–1974 (Lot 67 D 54, Entries A1 5305 and A1 5414, identifier 631403, unrestricted), the Central Decimal File, and the Central Foreign Policy File. *What the editors cited without printing*: Lot 62 D 430 and the NSC policy papers series again (Lot 66 D 95, identifier 2839192). Also the USIA Office of Policy and Plans Subject Files, 1953–1971 (Lot 59 D 260, Record Group 306, identifier 1257679, 47 linear feet, partly restricted). Also the Policy Planning Council's Chronological Files, 1947–1962 (Lot 65 D 101, identifier 1136548, unrestricted). And three USIA lots the bundled data cannot place (63 D 224, 62 D 255, 60 D 322). Among the 141 documents whose headings name an information agency, nearly half came from State's records and more than a quarter from the USIA's own (see the memo).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** *The term itself.* "Soft power" never appears in any of the 84 volumes searched. The plain word "soft" appears in about 350 documents there. Two control searches ran in the same pass: the phrase "Department of State" turned up in every volume, and a nonsense string turned up nowhere. So the question had to be asked in the period's words: information program, psychological warfare, USIS/USIA, Voice of America, cultural exchange, public diplomacy. *The audience research itself.* About 160 documents in the scope mention polls or surveys alongside the information agencies, and about 40 of those also name Japan. But FRUS prints USIA research only in summaries, such as the Saigon survey of 1970 below. The research reports sit in the USIA's Office of Research files in Record Group 306, which the bundled data names but gives no identifier. *The Pacific islands.* Only 6 documents in the scope pair the information agencies with Micronesia, Fiji, Samoa, Guam or the Trust Territory, so the Pacific half of the question is barely printed. Australia and New Zealand fare better, with about 70 documents. *Exchanges' measured results.* The Fulbright programme appears in about 380 documents, but none of them was read for an evaluation, and this overview makes no claim about one.

**Scope.** 84 volumes: 76 regional volumes on East Asia and the Pacific covering 1945–1980, plus the eight volumes the editors devoted to the information programme and public diplomacy. Together they hold about 39,000 documents, excluding front matter and the editors' own notes. The index searched holds all 553 volumes of the series.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I counted information, cultural and psychological programmes, from the USIS libraries to psychological operations in Vietnam. Adding economic aid and military civic action would bring in most of the aid literature and shift the findings toward development. Narrowing to cultural exchange alone would leave only a thin printed record.
- **Where the region ends.** I took East and Southeast Asia, with South Asia only where a volume covered "South and Southeast Asia" together. Including the subcontinent fully would bring in the large Indian material. Requiring the Pacific islands would leave almost nothing printed.
- **When it starts.** I began in 1945. The wartime "cultural relations program" with China (1942–1944) has three chapters of its own and would extend the story back. I left it out meanwhile.
- **Whether wartime psychological operations belong.** Vietnam supplies about a quarter of the collection. If propaganda aimed at the enemy and at a wartime ally's own villages does not count as soft power for you, Finding 4 drops out and Finding 2 narrows to the elite programmes.

## Findings

### 1. Officials believed the American image in Asia rose and fell with the credibility of American commitments

From 1949 on, officials treated Asian opinion as a judgement of American policy, not of American culture. Jessup's outline for the President put "the basic attitude of the peoples of Asia towards the United States" above "any temporary ebb or flow in the political tide". He called for an expanded information program to show sympathy with Asian nationalism (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). Twenty years later the agency's officers agreed that the image of the United States "usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US". They also agreed "that US prestige is diminished when we are not able to solve our own problems at home" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). After Indochina fell, the posts' reports were a catalogue of doubt: "The results in Indochina have heightened the ever-present doubts on Taiwan about USG intentions and capabilities in Asia" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). In 1977 the agency's East Asia director restated the task as "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79). From Jakarta the ambassador judged the new administration's public diplomacy "effective" yet reported "deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing”" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83).

### 2. The messaging strategy chose elites, and argued repeatedly about the villages

The programme's standing choice was the educated few. In 1952 the Saigon embassy reported its press and publications program aimed at leader groups. It admitted that rural populations "are not a primary target group", although they were the Viet Minh's: "Yet they are primary target for VM propaganda which follows Soviet line of giving masses saturation treatment" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). The Psychological Strategy Board's 1953 plan for Thailand called for working "with and through indigenous leadership" and for "suitable information programs for the general public, taking into account local cultural attitudes" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). By 1955 the NSC's status report recorded Thai provinces "saturated" by an anti-Communist indoctrination program run through the Thai government (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190). A 1964 inspection of Vietnam pressed the opposite case: "I believe the war is going to be won or lost out in the hamlets, where the VC are" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). Yet the 1966 summary of every Far East post's plan still read: "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups". It made exceptions for Vietnam, Laos and Thailand, and named as basic "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73).

### 3. Effectiveness was asserted from placement, attendance and anecdote, and only occasionally measured

Officials described their own success through outputs. In 1950 the expanded Southeast Asia programme reported: "There is already evidence, however, of increased usage of USIE news and feature material in the local press throughout the area." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31). The Saigon embassy in 1952 counted film audiences and library visits, then conceded: "All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). The 1955 report measured Japan in "8,000 column inches a month" reprinted by Japanese newspapers (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 185, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d185). Ambassador Reischauer judged John Glenn's 1963 tour "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). Self-assessments could be bleak: Indonesia in 1957 would "meet heavy odds" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207). Where a poll was printed, it cut against the programme. In May 1970, "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88).

### 4. In Vietnam, officials treated the American presence as the message's chief liability

From the first years of the war, information officers warned that what the United States did would outweigh what it said. In 1961 the agency's Far East director opposed defoliation on these grounds: "the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265). In 1962 the Saigon public affairs officer advised that "the image of the GVN in the world press can be no better than the face the GVN presents to the public" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 16, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d16). The 1965 guidance for the troop build-up started from the premise that "The American presence is a “foreign” presence that unavoidably generates resentments which are likely to grow as the presence grows". It therefore framed every American action as support for "the Vietnamese people’s struggle" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 48, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d48). By 1969 the agency described its psychological operations as always "short-term in nature" and was handing them to the Vietnamese government (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58).

### 5. Beliefs about the audience: race, nationalism and an "Oriental mind"

Three beliefs recur. First, Asian audiences were thought sensitive to race and to colonialism: in 1955 the agency "was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190). Second, audiences were thought to judge Washington's motives through local politics. In 1947 a USIS officer found Chinese students condemning American policy as "the product of a stupid or wicked government rather than the will of the American people" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 245, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d245). In 1971 the agency read the overseas Chinese as "proud of the achievements of Peking" but "reluctant to accept communist political and economic models" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). In 1969 the posts noted that "the Japanese image in Southeast Asia is not good", a problem for the policy of a larger Japanese role (Document 31 of the same volume). Third, there was a generalised "Oriental" or "Asian" mind, invoked, mostly by American officials, in 30 documents across 22 volumes, most of them from 1949 to 1963. The ambassador in Rangoon blamed the "failure or inability explain our motives convincingly to oriental mind", and added: "Bulk of Burmese people simply unable understand aid in terms of altruism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60).

### 6. With China, 1971–1978, officials managed messages by restraint as much as by publicity

The opening to Peking reversed the agency's habits. The Department asked USIA to hold back a Tibetan film: "While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 143, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d143). The agency's East Asia office proposed moon rocks, an astronaut and a library of books for Nixon's trip, to turn "a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one" (Document 146 of the same volume, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146). By 1978 the liaison office in Peking saw exchanges as the opening, while the line lasted: "we have an opportunity to enhance the communication process to a significant degree" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d152).

## How this was done

I searched a full-text index of all 553 FRUS volumes, read-only. I first read the editors' chapter headings to learn the period's own names for the subject, then defined two sets of volumes from the published volume list: 76 regional volumes on East Asia and the Pacific for 1945–1980, and eight information and public-diplomacy volumes. Front matter and the editors' notes were excluded. Searches ran with a positive control (a phrase present in every volume) and a negative control (a nonsense string, found nowhere). For each search phrase I checked, document by document, that the matched text really contains the phrase. The index treats words that share a root as the same word, so a check is needed. The checks held for every phrase used here. One false friend surfaced: in 1948–49 "USI" means the United States of Indonesia, not the information service, and those two volumes were left out of the rate-by-decade figures. Candidates came from three routes: document headings naming the information agencies, a ranked search for assessments of Asian attitudes, and the app's semantic neighbours, which link documents by overall similarity of content. The neighbours surfaced one document no word search had reached, the Saigon embassy's 1952 assessment. They are leads, not evidence, and that document was read before use. Of 61 documents retrieved whole, 25 are quoted. Archival series were identified from each printed document's source note and the editors' footnote citations, then matched to National Archives series through the app's bundled finding aids. I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection matches its document's text exactly. I also checked that every document cited here is in the collection. Exact counts, queries and denominators are in the memo.
