# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the East Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From 1949 to 1980, officials treated American appeal in Asia as an instrument to be aimed, not an asset to be counted. Their main strategy was indirection. Messages went out through Asian governments, delegates and media, with American visibility kept low (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403; 1955–1957, Volume XXI, Document 23; 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). They pictured the audience as nationalist, alert to race and to colonial memory, and moved by emotion more than argument (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 58; 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). They rarely claimed success. They judged effect by attendance and by events, and repeatedly concluded that deeds outweighed words (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 197; 1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 126). After Saigon fell, the question became the credibility of American commitment (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). The finding rests on twenty-two documents, read whole, from eighty-six volumes.

What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The phrase "soft power" appears in none of the 553 volumes; I confirmed the search worked with a phrase found in every volume and a nonsense control found in none. So the question has to be asked in the period's own words, as I did. Audience research is the larger gap. I searched for opinion polls and opinion or attitude surveys alongside the information agency across all eighty-six volumes, with the same two controls. I found fewer than thirty documents, and only one of them in a regional volume. The polls themselves, and the agency's country plans and post reports, stay in the information agency's own records listed above. Asian audiences never speak for themselves in this record. What Asians thought reaches FRUS only through American reporting.

**Scope.** I searched seventy-five regional volumes on East Asia and the Pacific from 1945, about 37,200 documents, and eleven thematic volumes on information and public diplomacy, about 3,300 documents. I excluded front matter and the editors' own notes, and used region words to search the thematic volumes.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I read it as the period's information programs, psychological strategy, prestige and cultural exchange; adding military training and economic aid as influence would bring in many more documents and shift the argument toward alliances.
- **Where the region ends.** I followed the State Department's Far Eastern bureau, Burma and Indonesia included and South Asia excluded; adding South Asia would bring India's neutralism into the audience picture.
- **How much of Vietnam.** I kept wartime psychological operations only where they speak to effectiveness or to the regional audience; taking them whole would make Vietnam most of the record, since the 1960s carry the densest coverage.

## Findings

### Officials preferred to speak through Asian voices

The earliest plans aimed messages at a target and kept the American hand light. In 1949 the information program in China sought "to foster possible rifts between Chinese Communists and the USSR" (1949, Volume IX, Document 558). The 1953 psychological strategy for Thailand set out to "prepare the minds and emotions of the people" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). It added a rule that recurs throughout this record:

> In projecting U.S. leadership, however, we must work with and through indigenous leadership wherever possible. (Document 403)

The departing ambassador in Bangkok judged that information programs had already brought "the dangers of Communism" home to the Thai people. He asked for more staff, "preferably personnel having at least a basic knowledge of the Thai language" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 396). Before the Bandung conference, the Department coached friendly delegations while "wishing avoid show undue interest" (1955–1957, Volume XXI, Document 23). The agency's regional officers repeated the rule in 1969: "One caveat: US visibility should be low" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). By then even Vietnam's psychological operations were being handed over, since such work "must ultimately be performed by the Government of Viet-Nam (GVN)" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58).

### They pictured an audience moved by nationalism, race and memory

In 1952 the embassy in Saigon described an enemy propaganda that "exploits nationalist and racial feeling" (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). The agency's report for 1955 said that in the Far East it had to deal with "mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190). The plan for Burma in 1957 saw "emotional nationalism" tied to "memories of white man’s past colonial domination" (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 58). Its conclusion was a strategy of restraint:

> Patronizing attitudes or demands for approbation would tend to minimize our limited potential for effectiveness. (Document 58)

Ambassadors in Tokyo wrote of Japanese emotion. In 1954 one saw a "curious ambivalence in Japanese character" after the fishing boat was caught in American nuclear fallout (1952–1954, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 762). In 1965 another explained Japanese sympathy for the bombed North Vietnamese, who were "racially, culturally and geographically closer to the Japanese than are the caucasians who come from afar" (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). By 1971 the agency read the overseas Chinese differently: "The predominant mood is apolitical" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149).

### They doubted that words could do much without deeds

Measurement was thin from the start. Saigon's 1952 assessment counted films, libraries and English classes, then conceded the "degree cannot be accurately measured" (Document 116 above). The agency's 1957 report admitted: "Despite all efforts, the trend toward accommodation to Communist pressures in the area continued" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 207). In 1963 the agency feared a campaign against Indonesia's charges would seem "mere words without deeds" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 126). The sharpest statement came from a 1964 meeting of officials on Vietnam:

> there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories, or GVN lassitude appear to be fiery enthusiasm (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 197)

A year later the agency's director told the President that efforts to build Vietnamese unity "have not progressed to my satisfaction" (1964–1968, Volume II, Document 160). From Tokyo, the 1965 verdict was that the "basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run" (Document 46 above). The agency's regional officers concluded in 1969 that counting audience exposures should be dropped as a measure. They thought America's image depended "more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on what the agency said (Document 31 above).

### After Vietnam, the message became American staying power

In 1969 the regional officers already reported that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers" (Document 31 above). After Saigon fell in 1975, the agency asked its posts to reassess. Bangkok reported "Thais still see us as friends and admire much in the American system." Wellington reported a "belief that the US no longer has the ability or the desire to control events in Asia." Singapore's post proposed programming around the lesson "that America is not omnipotent" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). In 1977 the new Assistant Secretary asked ambassadors about "the future role of public diplomacy in support of our policies in Asia" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 81). From Jakarta the ambassador answered that a belief in American withdrawal persisted despite every statement: "I confess I am baffled by phenomenon" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83).

### The longer game was students, exchanges and broadcasts

Where officials expected lasting effect, they invested in people. The agency's plan for Indonesia in 1962 stressed youth, American studies and reading rooms, with "a demonstrated respect for Indonesia’s neutral stance." It warned that "we should refrain from glamorous, immediate-impact actions" (1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 289). In 1977 the agency's East Asia office promoted exchanges among Japanese, European and American economic editors on trade, "gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 2). The clearest measured success in this record is a broadcast audience:

> Since the cessation of jamming in 1977, VOA’s Chinese audiences have grown rapidly and are now estimated to number over 100 million listeners. (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 215)

## How this was done

I used the series' complete index of 553 volumes and searched it in the period's own vocabulary, which I took from the editors' chapter headings: information program, psychological warfare and strategy, the information agency, Voice of America and public diplomacy. I checked that each search phrase found the words themselves and not look-alikes. One abbreviation for the information service was set aside, because the search also matched the initials of the United States of Indonesia. Within the regional volumes these searches found about 700 documents in sixty-nine of the seventy-five volumes. They make up about one dated document in fifty in the 1950s and about one in twenty-three in the 1960s. I ranked candidates, read fifty-one documents whole, and quoted from twenty-two; all thirty-four quotations were checked word for word against the stored text. I traced each cited document's archival source, and each unprinted source its notes cite, through the app's bundled archival indexes. I also asked which documents most resemble two key memoranda, by no word search. That surfaced one document I had already found and none that I cite for the first time. Before finishing I checked that every quotation here was verified against its document and that every document cited here is in the collection.
