# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Officials never wrote of soft power; the phrase appears in no volume of the series. They wrote of information programs, psychological strategy and the American image. They planned messages to show sympathy for Asian nationalism and to expose Soviet imperialism {C:frus1949v07p2/d386}, believing that Asians would back whoever seemed to be winning {C:frus1950v06/d32}. They judged their own results guardedly: a Tokyo committee "has not thus far been effective" {C:frus1952-54v14p2/d820}, and the Information Agency's director found that efforts to build unity in South Vietnam had "not progressed to my satisfaction" {C:frus1964-68v02/d160}. Their recurring conclusion was that Asian audiences judged America by its deeds, its commitments and its staying power more than by its words {C:frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31}. About 1,300 documents in the 76 volumes on East Asia and the Pacific touch these programs.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I took information, psychological, cultural and exchange programs and officials' judgments of the American image; adding aid, the Peace Corps and military assistance would bring in the 60 documents that mention the Peace Corps alone and turn the question toward development policy.
- **Which region.** I took the volumes titled for China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and the Pacific and left out those titled for South Asia alone; two volumes mix South Asia with the rest, the 1951 volume titled Asia and the Pacific and the 1958 to 1960 volume on South and Southeast Asia, and I kept both, so their India and Pakistan documents are in the counts though none is cited.
- **Which years.** I searched 1945 to 1980; earlier years of missionary and wartime information work would extend the story but need a different vocabulary.
- **Whose assessment is *policymakers*.** I included Washington principals and public affairs officers in the field; narrowing to the Secretary and the White House would remove most judgments of effectiveness, which come from the Information Agency.
- **What counts as "effective."** I used only what officials said of their own results; if you want measured effect, the sources are the Agency's research reports, not FRUS.

## Findings

### 1. Washington read Asia as a revolutionary audience that would follow the winner

In 1949 the Department's consultants proposed to the President "[a] considerably expanded information program" for both Communist and non-Communist Asia {C:frus1949v07p2/d386}. Philip Jessup, reporting on his tour, passed on the French and British view that Asians "are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning" {C:frus1950v06/d32}. The planners treated momentum as more persuasive than ideology. A 1953 plan for Thailand aimed to extend American influence, "thus gradually creating a climate of victory" {C:frus1952-54v12p2/d403}. By 1954 the Operations Coordinating Board, the interagency body that followed up National Security Council policy, judged that Japanese reactions, "sensationally represented in their press," were prejudicing relations, and that the Tokyo committee for psychological strategy "has not thus far been effective" {C:frus1952-54v14p2/d820}.

### 2. In 1961 to 1963 credibility was the working constraint, and officials said so

In May 1961 the Agency's Far East office was told to start "a major propaganda campaign on Viet Cong insurgency" {C:frus1961-63v01/d49}. Within months its head warned that chemical defoliants in an Asian country could bring "a harvest of ill-will" across the region {C:frus1961-63v01/d265}. The public affairs officer in Saigon told the Ambassador that "We cannot do more with the image of the GVN than is made available by the GVN itself" {C:frus1961-63v01/d339}. A foreign correspondent, excluded from an American-only briefing, wrote that he no longer needed "to get my American propaganda from a source which restricts its outlets to its own people" {C:frus1961-63v02/d5}. In 1963 the Agency wanted a public campaign against Indonesian-led Afro-Asian propaganda and judged that without a diplomatic protest "much of our audience would tend to regard it as mere words without deeds" {C:frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126}. The Department replied that it was "questionable" to publicize its protests, to protect its aid program {C:frus1917-72PubDipv06/d133}. In September 1963 the Agency's director reported "virtually no sympathy for the Diem regime, except in South Korea and the Philippines" {C:frus1961-63v04/d108}.

### 3. In Vietnam officials measured, and concluded that a message could not outrun conditions

In 1964 the Agency's director passed the President the view of an interagency committee in Honolulu that "there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories" {C:frus1964-68v01/d197}. The same memorandum counted the Agency's reach: "Vietnamese audiences at USIS film showings last year totalled an estimated 28 million," USIS being the Agency's overseas arm. In February 1965 the director reported that efforts at national unity "have not progressed to my satisfaction" {C:frus1964-68v02/d160}. Three days later he sent the President an Agency study of a province south of Saigon, which found the rural population "largely apathetic" and said the main obstacles were "inadequate security and ineffective Government" {C:frus1964-68v02/d172}. By September 1965 a White House staff report nonetheless said propaganda and psychological operations in the South "have also expanded and grown more effective" {C:frus1964-68v03/d138}. When Washington planned educational television for Vietnam in 1967, the director took the task outside his role because "the effort should not be construed as a propaganda mechanism" {C:frus1917-72PubDipv07/d171}. The reasoning assumed, in my reading, that audiences would discount anything labelled propaganda.

### 4. With neutral and client governments, the message was to be carried by locals, and the messenger came under pressure

In 1962 the Agency's plan for Indonesia stressed youth, American studies chairs and "patient persistence," and warned that any American action had to rest on "a demonstrated respect for Indonesia’s neutral stance" {C:frus1961-63v23/d289}. By February 1965 the Agency's own posts there were under attack. Its director would not close them voluntarily, because "Such an action would be interpreted by both our enemies and friends as a retreat" {C:frus1964-68v26/d106}. In 1968 the director told the President that the Thai government "has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support," and asked him to press for a Thai-initiated one {C:frus1964-68v27/d386}.

### 5. After Vietnam the audience was seen as watching American deeds, and the message became reassurance

At the 1969 conference in Manila of the Agency's public affairs officers, its senior officers at overseas posts, the Agency's East Asia chief sent them a summary that said "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers," and the American image depended on bilateral issues more than on American domestic news {C:frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31}. In 1971 the Agency's director asked for a fresh review of Japan policy, asking whether there was "a psychological threshold for economic frictions" {C:frus1969-76v19p2/d67}. After the fall of Saigon, posts reported that Hong Kong viewed the American government as "very clearly in default" and that in Thailand, unless Washington clarified its policy, programs on American security relations "lack credibility" {C:frus1969-76v38p2/d105}. Assistant Secretary Habib's report on a 1975 tour of Southeast Asia, passed to the President, said "our affirmations of good intentions are welcomed by all," while doubt centered on Congress {C:frus1969-76ve12/d16}. A National Security Council staff member wrote that most Asian nations believed Vietnam-style revolutionary warfare "is something that happens to other people" {C:frus1969-76ve12/d17}. By 1977 another noted that Asians "have become accustomed to seeing the United States go through periodic cycles of intense involvement in the Pacific" followed by indifference {C:frus1977-80v22/d3}. The Agency's East Asia chief then wrote that "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power" was necessary {C:frus1977-80v30/d79}, and the Department told its posts it wanted "an especially close working relationship between State and USIA" {C:frus1977-80v30/d81}.

## How this was done

I identified the volumes on East Asia and the Pacific from the series' catalogue and added the volumes on information policy and public diplomacy, then searched the full text of the indexed volumes for the Information Agency, its overseas posts, the Voice of America and information, psychological and exchange programs, reading the editors' headings first. The search treats related word forms alike, so I checked the family against the documents themselves: in a sample of 40 spread evenly through the results, 33 held the intended words, and the other seven held none of them. I ranked documents that pair these programs with assessments of effect or of the Asian audience, retrieved the top ones whole, and read them before citing. Three documents were found by their similarity to a 1975 Agency memorandum, by no word search I ran, and were read before they were cited. Every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one I verified against its document, and every document cited here is in the collection. Officials' statements of effect are their own judgments, not measurements.
