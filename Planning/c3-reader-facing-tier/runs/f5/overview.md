# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The phrase "soft power" never appears in the series, so the answer lies in what the editors print under other names: information programs, psychological operations, cultural and educational exchange, and, in the volumes of the 1960s and 1970s, "public diplomacy". Thirty-one documents carry the argument, drawn from Foreign Relations volumes between 1948 and 1977. Policymakers described a repertoire of counter-propaganda against communism, persuasion aimed first at leaders and the educated, broadcasting and film, libraries, and exchanges, and they tied it to aid. They wrote that Asian audiences distrusted American words, heard colonialism in Western themes, and were hard to reach. They rarely claimed to measure success; from 1962 they mostly called the effort weak; and after Vietnam they concluded that audiences judged the United States by what it did. Representative documents: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122) and Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The printed documents came from two channels, which I do not add together. *Where the printed documents came from:* of the thirty-one, eight came from the State Department's central files (record group 59); six from State Department lot files (numbered accessions of Department records), namely the Policy Planning Council's Alphabetical Files (lot 61 D 167, entry A1 1583A and A1 1583B, NARA identifier 2198167, 1948–1961, partly restricted), the Executive Secretariat's Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (lot 63 D 351, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted), the Operations Coordinating Board's Subject and Special Files (lot 62 D 430, entry A1 1586C, identifier 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted) and one Board file on Singapore and Malaya (lot 61 D 385) that the bundled reference data does not identify; eight from the United States Information Agency's own records in record group 306 (the Director's subject files and Policy and Plans files, cited by accession such as 72 A 5121 and 68 A 4933 and by entries UD WW 257, UD WW 193, A1 42, P 104 and P 207); five from the Johnson Library; and the other four from embassy files (two), the Nixon materials (one) and one citation printed as record group 305. The bundled data names the USIA series nearest to these: Subject Files of the Director (identifier 40967285, 1956–1972, 103 linear feet, partly restricted), Subject Files of the Office of Policy and Plans (identifier 1257679, 1953–1971, 47 linear feet, partly restricted) and Chronological Files of the Executive Secretariat (identifier 40967113, 1961–1966, partly restricted), but it cannot tie the printed entry numbers to them, so treat those identifiers as the nearest series, not as confirmed matches. *What the footnotes cite but do not print:* across the 711 documents on this topic in the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes and the public diplomacy volumes, footnotes give 392 pointers on 200 documents. The largest units are State Department files cited without a lot number (181 pointers), the Nixon NSC Files (33), the Executive Secretariat's NSC papers (lots 66 D 95 and 63 D 351, the same identifier 2839192), the Johnson Library's National Security File (19), the Kennedy Library's National Security Files (9), the Operations Coordinating Board's Subject and Special Files (9) and the Policy Planning Council's Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council (lot 62 D 1, identifier 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted). USIA's own files are named in only 11 footnote pointers in the whole index, so the USIA record is reached through the first channel, not the second.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *The term itself.* I searched all 553 volumes for "soft power" and found no document; the same search with the two words anywhere in a document finds 785 documents, none of them using the phrase. As controls, "Department of State" occurs in documents of every one of the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes, and a nonsense string finds nothing. "Public diplomacy" is a later editors' term: 211 documents in the series, 175 of them in volumes from 1977 on, and only two among the 38,059 documents of the 77 regional volumes. (b) *Measured effect.* The documents contain officers' judgments and one Public Affairs Officer's guess at Indonesian opinion; they do not contain audience surveys. The shelf that holds USIA's own research is record group 306: Program Files of the Office of Research and Intelligence (identifier 1254251, 1954–1958, partly restricted), the Policy and Plans Subject Files (identifier 1257679), and Records Relating to the Voice of America (identifier 7542975, 1948–1959, partly restricted). (c) *The audience's own voice.* Local newspapers, foreign ministries and polls in Tokyo, Saigon, Bangkok or Jakarta are not in the series; what FRUS gives is the American officials' report of them. (d) *Before 1940.* Only 11 of the 698 series described in the bundled data begin before 1940, so an empty pre-war roadmap would reflect the finding aids, not an absence of messaging.

**Scope.** I read the 77 volumes on East Asia and the Pacific from 1945 to 1980 (38,059 documents once front matter and editors' notes are set aside), plus the public diplomacy and information-program volumes, in an index that holds all 553 volumes of the series. Reprints are counted once, in the Vietnam or Japan volume, and South Asia and the Near East are left out.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I counted both attraction (exchanges, libraries, culture) and persuasion by information and psychological operations, because the documents do not separate them; if you want attraction only, the Vietnam material in finding 4 drops out and exchanges become a thin group. Used meanwhile: both, labelled.
- **Which region.** I took East Asia, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, including Japan, Korea, China and Taiwan, and left out South Asia; adding India and Pakistan would add a large block about neutralism. Used meanwhile: South Asia excluded.
- **Whose assessment.** I counted USIA officers and Public Affairs Officers as "policymakers", because they wrote most of the assessments of effectiveness; if you mean only the President and Secretary of State, finding 3 shrinks to a few remarks. Used meanwhile: both.
- **How to treat the period's vocabulary.** The officials' phrases about "Orientals" and "face" are reported as their beliefs and not as findings about Asian peoples.

## Findings

### 1. The repertoire: counter-propaganda, leaders first, and exchange

From 1948 the Department told its posts to contrast the United States with the Soviet Union, to reach the educated "middle group" before the masses, to print in Chinese for the Chinese minorities of Southeast Asia, and to put information beside aid. In Thailand by 1956 the program was described as indoctrination that worked downward from a small group of leaders. The funding the work received was uneven: the Philippine policy paper of April 1954 records cuts of about two-thirds to press services in a single year.

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, the Secretary of State to the Ambassador in China (Stuart), December 24, 1948 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d567): "If the United States is to win and retain moral leadership it must continue to discuss domestic conditions in the United States."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Memorandum by the Ambassador at Large (Jessup), November 16, 1949 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386): "A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for, both to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 484 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d484): "The USIA program emphasizes comprehensive anti-Communist indoctrination throughout Thailand."

### 2. Beliefs about the audience

Officials believed Asian audiences were unpersuaded by words alone: a 1953 report on Indonesia says the country is "more affected by our actions than our words" and hostile to non-Asian propaganda. They believed a Western theme could be heard as colonial: in 1954 the Department refused an "Asia for Asians" theme, and in 1955 USIA hesitated to expand in Malaya for fear of seeming to back colonialism. They believed the media and literacy limits were severe, and they wrote of "Orientals" and of capitulation being impossible once someone is cornered. Finally, in 1971 USIA's own posts judged the overseas Chinese apolitical and neutral. These are the officials' beliefs, and some show the period's stereotypes.

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 251 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d251): Indonesia, "which is more affected by our actions than our words and which is hostile to propaganda from any source, especially non-Asian."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447): USIA's objection was in part "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149): "The predominant mood is apolitical, seeking to be neutral, and concerned with economic and trade problems rather than strong ideological convictions."

### 3. How effective they thought it was: unmeasured, then weak

The early judgments are confident and unmeasured. Ambassador Heath in 1952 counted films, libraries and English classes as effective, then wrote that "degree cannot be accurately measured". A Public Affairs Officer in Indonesia guessed that 2.5 percent of 80 million Indonesians opposed American aims, 1.5 percent favoured them and 96 percent were unsure, and the report called the estimate unverifiable. By 1962 the Public Affairs Officer in Saigon asked whether anyone had "a really hard reading" on rural propaganda; in April 1964 the USIA director told the President that information and psychological warfare was "the weakest part of the war operation"; in February 1965 he said the effort to build national unity had "not progressed to my satisfaction"; and in Indonesia he predicted that USIS would soon have "nothing worthwhile left". Almost every such judgment is the program's own officer assessing it.

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, The Ambassador at Saigon (Heath) to the Department of State, September 20, 1952 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116): "All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122): "the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 197 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d197): "Does anyone have a really hard reading on how well GVN propaganda is working in the rural areas?"

### 4. In Vietnam and Thailand the message had to come from the host government

In 1961 Washington ordered a major propaganda campaign on the Viet Cong. By 1962 the Americans were debating whether to place information advisers at the Presidency and in the provinces; by 1964 USIA listed fourteen steps, including a large expansion of Voice of America broadcasting to the North. The field review of October 1964 explains the limit: the Vietnamese peasant would be "persuaded by other Vietnamese", and the government's information service was not doing its job. In 1968 the USIA director asked President Johnson to press the Thai Prime Minister to run a national information program of his own.

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32): "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386): "The Thai Government has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 85 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d85): "an unfortunately large amount of anti-Americanism under the surface in Viet-Nam."

### 5. After Vietnam: credibility follows policy

At the Manila conference of May 1969 USIA's East Asia officers wrote that the image of the United States depended on bilateral relations more than on events at home, and that many of them were unsure of American commitments in Asia. In 1970 the agency's first program memorandum said the United States would have to "lower its voice". After the fall of Saigon the posts replied that programs on security "lack credibility" until Washington states its policy. In February 1977 Sullivan, writing from Hong Kong, wrote that ASEAN and Japan had not been reassured by Washington's public position that nothing had changed, and in July 1977 USIA's East Asia office read the Administration's policy statements as meant to shore up "the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power". Two of the documents in this finding, the 1970 program memorandum and the 1977 Hong Kong telegram, were reached by comparing the numeric meaning fingerprints of documents that ship with the app, and not through a word search.

- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31): "PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105): "During this waiting period, programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations."
- Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXII, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Document 104 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v22/d104): "Rather than being reassured, the ASEAN countries and Japan interpreted our apparent unwillingness to come to grips with the “new realities” as another indication that the United States, after twenty years of exaggerating the extent of its interests in Southeast Asia, might now decide it had no interest there at all."

## How this was done

I first listed the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes by title, then searched for the editors' own vocabulary rather than the modern phrase, using exact-wording scans of every document's text (not the index's stemmed search, which treats "clearing" and "clear" as the same word). I counted documents naming USIA, USIS or the Voice of America by period (about one in seventy in the 1940s, one in forty-four in the 1950s, one in twenty-four in the 1960s and one in seventy-five in the 1970s, each among the dated documents of the 77 volumes; the exact counts, rates and the leading volume in each period are in the memo). A comparison of each document's nearest neighbours in the series' meaning-based reference data showed that three volumes of public diplomacy, which my first pass had missed because their titles do not name the region, held the Vietnam, Manila and 1970 papers. I then retrieved 95 documents whole, read passages of them, and quoted from 31, which make up the collection. Every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was checked against the full text of its document, and every document cited here is in the collection. Counts are of documents in this copy of the series, which holds all 553 volumes; they describe what FRUS prints, not what the government said.
