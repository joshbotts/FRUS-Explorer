# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series answers the question well for the years 1953 to 1977, when the United States Information Agency (USIA) and its overseas posts (USIS) were the government's instruments, and it answers it best in the volumes the editors gave to public diplomacy. The series never uses the phrase "soft power"; officials wrote of the "information program", "psychological" strategy, American "prestige" and "image". They judged effectiveness mostly by activity (audiences reached, copies distributed, classes filled) and by a few polls, and their own verdicts swing widely: confident in 1955, "the weakest part of the war operation" in Vietnam in 1964, an agency stretched past its proper role in 1967, and a situation "intolerable" in Indonesia in 1965. The beliefs about the audience that shaped the messages were that Asians wait to see who is winning, that neutralism and race feeling are the openings Communist propaganda exploits, that the Vietnamese lacked motivation, that Japanese opinion was pacifist and fearful of involvement, and that smaller friendly countries feel taken for granted. About thirty documents carry this argument (for example [[frus1955-57v09/d190]], [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73]] and [[frus1964-68v01/d122]]). Among the 105 volumes searched, which hold roughly 58,000 documents, about one document in forty-four names the information agency or its posts; the figure was about one in thirteen for documents of the 1960s (see the memo for the exact counts and how the hits were checked).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels, which should not be added together. *Where the printed documents came from* (the thirty documents the argument rests on): thirteen come from State Department files, namely the central decimal files and their successors (Record Group 59, the Department's records at the National Archives), including two named series, the National Security Council policy-paper files of the Executive Secretariat (lot 63 D 351; entry A1 1586E; NARA identifier 2839192; 1947 to 1979; 26 linear feet; restricted in part) and the Subject Files of the Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs (lot 69 D 260; entries UD-UP 174 to 177; NARA identifier 27022536; 1961 to 1969; 11 linear feet; unrestricted); ten come from the records of the Information Agency itself (Record Group 306, at the National Archives), cited by entry numbers such as UD WW 257, UD WW 193, A1-42, P-104 and P-142, which the bundled finding aids do not resolve to a catalogued series (the Agency's Office of Policy and Plans, 1953 to 1971, NARA identifier 1257679, and several Executive Secretariat series are the nearest listed and are leads only); and seven come from presidential libraries: five from the Johnson Library (three of them from its National Security File, NARA identifier 567979), one from Robert F. Kennedy's papers at the Kennedy Library and one from the Nixon National Security Council files. Access for the library files is not stated in the finding aids available here. *What the editors' footnotes cite without printing* (the channel across the ninety-nine East Asia and Pacific volumes, not specific to this question): the Department's central decimal files, most often; the Nixon National Security Council files and the Johnson National Security Files, each cited several hundred times; the Kennedy and Carter national security files; lot 93 D 82, "Vietnam Special Caption Messages" (NARA identifier 2225592; 1962 to 1972; closed in full); and lot 53 D 413, "Military Telegrams Relating to Korea" (NARA identifier 2127212; 1951 to 1954; restricted in part). For the thirty documents themselves the footnotes point at the Department's decimal class for international informational and educational relations (1955), the Policy Planning staff's Vietnam file (lot 71 D 273) and two Johnson Library files. The footnote channel records only the fragment of each citation and only decimal, lot and library anchors, so it cannot see citations to the Information Agency's own files.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) The phrase itself: a search of all 553 volumes for "soft power" found no document, and the controls worked ("Department of State" appears in 552 of 553 volumes; a nonsense string appears in none). The phrase is later than the period, so the absence says nothing about the idea, which is why I searched the officials' own terms instead. (b) What Asian audiences thought: FRUS prints officials' assessments and a handful of polls (one Japanese poll quoted by the ambassador in 1965, USIA surveys of one Vietnamese province in 1964, six countries in 1969 and eight in 1970), never the audiences' own words, and I found no Asian-language source; the shelf that holds the polls is the Information Agency's research reports (Record Group 306, Office of Research, entry P-142), and the area files on the Far East (Record Group 306, Director's subject files, entry UD WW 257), neither of which the bundled indexes catalogue. (c) Whether any message changed behaviour: there is no outcome measure anywhere in the set, only the officials' own appraisals. (d) The years before 1940: the only instruments the search surfaced were schools and the Boxer indemnity remission, and the bundled archival indexes reach few series before the war, so an empty pre-war roadmap is a limit of the tools, not of the archives. (e) Korea, the Philippines and the Pacific islands appear only in passing; the evidence is concentrated on Vietnam, Indonesia, Japan and Thailand.

**Scope.** I searched 105 of the series' 553 volumes (those on East Asia and the Pacific from 1931 to 1980 and the six on public diplomacy and information policy), about 58,000 documents with front matter and editors' notes left out, and read the thirty documents on which the argument rests. All 553 volumes are indexed on this machine, so the limit is the choice of 105, not a partial library.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I counted programs of persuasion and image (information, exchange, cultural and "psychological" programs, and officials' assessments of American prestige). Counting economic aid and the military presence as well would bring in most of the East Asia volumes and turn the question into one about American policy generally; counting only cultural exchange would drop the Vietnam material and most of the 1950s.
- **Whether psychological warfare belongs.** I included it where the Information Agency ran it, because the officials did not separate it from the information program. Excluding it removes the Vietnam finding but leaves the audience beliefs.
- **The region.** I took East Asia, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, and left out South Asia. Adding India and Pakistan would add the neutralism theme at its strongest.
- **The period.** The information agency begins in 1953, so I treat 1953 to 1977 as the core and the earlier material as background. Starting later would lose the 1950s beliefs about Asian psychology.
- **Whose judgment of effectiveness.** I report what officials said of their own programs, which is the only judgment the series holds; if you want audience reception, the answer must come from sources outside FRUS.

## Findings

### 1. Before the information agency, education was the instrument, and the 1949 closure of USIS in China ended it

In 1931 a Chinese education official cited the remission of the Boxer indemnity for schooling as an example the American government had set, and the American consul general replied that this was a subject "to which special attention would be given", in [[frus1931v03/d939]]. By 1948 the Embassy in China thought the information service a better tool than Fulbright money against anti-American feeling in the universities: "Embassy believes USIS (United States Information Service) is better instrument for this purpose", in [[frus1948v07/d55]]. In July 1949 the new authorities in Shanghai ordered the service shut, and the consulate protested that its loss meant "a corresponding impoverishment of the cultural and technical resources available to the people of China", in [[frus1949v08/d1576]]. The evidence here is thin: three documents, and the series does not describe the earlier instruments in the later vocabulary.

### 2. Officials believed Asians waited to see who was winning, and they counted success in activity

In April 1950 Ambassador-at-Large Philip Jessup reported the French and British view that "the Asians are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning", with the further complaint that the French were poor at public relations, in [[frus1950v06/d32]]. Answering a Senate inquiry in 1952, the American ambassador in Saigon judged that films "must be assumed" to be effective, and that "All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured", in [[frus1952-54v13p1/d116]] (audience of 75,000 to 100,000 a week for films; about 200,000 library visits a year). The 1953 Psychological Strategy Board plan for Thailand treated information as one strand of a program that also included guerrilla preparation and an appeal to the "ethnic bonds of the Thai peoples" across the region, in [[frus1952-54v12p2/d403]]. The Information Agency's own 1955 appraisal reports having to deal with "mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples" and Communist propaganda "designed to stir up race hatred", and sets out its Far East aim as to "orient Asian neutralists toward the U.S.", in [[frus1955-57v09/d190]]. Success in these documents is audiences, copies, tours and parades, not changed opinion.

### 3. In Vietnam the message became a war program, and the agency's own research said the message was not the problem

After a trip to Saigon in April 1964 the Agency's director told the President that "the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare", in [[frus1964-68v01/d122]]. A field reviewer that autumn put the difficulty on the Vietnamese: "The number one problem in Vietnam is motivation", and he described the civil population as "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32]]; that is the reviewer's belief about the audience, not evidence about the Vietnamese. In February 1965 the director conceded that efforts to build national unity "have not progressed to my satisfaction", in [[frus1964-68v02/d160]], and sent the President the Agency's survey in Long An province, which found a population "largely apathetic", whose main obstacles were insecurity and ineffective government, in [[frus1964-68v02/d172]]. By 1967 an internal review argued that some of the Agency's tasks "appear to lie outside the Agency\u2019s appropriate sphere of operations" and noted that in Laos USIS was "in effect the Ministry of Information for the Royal Lao Government", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d147]].

### 4. The audience answered back: race, empire and the limits of persuasion

Officials expected Asian audiences to read American actions through race and empire, and smaller friendly countries to resent neglect: a January 1963 working-group memorandum spoke of "the strong feeling in such countries that we take them for granted", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv06/d107]]. In 1963 the Agency proposed a campaign to "set the record straight" on "imperialism" and "neo-colonialism" in Indonesian-led Afro-Asian propaganda, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126]], and the State Department declined to publicise its protests for fear of its aid program, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv06/d133]]. In 1966 the Director forwarded a publisher's proposal to show people in the Far East "that we are not waging a racist war", using Asian-American and African-American public figures, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d72]]. Ambassador Bowles's view, passed on by the Vice President, was that the war was reported as "an uneven contest" between great powers and "a tiny nation of farmers", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d102]]. The results were measured in a few places. In Japan in May 1965 the share naming the United States among their three favourite countries fell from fifty-two to thirty-eight percent in five months, in [[frus1964-68v29p2/d46]]. In Indonesia the Director found "a situation that I can describe only as intolerable" and "nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation", in [[frus1964-68v26/d106]], and the Embassy concluded that the government did not want the libraries, in [[frus1964-68v26/d109]]. The Agency's 1970 survey after the Cambodia incursion found disapproval in most countries, with approval only in Australia and Manila, and "the most dramatic trend" a fall in confidence in American judgment, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv08/d94]].

### 5. Practitioners concluded that contact with elites and a low American profile worked; after 1975 the task was reassurance

The Far East area office's 1966 summary of its field officers' replies says programs were aimed at elites and that "Personal contact is accepted as our most effective medium", with budgets too small for mass efforts, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73]]. The 1969 Manila conference of field officers found that the image of the United States "depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US", and took up Assistant Secretary Green's "modesty, mutuality and multi-lateralism" as a theme, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31]]; the Nixon guidance to the media that August stressed "Asian initiatives", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv08/d30]]. The Agency's 1971 assessment of the overseas Chinese of Southeast Asia, an audience it treated as a group with its own loyalties, found the "predominant mood" to be "apolitical, seeking to be neutral, and concerned with economic and trade problems rather than strong ideological convictions", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149]]. What moved opinion was often a visit or an achievement: Ambassador Reischauer judged an astronaut's 1963 visit to Japan an "effective demonstration of typical American virtues", in [[frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127]], whereas the moon landing in 1969 had "a barely significant effect" on opinion of the United States in Japan and the Philippines, in [[frus1917-72PubDipv08/d34]]. After the fall of Saigon the task was reassurance: in July 1977 the State Department asked its posts for "the future role of public diplomacy in support of our policies in Asia", in [[frus1977-80v30/d81]]; a memorandum of the same month records that the Administration believed "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America\u2019s long-term staying power" was "real and necessary", in [[frus1977-80v30/d79]], and the Jakarta Embassy replied that a "deeply implanted and persistent feeling" of American withdrawal remained, in [[frus1977-80v30/d83]].

## How this was done

I defined the region from the series' own volume titles (99 volumes on East Asia and the Pacific from 1901 to 1980) and added the six volumes the editors gave to public diplomacy and information policy, then searched all of them for the Agency's names, for the officials' own terms ("information program", "psychological", "prestige", "propaganda") and for regional names. The index folds word endings together (it treated "USIS" and "USI", the United States of Indonesia, as the same word), so I confirmed every hit for the Agency's names against the literal text; of roughly fifteen hundred candidate documents, about thirteen hundred passed. I read the thirty documents the argument rests on in full where they were short and in part where they were long (the memo says which), and I checked each quotation here, and each excerpt in the collection, against the text of its document. I also used the application's semantic-vector files to find neighbouring documents of two anchors and to look at its map of the corpus; three of the thirty documents were reached first that way and are marked in the collection. Dates are the documents' own dates, not the volume's year. Counts for different decades are not directly comparable because the volumes are of very different sizes, and the set read is a judgment sample of the argument's documents, not a random sample.
