# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The phrase "soft power" never appears in FRUS; officials of 1949–1977 wrote instead of the "information program", "psychological strategy", the "image" of the United States and its "prestige". In that vocabulary, about 1,400 documents in the 81 East Asian and public-diplomacy volumes discuss the effort, and they show a consistent assessment: the information program could win attention among educated elites but rarely moved opinion against what American policy itself was doing. Ambassador at Large Jessup's 1949 review proposed that "A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for", to align the United States with Asian nationalism (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386); the National Security Council's 1957 report on the program conceded that "the trend toward accommodation to Communist pressures in the area continued" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207); Ambassador Reischauer in 1965 judged that Embassy and information-service work in Japan had "met with some success" intellectually while "the basic emotional response remains unchanged" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46); and the Information Agency's area office reported in 1969 that the American image in Asia "depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations … than on developments within the US" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). Twenty-three documents carry the argument below.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* more than half of the 1,400 come from the State Department's own files (Record Group 59), chiefly the decimal central files and, for policy papers, the Executive Secretariat's "Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers" (Record Group 59, entry A1 1586E, National Archives identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted under national-security exemptions, College Park), which also holds the annual reports on the information program; and about one in eight come from the Information Agency's own records (Record Group 306, the Records of the U.S. Information Agency, identifier 622) — the Director's subject files and the East Asia area office's files, which the bundled finding aids can only trace to an accession that NARA has since split across 3 to 18 series each, so no single shelf can be named; the presidential libraries' National Security Files supply most of the rest. *What the editors cited without printing:* the same National Security Council policy-papers series (its lots 63 D 351 and 66 D 95); the Executive Secretariat's "Subject and Special Files" (Record Group 59, entry A1 1586C, identifier 2839191, 1953–1961, partly restricted); the Policy Planning Council's "Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council" (Record Group 59, entries A1 1583E–F, identifier 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted); and, cited behind the 1957 report, the Information Agency's Office of Policy and Plans "Subject Files" (Record Group 306, entries UD-WW 151 to 382, identifier 1257679, 1953–1971, 47 linear feet, partly restricted), the closest shelf to the program's own policy guidance; the agency's "Alphabetical Subject Files Containing Policy Guidance Records" (Record Group 306, entry P 311, identifier 40943515, 1953–1969, partly restricted) is named in the volumes' source lists. Only about one document in four of the 1,400 carries such a citation, so this second list is thin.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Three things. First, the term itself: a search for "soft power" across all 553 volumes found nothing, against a control ("Department of State") found in every volume of the scope and an impossible test word found nowhere; the question has to be asked in the period's own words. Second, measured effectiveness: the posts' surveys, the Information Agency's opinion research and the country plans are summarized but not printed — the 1969 Manila conference report, sixty pages, survives in FRUS only as a summary, and the 1975 post cables are printed only as abstracts. Those are in the Information Agency's records (Record Group 306), where the bundled finding aids can name the record group and accessions but not one series. Third, the full round of 1952 embassy reports on information-program effectiveness: a search for the Department's 1952 circular asking every major post to assess its program found one printed reply, from Saigon; the rest sit in the State Department's 1950–54 decimal file under class 511 (the class the 1950s filing manual labels "International Informational and Educational Relations, Cultural Affairs and Programs").

**Scope.** I read the 72 postwar volumes on East Asia, Southeast Asia, China, Japan, Korea and Indochina from 1946 to 1980, plus nine volumes on the information program and public diplomacy, in which a document counted only if it named an Asian place: 81 volumes and about 35,000 documents, with front matter and editorial notes excluded. The 1981–1988 volumes and the wartime Far East volumes were left out.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Whether "soft power" means the information program, or influence in general.** If the first, the about 1,400 documents built here are the right set; if the second, aid, trade, base and treaty documents belong in it too (I did not count them), and the finding that image followed policy becomes the subject rather than a conclusion. Meanwhile I used the first.
- **Whether Vietnam counts as "messaging" or as war.** Counting the Vietnam psychological-warfare papers makes the 1960s the busiest decade by far (about one document in twenty of the regional volumes); leaving them out makes the story one of elite programs in Japan and Southeast Asia. Meanwhile I included them, but as one finding of five.
- **Whether officials' remarks about "the Oriental mind" are evidence of their beliefs about the audience, or of a figure of speech.** I treated them as evidence when they were tied to a recommendation about how to address Asians; I did not count every use of "face".
- **Which end date.** I stopped at 1980 because the 1981–1988 volumes were outside this pass; including them would add the Reagan-era public diplomacy program.

## Findings

### 1. From the start, officials judged the program by whether it could put the United States on the side of Asian nationalism — and doubted it could

In 1949 Jessup's review of Far Eastern policy proposed an information program "to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). The Psychological Strategy Board's 1953 Thailand plan added that "Southeast Asia is not a political entity but that each nation is an entity basically different from the others and requires special treatment" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). When the posts were asked in 1952 how effective their work was, Saigon measured reach — films, libraries, English classes oversubscribed — and read it as "intense interest in US by educated groups", while admitting that Viet Minh broadcasts "go unanswered" in the countryside (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). The annual reports to the National Security Council were candid: in 1955 the agency had to answer "mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples" and propaganda "designed to stir up race hatred" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190), and by 1957 "Despite all efforts, the trend toward accommodation to Communist pressures in the area continued" (Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207).

### 2. A belief about "the Oriental mind" shaped the advice: aid looked like weakness, and only American staying power would persuade

Fourteen documents in the scope use the phrase "Oriental mind" — more than half of the 25 in the whole series, although these volumes hold only about one document in nine — so it was a habit of thinking about this region rather than a commonplace of the series. The officials who used it tied it to a recommendation. General Ridgway in Tokyo in 1952: "we must by word and deed do everything within our power to overcome any feeling in the Oriental mind that our interest in Asia is casual, temporary or overshadowed by our interests in other regions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, China and Japan, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 551, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v14p2/d551). From Rangoon in 1953, Ambassador Sebald: "Bulk of Burmese people simply unable understand aid in terms of altruism" (Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60). From Saigon in 1954, McClintock: "In Asia give-away programs are too frequently mistaken in the oriental mind as meaning that the donor not only has a soft heart but also a soft head" (Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p2/d992). By 1958–1962 the same idea came back from the other side: Ambassador Durbrow reported Diem's conviction that "Not being Asians, Americans do not understand the Oriental mind and point of view" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 26, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d26), and Pacific Command's political adviser recorded the Saigon government's view that "American concepts, policies and capabilities do not always fit the oriental mind" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 29, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d29).

### 3. In Vietnam the information program became a weapon of the war, and officials feared what the war did to the American image across Asia

The Information Agency's Far East director warned in 1961 that defoliants would hand Communist propaganda a weapon across the region: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265). The Saigon public affairs officer asked in 1962 whether it was possible "to laugh at the VC, to make them look ridiculous and thus lose face?" (Volume II, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d197). Director Rowan told President Johnson in 1965 that "these programs to affect what the Vietnamese people think must be given the same status, the same concern, the same adequacy of working resources, as our military and economic operations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 37, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d37; see also Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197).

### 4. In Japan and among the region's elites, officials thought personal contact and visible achievement worked, but could not outrun policy

The 1954 progress report on Japan recorded "Vigorous information programs" to "combat leftist and neutralist influences" alongside "a serious increase in neutralist sentiment" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, China and Japan, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 820, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v14p2/d820). Ambassador Reischauer judged John Glenn's 1963 tour a model: his family "were effective demonstration of typical American virtues" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). Two years later, under the bombing of North Vietnam, he blamed Japanese opinion on "the ostrich-like pacifism of the Japanese" and conceded that the emotional response was "probably unchangeable over the short run" (Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46). The agency's Far East office in 1966 aimed its programs "at elite groups: government officials, university and high school teachers, media representatives, top-level business executives", with "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" as the common objective (Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). The 1969 Manila conference of public affairs officers concluded that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", that image followed the state of each bilateral relationship, and, for seminars, that "US visibility should be low" (Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

### 5. After the opening to China and the fall of Saigon, the measure became credibility

In 1971 the East Asia office saw in the President's trip to Peking a chance "to build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one" (Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, Document 146, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146), and turned to the overseas Chinese "as a USIS audience" (Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). After Saigon fell in 1975 the posts' reassessments, printed as abstracts, ran from Singapore's advice to accept "that America is not omnipotent" to Kuala Lumpur's report that "Confidence in US economic power, technological and scientific capability, and our leadership role … remains" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). By 1977 the Japan program was recast as communication among the industrialized democracies on trade, "gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 2, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d2).

## How this was done

I searched a full-text copy of the FRUS index (553 volumes) for the period's own words for the information effort — the agency's and service's acronyms, "information program", "psychological strategy" and "psychological warfare", "cultural exchange" — within 81 volumes chosen from the series' titles, and checked a sample of every phrase count against the documents' literal text, because the index treats different forms of a word as the same word. That check found that "USI" also meant the United States of Indonesia in 1948–49, so 172 documents were dropped, leaving about 1,400. Decade shapes are given as a share of each decade's dated documents in the regional volumes, with editorial footnotes still mixed in. I read 121 documents whole, quoted 23, and verified each quotation word for word against its document. The record groups and series come from the source notes and footnote citations stored with each document, resolved through the finding aids shipped with FRUS Explorer; I also used the app's document-similarity vectors to look for neighbours of three key documents, which led to one document (Saigon's 1952 report) that the full-text search had also caught but I had not read; similarity was treated as a lead only. Exact counts, queries and denominators are in the memo. I checked before finishing that every quotation here and every excerpt in the collection was verified against its document in the memo, and that every document cited here is in the collection.
