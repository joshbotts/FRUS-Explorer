# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Officials judged their information, cultural and psychological programs in the region mostly by activity and impression, and seldom by changed opinion. About thirty documents, out of roughly 58,000 in 105 volumes, bear on the question. They record the belief that Asians waited to see who was winning, and that race feeling and neutralism had to be countered (1950, Volume VI, Document 32; 1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190). They show programs judged effective although the effect could not be measured (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116), and the director of the United States Information Agency (the Agency, from here on) calling its Vietnam work the weakest part of the war (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122). They show field officers concluding that personal contact with elites worked best (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). And they show the Agency's own survey recording a sharp fall in confidence in American judgment (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 94).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels lead to archives. The first is the series the printed documents came from, named in their source notes. I tallied it over the 30 documents I selected. The second is the series the editors' footnotes cite without printing. I tallied it over the 99 volumes on East Asia and the Pacific.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The question's own term is absent. I searched all 553 volumes for the phrase "soft power" and found it in none of 321,749 documents. The same search found "Department of State" in 552 of the 553 volumes and a nonsense string in none, so the search works and the phrase is simply not there. I searched instead for the officials' words: the Agency's names and those of its posts, "information program", "psychological", "prestige" and "image". I also searched on the area office's names. The edition cannot show how Asian audiences themselves judged American messages. I did not search for foreign-language enclosures, and the Agency's surveys are cited but not printed. The Agency's Far East area-office files resolve to no catalogued series in the archival indexes I used; the leads above are the nearest. The edition is thin before the war: the phrase "Boxer indemnity" occurs in 99 documents in the East Asia volumes, mostly from the 1930s, and the archival indexes reach only 11 of 695 series for the years before 1940. Korea, the Philippines and Australia appear only in passing. Whether the officials' beliefs about Asian audiences were accurate is a question the edition cannot settle.

**Scope.** I searched 105 of the 553 volumes: 99 on East Asia and the Pacific, covering 1901 to 1980, and six whose titles name public diplomacy or the foreign information program. They hold roughly 58,000 documents once the volumes' front matter and the editors' notes are set aside, and I left every other volume out, South Asia and the Near East among them.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** The term is not the officials'. I counted only information, cultural, educational and psychological programs aimed at the region, so economic aid and military presence are left out as instruments; counting them would turn the question into one about aid and bases, which this set does not address.
- **What the region includes.** I took China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and the Pacific and left out South Asia; if you want India's audience, the search must be widened to its volumes.
- **What counts as policymaker.** I included field officers and the Agency's researchers as well as presidents, secretaries and ambassadors; restricting it to the senior group would drop the field reports on which much of the evidence on effectiveness rests.
- **What counts as effective.** I took the officials' own verdicts and the Agency's surveys, because the edition prints no outcome measure; a stricter rule would leave only the surveys.
- **Whose beliefs count as evidence.** I treat what an official believed about an audience as the official's belief and never as evidence of the audience's views.

Meanwhile my inclusion rule is this. A document goes in only if it assesses the effect, reception or audience of an information, cultural, educational or psychological program aimed at the region; or states a belief about the regional audience that shaped such a program; or records the program's end.

## Findings

### Before the Agency, the instruments were schools, scholarships and a consulate's protest (1931–1949)

The earliest instruments in the set are educational. In 1931 a Chinese education official cited the Boxer indemnity remission as an American example, and the American consul general stressed his government's interest in Chinese education (1931, Volume III, Document 939). In 1948 the embassy in China preferred the information service to Fulbright money as the means of answering anti-American feeling in the universities (1948, Volume VII, Document 55). In 1949 the Shanghai consulate protested when the Chinese Communist authorities ended the service (1949, Volume VIII, Document 1576). No Agency existed before 1953; these posts belonged to the State Department. The set is thin here.

> “Mr. Peck referred to the interest taken by the American Government in educational affairs in China, and said that this was a subject to which special attention would be given.”

> “Embassy believes USIS (United States Information Service) is better instrument for this purpose and in any event does not see how the Fulbright money could be used unless an outright grant were made to USIS.”

> “The cessation of these cultural and technical services of the United States Information Service means a corresponding impoverishment of the cultural and technical resources available to the people of China.”

### Beliefs about Asian audiences, and programs judged effective without a measure (1950–1955)

Ambassador Jessup reported in 1950 the view of the French High Commissioner and the British Commissioner that Asians would wait to see who was winning (1950, Volume VI, Document 32). In 1952 the Saigon embassy answered a Senate inquiry that films and the other programs must be assumed effective, and it reported weekly film audiences of 75,000 to 100,000 and library attendance of about 200,000 a year (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). A 1953 plan for Thailand treated information as one strand of a wider psychological program that included the ethnic ties of Thai peoples across the region (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). In 1955 the Agency itself named race feeling, fear of atomic weapons and neutralism as the problems it had faced (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190). Success was reported as audiences, not as changed minds. This is four documents from a decade in which about 250 documents name the Agency or its posts.

> “Both Pignon, the French High Commissioner for Indochina, and MacDonald, Commissioner for the United Kingdom in Southeast Asia, believe that the Asians are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning.”

> “All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured.”

> “In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred.”

### In Vietnam the message became a war program, and the Agency's own research said the message was not the problem (1964–1967)

From 1964 the Agency took over the Saigon government's information effort and the American psychological-warfare effort. Its director, Rowan, told the President that this was the weakest part of the war (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122). A field reviewer put the trouble in the Vietnamese public (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32). That is the reviewer's belief and not evidence about the Vietnamese. The Agency's own study at Long An in 1964 pointed elsewhere: apathy, produced by insecurity and weak government (1964–1968, Volume II, Document 172). In February 1965 Rowan told the President that efforts to build national unity had not progressed to his satisfaction (1964–1968, Volume II, Document 160). By 1967 the Agency's own review judged that some of its tasks lay outside its proper sphere. In Laos its post in effect served as the government's information ministry (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 147). This finding rests on the memoranda of four authors.

> “I came to the conclusion that the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare.”

> “The number one problem in Vietnam is motivation.”

> “The USIA researchers conclude that the population is largely apathetic and is primarily interested in ending the twenty years of war; they care less as to which side will win, although there appears to be a substantial degree of approval of the Viet Cong.”

### The audience answered back: race, imperialism and the limits of persuasion (1963–1970)

Officials expected Asian audiences to read American actions through race, colonialism and fear of involvement. In 1963 one official granted that smaller friendly countries had some justification for feeling taken for granted (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 107). The Agency proposed to answer Indonesian-led charges of imperialism and neo-colonialism in public (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 126). The State Department replied that publicizing its démarches to Indonesia was questionable, because it might endanger the aid program (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 133). Marks forwarded a publisher's proposal to show Asians that the war was not racist (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 72). The results were recorded. In Japan, the ambassador quoted the only available poll, in 1965 (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). Attacks on the Agency's posts in Indonesia, including the seizure of its libraries in 1964 and 1965, led Rowan to fear the end of his operation (1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Document 106). An Agency survey of eight countries after the incursion into Cambodia in 1970 recorded the change in confidence (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 94).

> “Similarly, the number of persons naming the United States as one of their three favorite countries has dropped from 52 percent to 38 percent since last December and those naming the United States as a country they dislike has risen from 4 percent to 8 percent.”

> “We shall soon have nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation.”

> “The change in confidence in U.S. ability to deal wisely with world problems, from before to after U.S. entry into Cambodia, is the most dramatic trend in the present study.”

### What field officers thought worked: elites, personal contact and the state of bilateral relations (1963–1971)

In 1966 the Far East area office summarized its field officers' replies. Programs were aimed at elites, and personal contact was judged the most effective medium (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). Officers meeting in Manila in 1969 judged that the image of the United States depended chiefly on bilateral relations, and they recorded Assistant Secretary Green's themes of modesty, mutuality and multilateralism (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). Guidance of August 1969 presented the Nixon doctrine as peace resting on Asian initiatives (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 30). Events moved opinion unevenly. In 1963 Ambassador Reischauer judged an astronaut's visit to Japan a success for the American image (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). A 1969 survey of six countries found that the moon landing had scarcely any effect in Japan and the Philippines (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 34). In 1971 the Agency assessed overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia as a group with its own loyalties, wishing to stay neutral (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149).

> “Personal contact is accepted as our most effective medium, yet the nature of our programs is such that we do too little of it.”

> “PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US.”

> “The moon landing improved considerably general opinion of the United States in France, but had a barely significant effect in Britain, Japan, or the Philippines.”

### After Vietnam the task was reassurance (1977)

In July 1977 an Agency memorandum said the Administration believed that confidence in America's staying power had to be restored (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79). That month the State Department asked its posts what role public diplomacy should play (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 81). The embassy in Jakarta replied that Indonesians still thought the United States was withdrawing, and that they heard its human-rights message as accusation (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83). One volume supplies half the documents of the 1970s that name the Agency, so I read this as a sample and not as a trend.

> “I don’t think there should be any serious doubt that this Administration believes shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary.”

> “I would welcome any comments that you, in collaboration with your PAOs, might have on the future role of public diplomacy in support of our policies in Asia.”

> “We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia.”

## How this was done

I took the index of the whole edition, which holds 553 volumes, as the universe, and ran a control first: a phrase found in nearly every volume, and a nonsense string found in none. I chose 105 volumes by their printed titles, searched them with the officials' own words, and dated each document by its own date and not by its volume's year. The search treats related word forms as the same word, which confused the Agency's initials with "United States of Indonesia"; I therefore re-read all of about 1,500 candidate documents and kept about 1,300 that name the Agency or its posts. That is about one document in 44 in the scope, and about one in 13 among the documents of the 1960s, a rate inflated by the six information volumes, which are dense in the Agency by construction. "Propaganda" and "prestige" occur only modestly more often in these volumes than elsewhere, so I used them only to find documents. I retrieved 62 documents in full, selected 30, and read 19 of the 30 in full and 11 in the opening and the passages around key words. Three of the 30 I reached first through a ranking and a map of documents by closeness in meaning, which misses about one in ten of the true neighbours; I read each before using it. Every passage quoted here is word for word from its document. The evidence on effectiveness is the officials' assessments, three Agency surveys and one poll quoted by an ambassador; there is no outcome measure. All of it depends on what the editors chose to print, and I did not test their selection.
