# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series never uses the phrase *soft power*, but it answers the question in the officials' own terms. This research rests on 25 documents from 1949 to 1978, chosen from about 1,200 documents in the regional volumes that name the U.S. Information Agency, its overseas posts or one of four phrases for information and psychological work (about one document in 26 of the roughly 30,000 in those 69 volumes), and from seven volumes of public-diplomacy papers. The officials who ran American information work described their message as contrast with communism, American strength and, after 1975, reassurance, and not as the attraction of American society. A November 1949 memorandum by Ambassador at Large Jessup asked that “A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). They believed their audience was nationalist, poor and wary of whites, and that a Vietnamese peasant was “going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese”, not by Americans (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32); Ambassador Reischauer in 1965 ascribed Japanese hostility to the bombing of North Vietnam to “ostrich-like pacifism” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46). And their own verdict on effect moved from counting what had been placed in the press, as in a 1955 National Security Council report (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190), to the Agency director's blunt 1964 statement to President Johnson that “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197). Every one of these is a US official writing; the audience appears only as reported by them.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels lead to archives, and they are counted over different sets of documents, so they are never added together. A Record Group (RG) is the National Archives' unit for one agency's records; an entry is a numbered finding-aid unit inside it; a lot file is a set of State Department records filed outside its central files and named by a year-and-letter number; an accession is the National Archives' record of one transfer of files; and the NARA identifier (NAID) is the number the National Archives' Catalog gives a series.

*Channel one, the series the printed documents came from.*

- **U.S. Information Agency records, RG 306** (the Agency's own files). Of the 42 documents in the public-diplomacy volumes whose headings name an Asian or Pacific place or office, 27, about two in three, came from this group; 8 came from State Department records (RG 59), 1 from RG 305 and 6 from presidential libraries. (The 42 are a loose finding aid, not a measured set: 9 headings name South Asia or the Near East and at least three of the documents are State Department papers.) The source notes of the documents cited here name: Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972, Entry A1-42; Office of Policy and Plans, Entry P-207; Director's office Subject Files (*DIRCTR Sub Files*), 1963–1969, accession 72A5121, Entry UD WW 257; and the Executive Secretariat Correspondence Files of the Director's office, 1973–1980, Entry P-104. None of these entries could be matched to a NARA identifier in the application's bundled catalogues; only the accession level matches, to many series. Two series are candidates by creator alone and are not verified against the boxes cited: NAID 40967285, Office of the Director, Executive Secretariat, 103 linear feet 1 inch, 1956–1972, access partly restricted; and NAID 1257679, Office of Policy and Plans, 47 linear feet, 1953–1971, partly restricted. Neither covers the 1973–1980 files of Entry P-104, whose dates fall after theirs end.
- **State Department lot files, RG 59**, behind the printed documents of the regional volumes: lot 63D351, Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (Executive Secretariat), NAID 2839192, 26 linear feet 3 inches, 1947–1979, partly restricted, the source of 19 of the roughly 1,200 regional documents; lot 62D430, Subject and Special Files (Executive Secretariat), NAID 2839191, 4 linear feet 5 inches, 1953–1961, partly restricted, 12 documents; lot 61D417, Meeting Summaries and Project Files (Executive Secretariat), NAID 2103073, 2 linear feet 7 inches, 1951–1959, unrestricted, 8 documents. Of those roughly 1,200 documents, 118 name State Department records (RG 59) with no file series given, 67 the Johnson Library's National Security File, 39 the Kennedy Library's National Security Files and 39 the Nixon Presidential Materials' NSC Files.

*Channel two, the series the editors' footnotes cite without printing.* Of the roughly 1,200 regional documents, about one in four carry at least one such footnote citation, about 550 citations in all: about 300 to decimal file classes of the Department's central files (a filing number, with no series identifier), about 130 to lot files and about 120 to presidential library files (36 to the Johnson Library's National Security File, 25 to the Nixon NSC Files, 18 to the Kennedy Library's National Security Files). In the public-diplomacy volumes only 4 of the 42 documents carry one (Kennedy and Johnson Library files, Kissinger's office files at the Nixon materials, and one State Department lot file). The lots cited most often resolve as: lots 66D95 (17 documents) and 63D351 (9), both NAID 2839192 above; lot 62D430 (11 documents), NAID 2839191 above; lot 62D1 (7 documents), NAID 2838992, a Policy Planning Council series on its activities with the National Security Council (the bundled title is cut off), 3 linear feet 1 inch, 1948–1961, partly restricted. Lots 62D181 (4 documents) and 61D385 (3) are not in the bundled catalogue. Lots 63D351 and 62D430 appear in both channels, over different documents. Access status for the library files was not established.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.**  First, the term itself: the exact phrase *soft power* returned no document in the 69 regional volumes (about 30,000 documents, editors' notes and front matter excluded), nor did the two words within three words of each other; the two words anywhere in the same document returned 127 documents, mostly *soft* beside an unrelated *power*, which were not read. A count run before the scope was set, over the whole corpus, was also zero. Related terms: *public diplomacy* appeared in 2 documents, *cultural diplomacy* in none and *Radio Free Asia* in none. To show the searching worked, a phrase certain to occur (*Department of State*) returned about 13,500 documents in the same volumes and a nonsense string returned none. Second, the audience's own voice: the documents are US government writing, so the audience is present only as officials reported it; no search could settle otherwise, and the shelf that holds the Agency's research on audiences is the Agency's records at the National Archives, summarised in these documents but not printed; the nearest research series in the bundled catalogue is NAID 1254251, Office of Research and Intelligence, 9 linear feet 2 inches, 1954–1958, partly restricted (a match by creator only), alongside the RG 306 candidates above. Third, controlled measures of effect: what the documents give are counts of output and a few surveys, and the research reports behind them are on the same shelf. Fourth, the cultural and private channels: *Asia Foundation* appeared in 12 documents, *Peace Corps* in 59, *cultural exchange* in 92, *educational exchange* in 54 and *Fulbright* in 278 (mostly the Senator, so not used); these were counted and not read, and no shelf was identified. Fifth, thin ground: the reading barely touched Korea after 1960, the Philippines, Indonesia, Australia and New Zealand, the Voice of America's audience in China and exchange programs. Last, if the start date moves before 1948 the shelf pointers thin out: 11 of the 698 archival series in the bundled catalogue begin before 1940, about one in sixty-three.

**Scope.** Seventy-six volumes: 69 regional volumes dated 1948 to 1980 (about 30,000 documents) and 7 public-diplomacy and information volumes (about 1,600 documents, of which 42 have headings naming an Asian or Pacific place or office), searched within the full 553-volume series. Excluded were editorial notes and front matter, the South Asia and Near East volumes, the 1942–1947 China volumes, and second editions (none fell in scope).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as *soft power*.** The documents never use the term, so I read it as American information and public-diplomacy work and officials' talk of image, prestige and credibility; if you also want aid, trade and military presence treated as attraction, the set grows well beyond the Agency's papers and the findings below would cover only one part of it. Rule used meanwhile: a document counts if it names the Agency, its posts or an information or psychological program, or if its writer assesses the American image.
- **What counts as east Asia and the Pacific.** I took every volume whose title names China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Indochina, Southeast Asia, East Asia, Northeast Asia, the Pacific, Indonesia, the Philippines, Laos, Cambodia, Malaysia, Taiwan or Asia, minus South Asia and the Near East; adding South Asia would bring in volumes that are named and excluded, at the cost of diluting the region.
- **Where the period starts.** I started in 1948 and left out the 1942–1947 China volumes; starting earlier would add wartime and early postwar information work, and the earliest document here is from 1949.
- **Whether enemy propaganda is in.** I dropped the word *propaganda* from the main search because in the Korea and Vietnam volumes it mostly describes enemy propaganda (it occurs in about one document in twelve of the regional volumes; the share that is enemy propaganda was not measured); keeping it would bring in a large body of papers about the other side's messages.
- **Whose assessment is a policymaker's.** I used officials of the Department, the Agency, embassies and the National Security Council, and did not argue from a foreign leader's belief about Americans reported by an ambassador; including those would add the audience's elite voice, but at second hand.

## Findings

### 1. The message was contrast, strength and reassurance, not attraction

From 1949 the stated task was to place American aims next to Soviet ones and next to the audience's own nationalism: Jessup asked for an expanded program “to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations” of Asian peoples and to expose the menace that Soviet imperialism posed to them, by contrasting the two powers' records (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). A September 1953 Psychological Strategy Board plan for Thailand said American leadership was to be projected through local leaders where possible, with information work one instrument among aid and paramilitary preparation (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). In 1955 the Agency's acting director told all posts to expect an upsurge of neutralism and to combat it. By 1963 the message about China was American strength delivered calmly, with the negative case left to others because the Agency's credibility on it was low. The 1969 objectives for the Midway meeting between President Nixon and President Thieu were written as a list of public images to strengthen (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 24, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d24).

- “A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386).
- “We can anticipate, too, an upsurge of neutralism. Our information program must energetically combat these tendencies.” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 189, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d189).
- The Agency's role would be to stress strength, “avoiding a preoccupation with the negative side”, because “our credibility on this negative aspect is relatively low” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 135, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d135).

### 2. Officials believed the audience was nationalist, poor, wary of whites and best persuaded by its own people

The December 1950 report of the joint State-Defense survey mission said white troops would always be associated with colonialism, that American information work was talking over the audience's heads, and that Americans must first learn the psychology of poor people (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d89). A July 1960 National Security Council policy kept the number of American officials to a strict minimum to head off the reaction expected to Americans in privileged positions (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, East Asia-Pacific Region; Cambodia; Laos, Volume XVI, Document 65, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v16/d65). A 1964 field review of the Agency's Vietnam post held that Vietnamese, not Americans, would persuade the peasants, and took a low view of the civil population. These are the officials' beliefs; the audience does not speak here.

- “At present we are talking over the heads of Southeast Asia, and doing so inadequately.” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d89).
- The policy line was to head off a reaction to Americans in “relatively privileged positions” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, East Asia-Pacific Region; Cambodia; Laos, Volume XVI, Document 65, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v16/d65).
- “Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese”, while the civil population was called “family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32).

### 3. Their own judgments of effect moved from counting output to doubting that messages could outrun events

The 1955 National Security Council report on the foreign information program listed placements and conceded an unreceptive press; its 1957 successor gave a country-by-country account of activity and, for Japan, a generally favorable public attitude beside rising dissatisfaction (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207). In June 1964 the Agency director put audiences at an estimated 28 million at Vietnamese film showings in 1963, and in the same memorandum said no program could turn defeats into victories (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197). By January 1966 a summary of the Far East posts reported programs aimed at elite groups and budgets too small for mass effort (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). A June 1970 report gave the results of a May survey of 637 adults in Saigon: the lowest rating yet, in thirteen surveys, of the US as a dependable ally, with 26 percent of the general public and 23 percent of the college-educated (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88).

- “the national press remained generally unreceptive to explanatory material on U.S. policy and Communism”, yet “a remarkable placement of material on the peaceful uses of atomic energy was achieved” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190).
- “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories, or GVN lassitude appear to be fiery enthusiasm” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d197).
- Among the images that hurt: “The image of a huge white nation attempting to suppress an Asian struggle for freedom.” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 100, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d100).

### 4. Officials concluded that the image followed policy and America at home

Reischauer reported in May 1965 the only available poll of Japanese opinion, in which those favoring alignment with the free world fell from 49 to 40 percent and those favoring neutralism rose from 22 to 32 percent; naming the United States a favorite country fell from 52 to 38 percent and naming it disliked rose from 4 to 8 percent. He judged the basic emotional response unlikely to change soon (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46). In June 1968 the Tokyo embassy read racial violence and unrest at home as making the American image less positive (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123). The 1969 Manila conference of public affairs officers concluded that Asians watch America but read its troubles for what they mean for policy toward Asia, and that the image depended more on bilateral issues (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). A November 1971 report found overseas Chinese communities pulled toward China by feeling and not necessarily by ideology (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149).

- “the basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46).
- “Racial violence and social unrest in America have roused concern over basic stability of American society” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123).
- “Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers”, and the image “usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations” than on events inside the US (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

### 5. After Vietnam the message became reassurance, and the audience was reported to doubt it

In July 1974 a National Security Council staff member judged Asian countries to be adjusting well to the Nixon Doctrine (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 10, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d10). In May 1975 a post-by-post summary after the fall of South Vietnam found Japanese opinion discounting the defeat and Hong Kong seeing the US government in default (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). Sullivan, reporting from Hong Kong in February 1977, wrote that ASEAN countries and Japan had read Washington's reluctance to reassess as a sign that it might decide it had no interest in Southeast Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXII, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Document 104, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v22/d104). In July 1977 the Agency was told the new Administration believed in shoring up confidence (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79) and posts were asked for views on public diplomacy in Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 81, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d81); Jakarta replied that the program was effective but the withdrawal fear persisted and the human rights message seemed accusatory (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83). Only in Beijing in October 1978 was an agency opening an audience and not reassuring one: the International Communication Agency's post there called the enlarged scope for its funded activities “real and important” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d152).

- “The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese since the US withdrawal two years ago”, while Hong Kong saw the US as “very clearly in default” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105).
- The Administration believed in “shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79).
- Jakarta reported a “deeply implanted and persistent feeling” that the new administration was withdrawing from East Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83).

## How this was done

The research ran on a local full-text index of the whole series (553 volumes), whose search treats variant forms of a word (for example *clearing* and *clear*) as one word and so can return a false match, and a similarity search over each document's meaning. Volumes were chosen by their printed titles and document headings alone, without reading the editors' chapter headings. A first broad search ranked the regional volumes; because it included *propaganda*, which mostly described enemy propaganda in the Korea and Vietnam volumes, I narrowed it to American information work and did not use that ranking to choose documents. Candidates came from ranked searches on audience beliefs, effectiveness, neutralism and prestige, from a scan of document headings in the seven public-diplomacy volumes, and from the meaning search, run from four anchor documents (the Manila conference summary, the 1975 reassessment, Reischauer's 1965 report and the August 1966 Marks memorandum). Its neighbours are suggestions: the method finds about nine in ten of the true nearest neighbours, so a document's absence from a list says nothing, and two of the 25 documents (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–12, Documents on East and Southeast Asia, 1973–1976, Document 10, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve12/d10; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXII, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Document 104, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v22/d104) were reached only this way. Of about 1,200 candidates I retrieved 52 documents whole and read passages of the rest; 25 are in the collection and rest the argument, so any statement about the officials is a statement about those documents. Three checks guard the counts. First, each phrase family was tested against its literal text: of the 135 documents the index returned for *information program*, all but one contain the words in order, with only spacing or punctuation between them, and all 252 for *psychological warfare* and all 15 for *psychological strategy* do; *United States Information* matched literally in about nine of every ten of the 114 (the rest were *United States informed* and the like) and was used only inside the mixed family. Second, the other 201 volumes of 1948 to 1980 (about 77,000 documents) served as controls for terms the method supplied: *USIS* or *USIA* is only about one and a half times as common in the regional volumes (about 26 per thousand against 18), so the acronyms do not by themselves identify the region and the argument rests on documents read; *audience* is rarer in the region (about 8 per thousand against 12) and was not used as a marker. Third, a term certain to occur and a nonsense string, run in the same pass, behaved as they should. The share of documents naming the Agency or its phrases was about 33 per thousand in the 1950s, 53 in the 1960s and 19 in the 1970s, but a single volume supplies about one hit in ten in the first two decades and about one in three in the 1970s, so the shape is indicative only. I checked, before finishing, that every quotation here is a word-for-word passage verified against its document, and that every document cited is in the collection. Exact counts, rates and denominators are in the accompanying memo.
