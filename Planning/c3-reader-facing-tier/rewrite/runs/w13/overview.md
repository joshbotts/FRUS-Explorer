# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record answers all three parts of the question, though never in the words "soft power" or "messaging". From 1950 to 1977, the officials who ran America's information work in East Asia judged its standing there mainly as a matter of credibility: whether Asians believed American commitments and saw, in the words of a 1966 summary of the Agency's country plans, "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). Their strategy began in 1950 from a belief in "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 50, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d50), aimed its programs at elites, and from 1968 tried to hand information work to Asian governments. They were doubtful judges of their own effect: the Saigon embassy could only assume that American films worked (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116); by 1970 South Vietnamese ratings of the United States as a dependable ally were the lowest in thirteen Saigon surveys (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88); and in 1975 the posts reported that programs on American security ties in Asia lacked credibility (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). The argument rests on 22 documents, each retrieved in full and chosen by reading from a set of about 2,700 documents in 273 volumes that pair an information-program term with an East Asian or Pacific place.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Four parts of the question lie outside the printed record. First, the phrase "soft power": a search of every document in all 553 volumes, front matter and editorial notes excluded, found nothing. The same search for "Department of State" found it in 552 of the 553 volumes, and a nonsense string found nothing, so the search works and the absence is real; the question's term is an analytical frame to be mapped onto the period's own words ("information program", "psychological warfare", "image of the United States"). No shelf holds the phrase. Second, "messaging": the search index treats "messaging" and "message" as the same word, so a search for it finds diplomatic messages everywhere. In the four public-diplomacy volumes for 1961–1972 and 1977–1980 it matched about one document in six, against about one in ten in the series as a whole, while the control phrase "Department of State" ran at almost the same rate in both. The literal word "messaging" appears in three documents in the whole series. The run therefore read for strategy, not for the word. Third, whole periods: a scan of the volume list found no public-diplomacy volume for 1945–1960 or for 1981–1988, so those years are covered only by information-program chapters inside other volumes, such as "United States Foreign Information Program" in the 1955–1957 economic-policy volume and "Information Policy" in the 1961–1963 organization volume. Fourth, the audience itself: the Agency's survey reports appear only as officials summarized them (Finding 4), and no Asian audience speaks in its own records, since FRUS prints the United States government's papers. A scoping search pairing information-program terms with the Agency's research office and with opinion research was run but not audited, so it settles nothing either way. The shelf for the reports is the Agency's own records, Record Group 306; Asian audiences' own records lie outside FRUS altogether.

**Scope.** The whole 553-volume series was searched, and the scope set is about 2,700 documents in 273 volumes, dated 1945 through 1980, that pair an information, cultural or psychological-program term with an East Asian or Pacific place; front matter, editorial notes and the second editions of two volumes were left out. A check of the literal text removed about 170 index matches that lacked an information-program word or a place name, 161 of them on the abbreviation "USI", which documents of 1946–1950 use for the United States of Indonesia and which the index confuses with "USIS" (see the memo for exact counts, rates and denominators).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** If it means the information program (press, radio, film, publications and psychological operations), the 22 documents are the core and the finding stands; if it also takes in educational and cultural exchange, aid and the American example as such, a separate reading of exchange documents is needed, since this run did not separate exchange from information. Meanwhile the rule was: a document in the scope set in which an official assesses, plans or measures the American image or the information, cultural or psychological program toward an audience in East Asia or the Pacific.
- **Whether wartime psychological operations belong.** Including tactical leaflet and broadcast operations in Vietnam would swell the set with operational detail; excluding them, as this run did, keeps the focus on assessment and strategy but leaves out how the Agency judged its war work day to day.
- **Where the region ends.** Including South Asia (the 1950 request to Truman covers Southeast and South Asia together) widens the audience beliefs to India and Pakistan; holding to East Asia and the Pacific, as this run did, keeps the comparison clean but cuts some regional planning documents in half.
- **Which years.** Ending at 1980 matches the printed public-diplomacy volumes; extending to 1981–1988 would need sources outside the public-diplomacy series, since none of its volumes covers those years.

## Findings

### 1. Information as the companion of aid and containment, with race and colonialism in view (1950–1955)

In the first five years the information program in Asia was justified as the explanatory arm of military and economic aid against communism. In 1950 Webb asked Truman for an intensified information and exchange program for Southeast and South Asia, on the premise of "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 50, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d50). The planners already saw race and colonial association as the weak points. The Agency's mid-1955 status report said: "In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190). The Agency resisted widening its Malaya program partly out of "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism", before the Far East bureau overrode it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447). The Psychological Strategy Board's plan for a strategy based on Thailand belongs to the same frame (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403).

### 2. Vietnam, 1961–1963: a deliberate campaign, and warnings that the image was suffering

The Kennedy-era Vietnam documents show the Agency running a campaign while its own officers warned that American methods and the Diem government's conduct were damaging the American image in Asia. In 1961 the Agency wrote: "We are in the process of cranking up a major propaganda campaign on Viet Cong insurgency in VietNam." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 49, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d49). Its Far East chief feared what the use of defoliants would do: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265). In a September 1963 memorandum, Mecklin wrote that "the U.S. image was being hurt", and grounded his proposed tactics in a belief about the audience: "With Orientals in general and notably with the Ngo Dinhs, capitulation is virtually impossible if they are painted into a corner." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 140, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d140). The same memorandum is printed again in the Vietnam volume for late 1963; it is counted once. The Saigon information office made the same point about the South Vietnamese government's own image in 1962 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 16, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d16).

### 3. Beliefs about the audience: elites, "America watchers", and an apolitical diaspora

The Agency's picture of its audience shaped whom it addressed. Its 1966 summary of country plans for the Far East said: "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups", and "In all cases, however, the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). The 1969 conference of the region's public affairs officers concluded that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", and its officers "emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on events inside the United States (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). A 1971 analysis of the overseas Chinese of Southeast Asia as an audience found that "The predominant mood is apolitical, seeking to be neutral, and concerned with economic and trade problems rather than strong ideological convictions." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). Two further documents show the same habit of mind: a plan against Indonesian-led anti-American propaganda that expected the audience to discount words without deeds (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 126, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126), and a 1966 report of anti-Americanism under the surface in Vietnam, explained by the country's history of colonization (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 85, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d85).

### 4. Measuring effect: surveys valued, results discouraging, evaluation doubted

Officials measured effect with survey teams and opinion surveys, and were frank about how little they knew. Heath, reporting from Saigon in the early 1950s, reasoned: "Since many new customs FE can be traced to Amer films, must be assumed that films is effective program though difficult to determine degree." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116; the telegram's own grammar). A 1964 inspection valued the Vietnam survey teams as the best view yet of rural attitudes (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). The surveys brought bad news: in May 1970 "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88), and in 1971 Shakespeare told Kissinger that surveys of Japanese attitudes confirmed strains in the relationship (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Japan, 1969–1972, Document 67, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p2/d67). The 1969 conference judged the Agency's formal planning-and-evaluation exercise harshly: "the total exercise as presently constituted has not been worth the effort" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

### 5. Handing the message to Asian governments, then selling staying power (1968–1977)

From 1968 the Agency sought to move information work to Asian governments: Marks told Johnson that Thailand lacked a national information program and that the Thai should take over programs the Agency ran (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386), and in 1969 it described its psychological-operations role in Vietnam as short-term from the start (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58). Its 1970 program memorandum said that in Southeast Asia "it will disengage as rapidly as feasible from information support to governments there", while warning against "taking for granted a favorable psychological climate in Japan" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 99, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d99). After the fall of South Vietnam the posts reported in May 1975 that "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105), and a 1977 USIA East Asia memorandum held that "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79).

## How this was done

The run searched a full-text index of all 553 printed volumes of Foreign Relations of the United States, left out front matter and editorial notes, and confirmed that the search worked with a phrase that should be everywhere and a nonsense string that should be nowhere. Because the phrase "soft power" does not occur, the scope set was built from the period's own vocabulary: documents dated 1945 through 1980 that name an information, cultural or psychological program (for example "USIA", "USIS", "information program", "psychological warfare", "Voice of America", "cultural exchange", "image of the United States") and an East Asian or Pacific place. A sample of 40 and then a check of the literal text of every match removed the matches the index made on the Indonesian abbreviation "USI". From that set a reading list was drawn by searching document headings and by ranking documents in the East Asian regional volumes by how strongly they matched; two more documents were added from a list of documents whose overall wording most resembled the 1969 public affairs officers' conference, and both were already in the scope set. All 48 documents on the list were retrieved whole; eight were read entire, eleven more from their openings, and all forty not read entire in the passages around the search terms, each quoted passage with its context. A comparison of overall wording also showed three documents printed twice in different volumes, and each is counted once. Twenty-two documents met the inclusion rule above, and every quotation was checked by script to be an exact passage of its document. The archival sources come from each document's source note and the editors' footnotes, matched to National Archives series descriptions where the lot number allowed. The argument rests on a reading selection, not a sample: most of the 22 come from the three public-diplomacy volumes for 1961–1972, the 1950s rest on five documents and 1973–1980 on two. I checked before finishing that every quotation here is one the memo verified against its document and every document cited is in the collection.
