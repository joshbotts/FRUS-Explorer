# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From 1949 to 1980, the officials in these volumes judged America's standing in East Asia less by the appeal of American culture than by whether Asians believed American commitments (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386; 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31; 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105; 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83). Their information programmes aimed chiefly at elites, and in wartime Vietnam officers argued over whether the villages mattered more (1917–1972, Volume VII, Documents 32 and 73). They judged success mostly by press placement, visits and anecdote (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 185; 1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). An opinion survey appears in a Saigon case (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88). Their messages rested on stated beliefs about a race-conscious, nationalist audience, written of as the "oriental mind" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190; 1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60). The collection holds 25 documents, chosen from about 2,000 in scope that use the information programmes' vocabulary.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The phrase itself is absent: I searched for *soft power* across the 84 volumes in scope and found no document. The same pass found *Department of State* in every one of those volumes and a nonsense string in none, so the search was working. The plain word *soft* turns up in about 350 documents, in senses such as going "soft" on Chinese Communism. The agency's own audience research is also mostly absent. I searched the same volumes for *poll* or *survey* alongside *USIA* or *USIS* and found about 160 documents; about 40 of them also mention Japan. The shelf to search is the information agency's records in Record Group 306, though the app's data names no East Asia area-office series among them. The Pacific islands are nearly absent: the agency names together with *Micronesia*, *Fiji*, *Trust Territory*, *Samoa* or *Guam* found 6 documents. Australia and New Zealand, with the same agency names, found about 70, which I did not read. I also found but did not read the documents on evaluating the Fulbright and other exchanges, so this overview makes no claim about how exchanges were judged.

**Scope.** The search covered the regional volumes for East Asia and the Pacific from 1945 to 1980 together with the information and public-diplomacy volumes: 84 volumes, about 39,000 documents. Front matter and the editors' own notes were excluded, and the wartime cultural programme with China, before 1945, lies outside the period.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I read it as the information, cultural and exchange programmes and the image officials tried to build; widening it to aid and military civic action would bring in much of the regional reporting and change the findings from messaging to policy, while keeping it narrow leaves the question as answered here.
- **Who counts as a policymaker.** I included the information agency's field officers alongside Washington; restricting the answer to Washington would drop most of the evidence on effectiveness and on the audience.
- **Which region.** I took East and Southeast Asia and left Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific islands unread; including them would add a largely Western audience whose documents I found but did not read.
- **Which period.** I used 1945 to 1980; reaching back to the wartime cultural programme with China would add a pre-Cold War strand that I set aside.
- **The inclusion rule meanwhile.** A document came into the reading set if its heading named an East Asian or Pacific place within the information volumes, or named an information agency, propaganda, psychological strategy or public opinion within the regional volumes, or if a ranked search or a similarity comparison surfaced it; I kept the documents the argument rests on.

## Findings

### 1. The image followed the credibility of American commitments

The officials returned repeatedly to one judgement: Asians read America through its policy and its staying power, not its culture. Jessup's 1949 outline put it first.

> More important than any temporary ebb or flow in the political tide is the basic attitude of the peoples of Asia towards the United States. (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386)

At the 1969 Manila conference, the public affairs officers made the same point about bilateral ties: "PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). They added that "US prestige is diminished when we are not able to solve our own problems at home." After the fall of Indochina in 1975, the posts reported that "The results in Indochina have heightened the ever-present doubts on Taiwan about USG intentions and capabilities in Asia." (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). In the Carter years a report judged that "public diplomacy of the new administration in this area has been effective" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83). The same report warned of a "deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia." The administration's own statement called "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power" "real and necessary" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79).

### 2. Elites first, with the villages argued over

The standing strategy aimed at leaders and opinion-makers. Reporting from Saigon in 1952, Heath conceded that the masses "are not a primary target group" for the American programme (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116).

> Yet they are primary target for VM propaganda which follows Soviet line of giving masses saturation treatment. (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116)

A strategy paper of the same years urged working "with and through indigenous leadership" and offering "suitable information programs for the general public, taking into account local cultural attitudes" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). In the mid-1960s a summary of the area's programmes stated the rule plainly: "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups: government officials, university and high school teachers, media representatives, top-level business executives." (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). It held that "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives". From Vietnam, a field report pushed back.

> Granted, there is undeniable value in programs aimed at intellectuals, urban leaders and students, but I believe the war is going to be won or lost out in the hamlets, where the VC are. (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32)

### 3. Effectiveness was asserted from outputs and rarely measured

The officials mostly counted what they had placed. In 1950 the first sign of success was that "There is already evidence, however, of increased usage of USIE news and feature material in the local press throughout the area." (1950, Volume VI, Document 31). A status report on the information programme for the National Security Council measured Japan in column inches.

> Major Japanese newspapers continued to reprint from a translated wireless bulletin prepared by USIA in Japan an average of 8,000 column inches a month. (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 185)

These figures are the agency's own measures, not mine. A later report in the same series was frank about Indonesia, where the programme "will meet heavy odds" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 207). A single visit could count as success: John Glenn's tour of Japan was "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint, since that of Attorney General." (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). Measurement, when it came, could be unwelcome; a Saigon survey of 1970 found that the "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88). Heath's 1952 report had already conceded the limit: "All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured." (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116).

### 4. In Vietnam, the American presence was the message's liability

Information officers warned that American actions would speak louder than American words. In 1961 an information official wrote: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me." (1961–1963, Volume I, Document 265). A memorandum of 1962 held that "the image of the GVN in the world press can be no better than the face the GVN presents to the public" (1961–1963, Volume II, Document 16). Guidance from the joint public affairs office in Saigon started from resentment.

> The American presence is a “foreign” presence that unavoidably generates resentments which are likely to grow as the presence grows. (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 48)

The agency also saw its own role there as temporary: "USIA has, from the beginning, considered its psychological operations (PSYOP) support program in Viet-Nam as short-term in nature." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58). Vietnam accounts for about a quarter of the collection.

### 5. The officials held explicit beliefs about the audience

The messages rested on beliefs that Asian audiences were race-conscious, nationalist and quick to read American motives through local politics. A status report to the National Security Council recorded that "In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred." (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190). From China in 1947, a report on students found that they separated the American people from Washington.

> But American policy toward China, which they conceive to be the product of a stupid or wicked government rather than the will of the American people, is heartily condemned by all student liberals. (1947, Volume VII, Document 245)

A report on Burma held that the "Bulk of Burmese people simply unable understand aid in terms of altruism." (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60). It blamed American "failure or inability explain our motives convincingly to oriental mind". Phrases of that kind, such as *Asian mind* or *Oriental mentality*, appear in 30 documents in 22 volumes of the scope, most of them from 1949 to 1963. Most such uses are American officials describing Asian audiences; a few put the phrase in an Asian speaker's mouth. Audiences were also read against one another: "PAOs noted that the Japanese image in Southeast Asia is not good." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). An agency analysis judged that "Although the overseas Chinese appear proud of the achievements of Peking, they are reluctant to accept communist political and economic models." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149).

### 6. The opening to China called for restraint, then exchange

The opening to Peking made the agencies weigh what not to say. One memorandum put the balance this way: "While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking." (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 143). An agency proposal saw the President's trip as a chance.

> The President’s projected visit to Peking presents us with opportunities to build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 146)

Later in the decade, a report from Peking saw "an opportunity to enhance the communication process to a significant degree" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 152).

## How this was done

I searched an index of the whole printed series. Because the term *soft power* postdates every document, I first read the editors' own chapter headings to learn the period's words for it: information policy, the foreign information programme and public diplomacy. I then searched the regional and information volumes, 84 in all, for that vocabulary, with a phrase every volume contains and a nonsense string as checks on each pass. The search treats different endings of a word as the same word, so I re-tested its finds with an exact pattern match; that showed that in the 1948 and 1949 Indonesia records the abbreviation *USI* stands for the United States of Indonesia, not the information service, and I left those two volumes out of the counts by decade. The share of regional documents using the vocabulary rises from about 18 per 1,000 in the 1940s to about 52 in the 1960s and falls to about 32 in the 1970s; the 1940s figure leans on one volume, the 1949 China volume on the closing of the information offices. I drew 141 candidates whose headings named the information agencies or the region, plus documents a ranked search surfaced. Of the 141, nearly half came from State Department files and more than a quarter from the information agency's. One document, the Saigon report of 1952, I reached only through a comparison of documents by overall similarity of wording, which no word search found. I read 61 documents whole and quote 25. I checked every quotation here word for word against its document, and checked that every document cited here is in the collection.
