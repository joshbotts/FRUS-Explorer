# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The officials in these volumes never wrote "soft power", a term coined by Joseph Nye in 1990, after every document here; they wrote of the "information program", the U.S. Information Agency and its overseas posts (USIA and USIS), "psychological strategy" and "psychological warfare", "cultural exchange" and the "image" of the United States. In that vocabulary, about 1,400 documents in 80 of the 81 volumes searched discuss the effort, and they show one assessment held from 1949 to the late 1970s: the information program could win attention among educated elites, but it rarely moved Asian opinion against what American policy itself was doing. A 1949 paper in the Far East volume held that "A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386); a later report on the program conceded that "Despite all efforts, the trend toward accommodation to Communist pressures in the area continued" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207); Ambassador Reischauer judged in 1965 that in Japan "Intellectually we have met with some success" while "the basic emotional response remains unchanged" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46); and the agency's 1969 conference of public affairs officers in Manila reported that "the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). Twenty-three documents, all in the collection named at the end, carry the findings below; the research retrieved 121 whole and read about 35 at length.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The archives reach the historian through two separate channels, which this overview never adds together: the series the printed documents were taken from, and the series the editors cited in footnotes without printing. Of the 1,400 documents, more than half were taken from the State Department's own files and about one in eight from the Information Agency's; only a little more than one in four carries a footnote citation to an unprinted source at all, so the second list is thin. (The exact counts, rates and denominators for every figure in this overview are in the memo; see the memo.)

*Series the printed documents came from:*
- The State Department's central decimal files (Record Group 59), the largest single source. The memo names no single National Archives series identifier for them. The 1952 information-program reports are filed under class 5, which the 1950s filing manual labels "International Informational and Educational Relations, Cultural Affairs and Programs".
- "Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers", Executive Secretariat (Record Group 59, entry A1 1586E, National Archives identifier 2839192), 1947–1979, partly restricted under the Freedom of Information Act's exemption (b)(1), at College Park. It is the source of three documents quoted below (lots 63 D 351 and 66 D 95). This series is in both channels.
- "Subject and Special Files", Executive Secretariat (Record Group 59, entry A1 1586C, identifier 2839191), 1953–1961, partly restricted (lot 62 D 430). Also in both channels.
- "Meeting Summaries and Project Files" (Record Group 59, entry A1 1261, identifier 2103073), 1951–1959, unrestricted (lot 61 D 417).
- The Records of the U.S. Information Agency (Record Group 306, identifier 622): the Director's subject files and the area offices' files that the Public Diplomacy volumes print from. The finding aids that ship with FRUS Explorer trace these only to accessions that the National Archives has since split across 3 to 18 series each, so no one shelf can be named; among them are "Regional Subject Files" (identifier 48349732), "Subject Files of the Director" (identifier 1102141) and "Vietnam Working Group Subject Files" (identifier 42125423), for which those finding aids give no dates or access status.
- The National Security Files of the Kennedy and Johnson Libraries, the Nixon National Security Council Files, and smaller runs from the Eisenhower and Carter Libraries, which together supply most of the remainder. The memo gives no series identifiers for these.

*Series the footnotes cite without printing:*
- The State Department's decimal files again, the most cited.
- The same "Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers" (entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192) and "Subject and Special Files" (entry A1 1586C, identifier 2839191) named above.
- "Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council", Policy Planning Council (Record Group 59, entries A1 1583E and 1583F, identifier 2838992), 1948–1961, partly restricted.
- "Subject Files", Information Agency Office of Policy and Plans (Record Group 306, entries UD-WW 151 to 382, identifier 1257679), 1953–1971, 47 linear feet, partly restricted, cited unprinted in the report on the program quoted above (Volume IX, Document 207); the nearest named shelf to the program's own policy papers. A related series, "Alphabetical Subject Files Containing Policy Guidance Records", Office of Policy (Record Group 306, entry P 311, identifier 40943515), 1953–1969, partly restricted, is named in the volumes' lists of sources, though no single document here is tied to it.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Three parts of the question are not in the printed series. First, the term itself: a search for the exact phrase "soft power" across all 553 volumes found no document, while in the same pass the control phrase "Department of State" was found in every one of the 81 volumes searched and a deliberately impossible test word was found in none; no archive holds the term either, since it postdates the period, and the question has to be asked in the period's own words. Second, measured effectiveness: the posts' opinion surveys, the agency's research reports and its country plans are summarized in FRUS but not printed. This was established by reading rather than by a count: the sixty-page report of the 1969 Manila conference is not printed, and the 1975 reassessments from the East Asian posts survive only as abstracts, the editors noting that the cables themselves were not found attached. Their shelf is the Information Agency's records (Record Group 306, identifier 622), which the bundled finding aids can name only to the accession, not to one series. Third, the full round of 1952 embassy reports on how well the information program was working: a search for the exact phrase "circular telegram 208", the Department's 1952 request to the posts, found one document, Saigon's reply, and a looser search for "circular telegram" together with "208" found eleven, of which Saigon's is still the only reply printed. The rest are in the State Department's decimal file 511.00 for 1950–1954 (Record Group 59).

**Scope.** The search covered 81 volumes holding about 35,000 documents: 72 volumes on the Far East, China, Japan, Korea, Indochina and Vietnam, Southeast Asia, Laos and Indonesia from 1946 to 1980, and 9 worldwide volumes on information policy and public diplomacy, in which a document counted only if it also named an Asian or Pacific place. Excluded were the wartime volumes of 1940–1945, the 1981–1988 volumes, the Public Diplomacy volume on the First World War, the volumes' editorial notes and front matter, and about 170 documents in which the abbreviation "USI" stood for the United States of Indonesia rather than the information program.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "soft power".** I counted the information program's own vocabulary (the Information Agency and its posts, "information program", "psychological strategy", "psychological warfare", "cultural exchange"); widening it to economic aid, military display or "prestige" would bring in far more documents but weaker ones, and the "prestige" search could not be checked reliably, while keeping it narrow centres the answer on what the agency and its overseers said about persuasion.
- **Whether wartime psychological warfare is "messaging".** I included it, so Vietnam's propaganda war weighs heavily in the 1960s; the phrase "psychological warfare" alone matches about 320 documents in the scope, and excluding it would leave a more purely civilian, cultural account.
- **Which years.** I took 1946 to 1980; adding the 1981–1988 volumes, which hold matching documents (two of them were ranked when the scope was chosen), would carry the story into the Reagan years, and adding 1940–1945 would bring in the wartime information agencies.
- **Which region.** I took the regional volumes whole and, from the worldwide volumes, only documents naming an East Asian or Pacific place, including Australia; Korea, the Philippines, Indonesia, Australia and New Zealand are therefore represented mainly through regional reports, and a country-by-country answer would need those countries' volumes read on their own.

## Findings

### 1. The program as alignment with Asian nationalism, judged an uphill effort (1949–1957)

From the start the information program in Asia was framed as a way to place the United States on the side of Asian nationalism against communism. The 1949 paper asked for a program "to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386). Planning in the early 1950s worked country by country, on the premise that each nation of Southeast Asia needed separate treatment (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). Posts measured reach, such as film audiences, library attendance and waiting lists for English classes, and read it as interest. Saigon's 1952 report on the program's effectiveness held that "Such demand can only be construed as intense interest in US by educated groups", while conceding that the rural majority was not a target and that "Daily VM broadcasts maligning US effort AS go unanswered except for sporadic efforts by VOA which not timely nor too effective because of difficulty of reception with average type of radio receiver used here" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). The reports to the National Security Council judged the work uphill: "In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190), and a later report in the same volume conceded that accommodation to Communist pressure in the area continued despite every effort (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207).

### 2. Beliefs about the audience: "the Oriental mind"

The beliefs behind the strategies were often stated in a stock phrase, "the Oriental mind", which appears in 14 documents of the scope. That is more than half of its uses in the whole series, though the scope holds only about one document in nine, so the phrase belongs to this region's reporting and not to the series at large. The belief was usually tied to a judgment about how American aid and interest would be read. McClintock, from Saigon in 1954, warned that "In Asia give-away programs are too frequently mistaken in the oriental mind as meaning that the donor not only has a soft heart but also a soft head" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p2/d992). Another document of the same years held that "we must by word and deed do everything within our power to overcome any feeling in the Oriental mind that our interest in Asia is casual, temporary or overshadowed by our interests in other regions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, China and Japan, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 551, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v14p2/d551), and a report on Burma found the Burmese unable to understand aid as altruism (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60). By 1958–1962 the same idea was being turned against the Americans: Ambassador Durbrow reported Diem's conviction that "Not being Asians, Americans do not understand the Oriental mind and point of view" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 26, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d26), and in 1962 Martin recorded the view of Diem and Thuan, as McGarr presented it, that American concepts, policies and capabilities did not always fit Asian ways of thinking (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 29, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d29).

### 3. Vietnam: information as a weapon of the war, and a regional liability

Attention to the information program in the regional volumes rose from about one document in sixty in the 1940s to about one in thirty in the 1950s and about one in twenty in the 1960s, then fell back to about one in thirty in the 1970s (these rates count the editors' footnotes with the documents). The Vietnam volume for 1962 holds more of the 1960s documents than any other. Officials saw the war's information effort as a liability for the region beyond Vietnam: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265). Inside Vietnam they treated it as a weapon, down to ridicule: "Is it possible, for example, to laugh at the VC, to make them look ridiculous and thus lose face?" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d197). And they asked that it rank with the fighting: "On the American side, these programs to affect what the Vietnamese people think must be given the same status, the same concern, the same adequacy of working resources, as our military and economic operations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 37, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d37).

### 4. Japan: vigorous programs, and the limits set by policy

Japan shows most clearly how officials judged effectiveness against events. The Operations Coordinating Board's 1954 report recorded that "Vigorous information programs have been carried on to give the Japanese a better understanding of the United States and of world problems and to combat leftist and neutralist influences", and in the same report that "there have arisen a serious increase in neutralist sentiment, periodic flare-ups of anti-Americanism and an intensification of communist overtures to Japan" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, China and Japan, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 820, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v14p2/d820). Personal contact was the model success: a 1963 report on John Glenn's visit found that "Glenn and his family, through the warmth and sincerity of their personalities and their genuine enthusiasm and friendliness, were effective demonstration of typical American virtues" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). In 1965 Ambassador Reischauer judged that "Intellectually we have met with some success, and there is a growing awareness of the U.S. point of view, which has perhaps blunted the attack somewhat, but the basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run", and located part of the difficulty in the audience: "Such simplistic attitudes are possible in Japan because of the ostrich-like pacifism of the Japanese during the past twenty years" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46).

### 5. The area-wide model: elites, strength, and a low profile

Across the area the strategy was to reach elites and to project strength. An Information Agency review of the area's programs in the 1964–1968 Public Diplomacy volume found that "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups: government officials, university and high school teachers, media representatives, top-level business executives", and that "In all cases, however, the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). The 1969 Manila conference of public affairs officers started from a belief about the audience, "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers.", found that the American image depended more on each bilateral relationship than on developments within the United States, and advised: "One caveat: US visibility should be low." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

### 6. Credibility after the opening to China and the fall of Saigon (1971–1977)

In the 1970s the aim became credibility. Planners saw that "The President’s projected visit to Peking presents us with opportunities to build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 146, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146), and another document weighed the overseas Chinese as an audience for the agency's posts (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149). After the fall of Saigon the posts reassessed; in the editors' abstracts of their cables, the post in Singapore proposed "We should focus our programming on the benefits accruing from the end of our involvement; hoping that we have all learned the lesson that America is not omnipotent and that other nations cannot rely on Uncle Sam to do what they should be doing themselves", and the post in Kuala Lumpur found that "Confidence in US economic power, technological and scientific capability, and our leadership role in seeking solutions for world economic and social problems remains" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). By the Carter years the agency was turning to Japan as a partner in communication among the industrial democracies, insisting that "We are dealing here with gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 2, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d2).

## How this was done

The research searched a full-text index of all 553 published FRUS volumes, read-only, with the editors' notes and front matter set aside. Because "soft power" is anachronistic, the scope was built from the period's own words: 81 volumes were chosen by their printed titles and by how many matching documents each held, and a document was included when it used one of the information program's terms (in the worldwide volumes, only if it also named an Asian or Pacific place). Every search was paired with controls: a phrase that should appear everywhere ("Department of State") and a nonsense word that should appear nowhere. Because the index matches different forms of a word as the same word, samples of each phrase's matches were checked against the exact printed wording; that check found that "USI" often meant the United States of Indonesia, and about 170 such documents were removed, and it ruled out two phrase families ("prestige" and "lose face") whose counts could not be trusted. The archival sources were read from each document's source note and footnotes and matched to National Archives series through the finding aids that ship with FRUS Explorer, keeping the "taken from" and "cited without printing" channels separate. A comparison of documents by overall similarity of content was used only to find leads: Saigon's 1952 report was found that way, as a close neighbour of the 1969 Manila summary, and then read, and it also met the inclusion rule. 121 documents were retrieved whole; about 35 were read at length and the rest at the relevant passages. The 23 documents and the 29 passages the collection marks were each checked against the document's stored text, and every quotation was confirmed to be the document's own words rather than an editor's paraphrase. Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this overview appears word for word in a passage the memo verified and the collection holds, and that every document cited here is in the collection.

## Read next

The collection "Information programs and the American image in East Asia, 1949–1977" holds the 23 documents of this research, grouped under the findings, with the quoted passages marked; all but one (a 1964 Vietnam document) are cited above. What the historian could add would open what FRUS leaves out: the other embassies' 1952 replies on program effectiveness, in the State Department's 1950–1954 decimal file 511.00, would show whether Saigon's mix of confidence and doubt was typical; the Information Agency's opinion research, country plans, the full Manila report of 1969 and the 1975 post cables, in Record Group 306, would let the agency's own measures of effectiveness be set beside the policymakers' impressions; the volumes for Korea, the Philippines, Indonesia, Australia and New Zealand, read on their own, would test whether the elite model and the "Oriental mind" held country by country; and the 1981–1988 volumes would carry the question into the Reagan years.
