# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The phrase "soft power" does not occur in the series, so the answer is in the period's own words: policymakers described their messages to East Asia and the Pacific as information programs, propaganda and psychological operations, wrote down the tools they meant to use and the beliefs about the audience that lay behind them, and judged the results. Thirty-one documents, dated 1948 to 1977 and chosen from some 38,000 documents in the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes plus three volumes titled Public Diplomacy, carry that argument. On method, they show a repertoire of counter-propaganda aimed first at educated leaders and paired with aid, broadcasting, libraries and exchange, for example Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 567 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d567). On belief, they show officials who thought Asian audiences judged America by its deeds rather than its words (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 251 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d251)), heard colonialism in Western themes, and knew little of American policy. On effect, the earliest assessments say the result cannot be measured (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116)); from 1962 the programme's own officers call the effort weak (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122)); and after the fall of Saigon they say that programs on security lack credibility until policy is stated (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105)). Almost every judgment of effect is made by the officer who ran the programme; no independent measure of the audience appears in the documents I read.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** FRUS reaches the archives by two channels, and they are different. The first is the source note of a printed document, which says which file it came from ("came from"). The second is the editors' footnote that cites a file without printing it ("points at"). The two are counted separately and are not added together. Dates and access are as the bundled archival lists give them; "partly restricted" is their wording.

The United States Information Agency is the notable gap between the channels. Across the whole series 427 printed documents came from its files, but only 11 footnote pointers name its files, so the Agency's papers reach a reader through the printed page and almost never through a citation. A search for the documents nearest in meaning to four of the key documents points the same way: of the 82 it returned, 25 came from State Department files (record group 59) and 23 from the Agency's (record group 306). That is a pointer to where the close material sits, not a measure. The footnote pointers cover only lot numbers, presidential-library collections and decimal file numbers, and none is recorded before December 1910.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Four parts of the question are not in the corpus, each established by a search.

- *The term itself.* I searched all 553 volumes for the exact phrase "soft power" and found no document. Because the search tool treats words with different endings as the same word, I also searched for the two words anywhere in a document: 785 documents contain both, and none uses the phrase. The cases check the tool: the phrase "Department of State" turns up in documents of every one of the 77 regional volumes, and a nonsense string finds nothing. "Public diplomacy" appears in 211 documents, but 175 are in volumes covering 1977 to 1988 and only two fall among the 38,000 regional documents; an exact-spelling check confirmed 210 of those 211 and a looser check all 211. So in the regional volumes the officials did not call their effort soft power or public diplomacy; they called it information and psychological work.
- *Measured reception.* I scanned 381 sentences that join an audience belief to an information term, in 1,608 candidate documents; 33 of them also contained a word of effect. Among the documents I selected, every judgment of effect is that of the programme's own officer, and the opinion figure in the Indonesia report is a public affairs officer's estimate. One 1971 memorandum says opinion surveys of Japanese attitudes confirmed strains, but the surveys themselves are not among the documents I selected. The Agency's research reports and post-by-post reviews would hold what the series does not. The nearest described shelves, all in the Agency's record group 306, are the Office of Research and Intelligence Program Files (identifier 1254251; 1954 to 1958; 9 linear feet 2 inches; partly restricted), the Records Relating to the Voice of America (identifier 7542975; 1948 to 1959; 6 linear feet 6 inches; partly restricted) and the Office of Policy and Plans Subject Files named above.
- *Soft power as attraction.* I searched for the vocabulary of cultural and educational exchange, which appears in 329 of the 38,000 regional documents, and read the ones that looked relevant. The ones I found describe exchange programs and do not assess them, so the collection holds none. I did not identify a shelf for this part.
- *Even coverage of the region.* Japan, Korea and Australasia supply only three or four documents each; the argument rests mostly on Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia and on the Agency's own East Asia office. The Chinese mainland as an audience, what the Voice of America actually broadcast (beyond the Vietnamese expansion of 1964), and the volumes of 1945 to 1947, where I found nothing to select, were not followed.

**Scope.** The main set is the 77 volumes on East Asia and the Pacific (1945 to 1980), which hold about 38,000 documents once front matter and editors' notes are set aside; South Asia and the Near East are excluded. I added 20 volumes on information policy and national security, and three Public Diplomacy volumes whose titles do not name the region, and drew the argument from documents of 1948 to 1977.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I read it as the information, propaganda, psychological and exchange programs the officials themselves named, because the phrase itself is absent; if you want only attraction (culture, exchange, libraries), the set shrinks to a few descriptive documents, and if you count every mention of propaganda, the pool of candidates grows from fewer than 900 documents that name the information agencies to more than 4,000.
- **Which region.** I kept the 77 volumes for East Asia and the Pacific and one South and Southeast Asia volume that carries the Thailand report; South Asia and the Near East were not read for this question, and the Chinese mainland as an audience has not been followed, so including either is further work.
- **Who is a policymaker.** I counted both the officials who set policy (Secretary of State, National Security Council, ambassadors) and the Agency officers and embassy public affairs officers who ran programs, labelled by author; if you take only the first group, the section on effect largely disappears, because those judgments are the officers'.
- **What counts as an assessment of effect.** I accepted an officer's own judgment, labelled as such, since no independent measure was found; if you require independent measures, the answer to "how effective" becomes "not shown in FRUS" and the shelf in part 3 becomes the next step.
- **How to date a document.** I periodised on the document's own date, not the volume's; counting by volume year would move the 1940s rate, which is almost half one volume.

The inclusion rule I used meanwhile: a document is in if it states an intended message, channel or audience belief for East Asia and the Pacific, or assesses the effect of such messaging, and is not front matter or an editors' note; a reprint is counted once.

## Findings

### 1. The repertoire was counter-propaganda aimed at leaders, joined to aid and exchange

From 1948 to 1956 the documents state their intentions plainly. The Department of State told Ambassador Stuart to use the newsletter for the educated middle group, to show what happens where communists take power, and to keep "moral leadership" by discussing America's own faults; Stuart himself asked for a large expansion of the Information Service in every Southeast Asian country, with Chinese-language print for the Chinese minorities; Jessup asked for a larger program on both sides of the communist line; and by 1955 the National Security Council described the Agency's tasks in the Far East after Geneva and Bandung. Detail follows from the Philippines and Thailand: a National Security Council paper listed libraries, press, anti-communist films and radio as the tools, and a 1956 staff study described a program of indoctrination working down from a small group of top leaders to the rural border areas. The strategy assumed that the educated and official groups should be reached before the masses.

> If the United States is to win and retain moral leadership it must continue to discuss domestic conditions in the United States.

> The Department considers the USIS newsletter to be the most effective method for reaching the influential middle group of educated Chinese

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 567 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d567), the Secretary of State to the Ambassador in China, 24 December 1948.

> A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for, both to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386), Ambassador at Large Jessup to the Secretary of State, 16 November 1949.

> USIA concentrated on activities to strengthen the resolution of U.S. allies, orient Asian neutralists toward the U.S., and counter Communist attempts to soften Asian resistance to varied forms of penetration.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190), report prepared by the National Security Council, 31 August 1955. Related, not quoted: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East: China, Volume VIII, Document 499 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v08/d499) (Stuart's request for expansion); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d359) (the Philippines tools); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 484 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d484) (the Thailand study).

### 2. Officials believed the audience judged America by its deeds, and listened for colonialism

The Indonesia report of 1953 describes an audience that responds to American actions more than to words and is hostile to propaganda from any source, especially non-Asian. In the same report the public affairs officer guessed at the spread of opinion among 80 million people, and his guess was that nearly all were undecided. The Department in 1954 rejected an "Asia for Asians" theme for the Agency, and in 1955 an Assistant Secretary explained that the Agency resisted expanding in Malaya partly out of a fear of seeming to back colonialism. These are officials' beliefs, and in the Indonesian case a single officer's estimate; they are not measurements of what Indonesians thought.

> which is more affected by our actions than our words and which is hostile to propaganda from any source, especially non-Asian.

> 2.5 percent may be opposed to United States aims, 1.5 percent favorable, and 96 percent “unsure”

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 251 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d251), memorandum by the Secretary of State and the Acting Secretary of Defense, 27 August 1953.

> widespread use in Philippines and elsewhere of slogan “Asia for Asians” would be most unfortunate.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 352 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d352), the Acting Secretary of State to the Embassy in the Philippines, 13 February 1954.

> a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447), memorandum from the Assistant Secretary of State for Far Eastern Affairs (Robertson), 5 December 1955.

### 3. The audience was thought to know little of America and to be reached through weak media, in the vocabulary of the day

Officials wrote of audiences with little understanding of American policy, of poor means of communication, and of 'face' and the 'Oriental'. In my reading of the 381 sentences described in part 3, somewhat more than a third (145) named Asians, 'Orientals' or 'face'. I report this as the vocabulary of the officials, not as a finding about Asian peoples. The later documents also record surprises that these beliefs did not prepare for: a 1966 memorandum says it came as a surprise to most Americans how much anti-Americanism lay under the surface in Vietnam (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 85 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d85)), and a 1971 poll of the Agency's posts found overseas Chinese apolitical and neutral rather than ideological (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149)).

> Comparatively few nationals understand the current policies of the United States or the history and tradition of this country.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 50 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d50), memorandum by the Under Secretary of State (Webb) to the President, 21 June 1950.

> U.S. informational, cultural and educational activities in Thailand are conducted against the background of difficulties of communication and lack of adequate media of expression common to most underdeveloped Asian countries.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, South and Southeast Asia, Volume XV, Document 548 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v15/d548), report prepared by the Operations Coordinating Board, 1 May 1960.

> Our insistence that our way is right and the Japanese way is wrong does not make sense to many Japanese who detect a latent ignorance and lack of understanding of the Oriental in our endeavors to impose American methods and way of life

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 127 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d127), the Acting Political Adviser in Japan (Sebald) to the Secretary of State, 20 August 1949.

### 4. How effective they thought it was: unmeasured at first, then weak

In 1952 the Ambassador at Saigon counted films, libraries and English classes as effective and admitted that the degree could not be measured. In 1954 a National Security Council paper recorded that the information services in the Philippines had been cut by about two-thirds in fiscal 1954 for lack of funds (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d359)). In May 1962 the public affairs officer in Saigon asked the psychological operations committee whether anyone had a hard reading of how well Vietnamese government propaganda worked in the rural areas, after hearing that its content was poor (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 197 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d197)). In 1964 the Agency's Director told the President that information and psychological warfare was the weakest part of the war effort; in February 1965 he told President Johnson that efforts to build national unity among the South Vietnamese had not progressed to his satisfaction (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume II, Vietnam, January–June 1965, Document 160 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v02/d160)); and the same month he wrote that the Agency would soon have nothing worthwhile left in Indonesia. Every one of these judgments is the programme's own.

> All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116), the Ambassador at Saigon (Heath) to the Department of State, 20 September 1952.

> the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare.

> I was informed that the program is a failure in several provinces because of the lack of small bits of money to pay for such things as the running of a projector, or paper on which to print leaflets.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122), memorandum from the Director of the United States Information Agency (Rowan) to the President, 21 April 1964.

> We shall soon have nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106), memorandum from the Agency's Director (Rowan) to the Secretary of State, 18 February 1965.

### 5. The host government was the messenger, and it was the ceiling

In Vietnam and Thailand the Americans wanted the local government to speak to its own people. In May 1961 the Agency's Far East office was "cranking up" a major campaign on Viet Cong insurgency (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 49 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d49)). The documents then show why the effort could go no further than the host service: the Vietnamese government's image abroad could be no better than the face it showed at home; peasants would be persuaded by other Vietnamese; and in 1968 the Thai government had not given attention to a national information program. Washington's answer was to put advisers and money inside the host service, as in the Director's fourteen-step list of May 1964, which included more Agency staff in Vietnam and a large expansion of Voice of America broadcasts to the North (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 177 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d177)).

> the image of the GVN in the world press can be no better than the face the GVN presents to the public

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 16 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d16), memorandum from the Assistant Director for East Asian and Pacific Affairs, United States Information Agency, 12 January 1962.

> Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese.

> The number one problem in Vietnam is motivation.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32), memorandum from James N. Tull of the Agency's Far East office, 30 October 1964.

> The Thai Government has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386), memorandum from the Director of the United States Information Agency (Marks) to President Johnson, 6 May 1968.

### 6. After Vietnam, credibility was said to follow policy

From 1969 the Agency's officers say the audience judges the United States by its policy and commitments, not by its programs. The Agency's names appear in documents at a rate that rose and then fell: roughly one document in seventy in the 1940s, one in forty-four in the 1950s, one in twenty-four in the 1960s and one in seventy-five in the 1970s (the 1940s figure is not a regional rate, since 45 percent of it comes from the volume on China for 1949). In 1970 the Agency's first program memorandum said partnership meant the United States would have to lower its voice (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 99 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d99)); in 1971 the Director described strains in Japan that opinion surveys confirmed (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Japan, 1969–1972, Document 67 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p2/d67)); and in 1977 the Agency's East Asia office spoke of shoring up East Asians' confidence in American staying power (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79)). Two documents in this section, the 1970 memorandum and the 1977 telegram from Hong Kong (the telegram is quoted below), reached me through the search on similarity of meaning and through no keyword search.

> PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31), letter from the Assistant Director, East Asia and the Pacific, United States Information Agency, 5 August 1969, reporting a conference of the Agency's public affairs officers (PAOs).

> During this waiting period, programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105), memorandum from the Assistant Director for East Asia and the Pacific, United States Information Agency, 9 May 1975.

> Rather than being reassured, the ASEAN countries and Japan interpreted our apparent unwillingness to come to grips with the “new realities” as another indication that the United States, after twenty years of exaggerating the extent of its interests in Southeast Asia, might now decide it had no interest there at all.

Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXII, Southeast Asia and the Pacific, Document 104 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v22/d104), telegram from the Consulate in Hong Kong to the Department of State, 7 February 1977.

## How this was done

I worked in a read-only copy of the searchable index of all 553 volumes, and confirmed it could not be written to. I took the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes from the printed volume titles and counted terms in their roughly 38,000 documents by exact spelling (the Agency's name, its overseas posts' name, the Voice of America, psychological warfare, exchange, information program, public diplomacy and soft power), with the "Department of State" and nonsense-string controls described above, and I periodised each document by its own date. Counting by exact spelling avoids a tool habit that treats different word endings as one word, so the counts are of defined spellings. I then scanned the 77 volumes and 20 further information-policy volumes for candidate documents, scanned their sentences for audience beliefs and for words of effect, and used a measure of similarity in meaning between documents to find neighbours of the key memoranda; that search showed me I had missed the three Public Diplomacy volumes, and I then scanned them with ordinary text searches and selected documents from them. The meaning search finds about nine in ten of a document's true neighbours, so I treated its results as leads and read every document I used. I retrieved 95 documents whole and quoted from 31; every one of the 39 quotations was checked to be an exact passage of its document. Three memoranda appear in two volumes, a regional volume and a Public Diplomacy volume, with different annotation; I counted each once and cite the regional copy. Figures are for this index and for the selection FRUS itself made, not for the archives. I did not open the collection in the app, I did not read the underlying XML of the volumes, and the harvest of the National Archives catalogue was not available. The exact counts, rates and denominators are in the memo; see the memo.
