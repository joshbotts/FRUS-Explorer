# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Twenty-six printed documents, dated 1950 to 1977, show State Department and Information Agency officials judging America's appeal in East Asia and the Pacific. Their stated aim was "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship," and "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). They preferred that Asians carry the message and that American visibility stay low (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32; 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). They judged effect by use of their material in the local press, by demand for it, and by 1970 by opinion surveys (1950, Volume VI, Document 31; 1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116; 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88). They believed Asians judged the United States more by bilateral relations than by its life at home. After the American defeat in Vietnam, the question they asked was whether Asians still believed the United States would stay (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105; 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The question's own term is absent. I searched the full text of 21 volumes, the seven information volumes and fourteen East Asia volumes, for "soft power" with or without a hyphen, and found it in none. As checks, the phrase "Department of State" was found in every one of them and a made-up string in none. The officials wrote instead of an "image of the United States," of "psychological" objectives and of "public diplomacy," and no shelf holds the later term for these years. The opinion surveys themselves are not printed; documents only summarise a 1970 Saigon survey and 1971 assessments of Japanese attitudes. I searched every volume, leaving out front matter and editorial notes, for survey, poll or public opinion together with the information agency's names and Asian place names, with a made-up word as a control that found nothing. The same title search found the matching volumes up to 1980, so the edition stops there for this question, and I found no shelf for the later years.

**Scope.** I worked in all 553 volumes of the series, drawing on the seven volumes devoted to information and public diplomacy and on the East Asia volumes up to 1980. I retrieved forty documents whole and kept twenty-six, leaving out South Asia, the Near East, press relations in Washington, negotiation at the United Nations and internal reporting procedure.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What stands for "soft power."** The officials never used the term, so you must choose what counts: a narrower reading (culture and exchange only) drops most of the Vietnam and Thailand material, and a wider one (aid, trade or military posture as image) needs the area volumes' general policy papers, which I did not search for this.
- **Whether wartime information work belongs.** Keeping counterinsurgency information work in Indochina and Thailand lets Vietnam dominate, since about three in four of the area-volume headings found came from Vietnam volumes; leaving it out keeps the account to persuasion of elites in peacetime.
- **What "the region" covers.** Leaving out South Asia and the Near East removed nine headings that the word "Asia" matched; taking the Pacific to include Australia and New Zealand adds little, since they appear only in one 1975 round-up, as do Korea and Burma.
- **The late 1950s.** Accepting the set as it stands leaves those years nearly empty, which may reflect my searches and not the edition; filling them needs a further search of those volumes.

Meanwhile I included a document if it assessed, planned or instructed U.S. information, cultural, psychological or public-diplomacy work aimed at East Asian or Pacific audiences, and I had read it.

## Findings

### Information as anti-communist strategy, routed through local voices

In the early 1950s officials treated information work as one arm of a psychological strategy against communist expansion in Southeast Asia. A plan for Thailand called for "utilizing every suitable operation to prepare the minds and emotions of the people to collaborate in the effort" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). The same plan held that "each nation is an entity basically different from the others and requires special treatment." The 1952 Saigon assessment, written for a Senate inquiry, read demand for American material as "intense interest in US by educated groups" (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). It said rural and village people were "not a primary target group," and judged films effective "though difficult to determine degree." It told the program to "deliberately soft-pedal suggestions" that might foster anti-French feeling "or to stimulate unbridled nationalism." Planning for Malaya recorded "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism" (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 447).

### An image of strength, aimed at elites

A 1966 summary stated the aim plainly: "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). Officials also judged success by how people received an American in person. One report called a visit "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). It added that many "seemed, for first time, to appreciate openness of the U.S. space program as compared to Russian." At a 1969 conference, the public affairs officers who ran the posts held that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). They reported that the image of the United States depended more on bilateral relations than on events at home. Their advice on method was short:

> One caveat: US visibility should be low. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31)

### Counterinsurgency: a voice meant to become local

Where there was insurgency, officials wanted Asian governments to carry the message, and their view of the audience was at its harshest. A field review of the Vietnam program held that "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32). The same review called the population "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times." For Thailand the complaint was that "The Thai Government has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support" (1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Document 386). By 1970 the surveys gave a bleak verdict on the American role in South Vietnam:

> Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88)

### Respect for neutrality, and where the audience pushed back

Planning for Indonesia in 1962 warned that "we should refrain from glamorous, immediate-impact actions" (1961–1963, Volume XXIII, Document 289). It said that American efforts "must be pegged to a real understanding of and a demonstrated respect for Indonesia’s neutral stance." Later the information posts in Indonesia became targets themselves. Anti-American attacks on them had created, in the writer's words, "a situation that I can describe only as intolerable" (1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Document 106). The editors record that a White House aide judged the proposed response an over-reaction. In Japan in 1971, "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" (1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Document 67).

### China and the overseas Chinese

Toward China, officials weighed each message against opposite readings of it. One request put the balance this way:

> While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 143)

Another paper counted on Chinese curiosity, arguing that a proposed step, "by virtue of immense Chinese interest and curiosity about space, could generate favorable opinion toward the U.S. and its scientific accomplishments" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 146). A report on the overseas Chinese found an audience wary of both sides: "Although the overseas Chinese appear proud of the achievements of Peking, they are reluctant to accept communist political and economic models" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149).

### After the defeat in Vietnam: credibility as the message

A 1975 reassessment, post by post, found that "The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). It found that "Thais still see us as friends and admire much in the American system." It also warned that "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations." A later paper held that "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79). An embassy telegram reported the doubt that remained:

> We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia. (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83)

## How this was done

I worked in a read-only full-text copy of all 553 volumes. I searched the headings of the seven information and public-diplomacy volumes for East Asian and Pacific place names; of the 42 documents found, 33 concern the region, and the nine set aside belonged to South Asia or Near East offices that the word "Asia" also matched. I searched the headings of the East Asia volumes for information words. About three in four of the documents found came from Vietnam volumes, about thirty of them from the four 1961–1963 Vietnam volumes. A ranked search of the regional volumes surfaced groups of documents on Thailand and on Malaya. A search for documents whose wording as a whole resembles a chosen document's added the 1952 Saigon assessment, which no word search had found. I retrieved forty documents whole and kept twenty-six after reading. I counted the question's vocabulary across 21 volumes, with a phrase every volume should hold and a made-up string none should hold as checks. I checked every quotation here, character for character, against its document's text, and checked that every document cited here is in the collection.
