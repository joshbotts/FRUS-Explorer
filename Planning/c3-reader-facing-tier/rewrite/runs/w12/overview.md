# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From 1950 through the 1970s, officials judged America's standing in the region as a matter of credibility, though never in the words "soft power." Their goal was "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). They started from "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (1950, Volume VI, Document 50). They aimed their programs at elites, and later stepped back from supporting Asian governments' information work (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 99). They doubted their own measures. A report from Indochina could only assume that American films worked (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). Saigon surveys rated the United States lowest as a dependable ally in thirteen rounds (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88). A later assessment found security programs lacking credibility (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). This rests on 22 documents I read closely, among about 2,700 the search found.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The phrase "soft power" is not in FRUS: I searched all 553 volumes of the series and found nothing. The same search found "Department of State" in 552 of the 553 volumes and a nonsense string in none, so the search itself worked. "Messaging" cannot be searched either: the search treats "messaging" and "message" as the same word, and "messaging" itself appears in only three documents. I found no shelf for either word, and searched for the period's own terms instead. FRUS has no public-diplomacy volume for 1945–1960 or for the 1980s, which I established from the list of volumes in the series; for those years the shelf is the Record Group 306 and Record Group 59 series above. No Asian audience speaks for itself here; reception reaches FRUS only through American reports and surveys, and no shelf named in these volumes holds the audience's own record. Whether USIA's audience survey reports are printed I did not settle: I ran scoping searches pairing the information terms with "survey," "poll" and "office of research" near Asian place names, but did not audit them.

**Scope.** I searched every volume of the series and kept about 2,700 documents in 273 volumes, dated 1945 through 1980, that pair an information-program term with an East Asian or Pacific place. I left out editorial notes, front matter and second editions, and dropped documents that matched only on the search's word forms, among them 161 that matched "USI," the period's abbreviation for the United States of Indonesia.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Information only, or exchange too.** Counting only the information program and the American image keeps the answer on the United States Information Agency (USIA) and psychological strategy, while counting cultural and educational exchange would add programs I did not separate out.
- **Wartime psychological operations.** Counting Vietnam's battlefield operations would weight the answer heavily toward the war, while leaving them out, as I did, keeps it on how officials addressed civilian and elite opinion.
- **The region's edges.** My place list ran from Japan, Korea and China through Southeast Asia to Australia and the Pacific; narrowing it to Northeast Asia would drop the Vietnam, Thailand and Malaya documents that carry several findings.
- **The period.** I set the span at 1945 through 1980; reaching into the 1980s would add documents but no public-diplomacy volume.
- **Meanwhile, my inclusion rule:** a document in the found set in which an official assesses, plans or measures the American image, or the information, cultural or psychological program, toward an audience in East Asia or the Pacific; I set aside purely tactical operations and press procedure.

## Findings

### Information work began as the explanation of aid, already wary of race and colonialism

In 1950 an argument for more information work in the region began from ignorance of America. It cited "the generally meager knowledge and understanding of the United States on the part of the peoples of this area" (1950, Volume VI, Document 50). By the mid-1950s USIA faced a sharper problem:

> In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred. (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190)

A discussion of the program for Malaya recorded "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism" (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 447).

### In Vietnam the message was a campaign, and its own officers saw it backfire

An official wrote early in the Kennedy years: "We are in the process of cranking up a major propaganda campaign on Viet Cong insurgency in VietNam" (1961–1963, Volume I, Document 49). Another official feared the cost of American methods: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (1961–1963, Volume I, Document 265). A later memorandum warned that "the U.S. image was being hurt" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 140). The same memorandum shaped its tactics on a belief about the audience: "With Orientals in general and notably with the Ngo Dinhs, capitulation is virtually impossible if they are painted into a corner."

### Credibility meant strength, reliability and deeds, and the target was the elite

A review of the area's programs set the goal and the audience. "In all cases, however, the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives," it said, and "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). Planners expected words alone to fail, writing that "much of our audience would tend to regard it as mere words without deeds" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 126). A later assessment found that "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105).

### Officials saw the audience as watchful, pragmatic and quietly suspicious

At a 1969 conference, the region's public affairs officers, who ran each embassy's information work, described their audience: "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). The same officers held that "the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations." An analysis of another audience found that "The predominant mood is apolitical, seeking to be neutral, and concerned with economic and trade problems rather than strong ideological convictions" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149). An official warned that "there is an unfortunately large amount of anti-Americanism under the surface in Viet-Nam" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 85).

### Effect was assumed, then surveyed, and the evaluations disappointed

Early judgments of effect were inferences:

> Since many new customs FE can be traced to Amer films, must be assumed that films is effective program though difficult to determine degree. (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116)

Later, surveys gave bad news: "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88). The same conference said of a formal planning exercise that "the total exercise as presently constituted has not been worth the effort" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31).

### From propaganda to reassurance: stepping back from Asian governments, and selling staying power

USIA's planners resolved that "it will disengage as rapidly as feasible from information support to governments there" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 99). The same plan warned against "taking for granted a favorable psychological climate in Japan." In Japan, "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" (1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Document 67). By the late 1970s the task was reassurance: "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79).

## How this was done

I searched the full text of all 553 volumes for documents dated 1945 through 1980 that pair an information-program term (among them USIA, USIS, "information program," "psychological warfare," "psychological strategy," "Voice of America," "public diplomacy," "cultural exchange" and "American image") with an East Asian or Pacific place name, leaving out editorial notes, front matter and second editions. The search treats inflected forms as the same word, so I checked every found document for the literal words: about 94 in every 100 contain them, and most misses were the 161 "USI" documents for Indonesia. I chose a reading list from documents whose heading names both an information term and an Asian place, and from the best-ranked matches within the East Asian regional volumes. I retrieved 48 documents in full and quoted 22, and I checked every quotation word for word against its document's text. A measure of how alike documents are in content added two documents near the 1969 conference summary; both were already in the found set. The same measure showed three pairs of documents printed twice in different volumes, and I cite each only once. Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this overview matches its document word for word, and that every document it cites is in the collection.

## Read next

The collection "American soft power in East Asia and the Pacific: how policymakers assessed it" holds the 22 documents, each with its verified passages, arranged by theme and in date order within each, with a closing list of the archival series. Your decisions above would decide what to add. Adding exchange programs would answer the cultural side of "soft power," and the 1949 China volume and the 1955–1957 China microfiche supplement each hold more than forty matching documents that I did not read.
