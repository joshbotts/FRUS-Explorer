# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series never uses the phrase *soft power*: it does not occur in any of the roughly 35,900 documents of the 79 volumes I read, nor in any of the roughly 83,700 documents of a comparison set of 220 other postwar volumes (the search treats inflected forms such as *soft powers* as the same words). The judgments are there under the older names the State Department and the U.S. Information Agency (USIA) used: information program, psychological strategy, public affairs and, from the 1960s, public diplomacy. Read under those names, 33 documents, chosen by reading from the 79 volumes (about one in 1,100), carry the answer. In 1950 officials judged their own regional information material weak and aimed at no one in particular (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32)). Throughout the period they repeatedly assumed that Asian audiences follow the winner, respect resolve and read generosity as softness (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 266 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d266)). They credited American society and single events, such as a 1963 astronaut's visit to Japan, with more attraction than any message (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127)), and they blamed Tet and American racial violence for wearing the image down (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123)). After Vietnam and the opening to China, the message became reassurance about American staying power, which posts reported audiences did not credit until policy was clear (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105)). The officials' own verdicts on effectiveness are few and mostly anecdotal; the one measurement the agency printed shows trust in the United States falling in Saigon, and in 1977 a post called the new administration's public diplomacy effective while reporting that the belief in American withdrawal persisted (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83)). The 33 documents are a reading choice, not a random sample. They lean toward Vietnam and the years 1961 to 1977, because that is where the agency's own papers are best printed; Japan, Korea and the Philippines are thinner, and Korea and Australia and New Zealand are not represented among the 33.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** FRUS reaches the archives through two channels, and I report them separately and do not add them together. *The series the printed documents came from* (the editors' source notes, for the 33 documents): the State Department's central decimal files (Record Group 59, the Department's subject-numbered filing system), 15 documents, 11 of them from 1950 to 1957 and the others from the central files of 1967 to 1973 and the central foreign policy file of 1977; the records of the U.S. Information Agency (Record Group 306, chiefly the Director's Subject Files and the Office of the Director, ten documents, plus one from Record Group 305), for which I could not tie a series to a NARA (National Archives) identifier, an entry or an access status; three State Department lot files (office files held as separate lots), of which one resolves, lot 61 D 167: the Alphabetical Files of the Policy Planning Council, Record Group 59, entry A1 1583A/B, NARA identifier 2198167, 1948 to 1961, 48 linear feet 7 inches, access "Restricted - Partly" (FRUS labels this lot the planning staff's National Security Council files, so the match is the bundled finding aid's, not mine), while the other two, lot 61 D 385 and lot 65 F 98 (Record Group 84), did not resolve; and four presidential-library series (the Robert Kennedy papers at the Kennedy Library, the National Security File and the Marks papers at the Johnson Library, and the Nixon National Security Council Files). *The series the editors' footnotes cite without printing:* in the five public diplomacy volumes, 202 unprinted citations in 143 documents, led by lot 84 D 204 (whose finding aid is not in the bundled set), then lot 78 D 295, the Administrative Correspondence and Policy and Procedural Files of the Deputy Under Secretary for Management (NARA identifier 27022973, Record Group 59, entry UD-UP 80, 1968 to 1975, 7 linear feet 2 inches, "Restricted - Partly"), and lot 78 D 184, the Subject Files of Assistant Secretary John Richardson of the Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs (identifier 5038052, entry P 242, 1968 to 1976, 4 linear feet 8 inches, "Restricted - Partly"), and library series: Nixon National Security Council Files (27 citations), Carter Library National Security Affairs (21) and Ford Library National Security Adviser files (21); in the 74 East Asia volumes, about 12,800 unprinted citations in about 7,900 documents, led by lots 66 D 95 and 63 D 351, which both resolve to the Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers of the Executive Secretariat (identifier 2839192, entry A1 1586E, 1947 to 1979, 26 linear feet 3 inches, "Restricted - Partly"); none of the top lots I resolved was created by the information agency. For the collection's own documents the editors also cite, unprinted, lot 58 D 726, the Subject Files of the Officer in Charge for Malaya Affairs (identifier 2544398, 1955 to 1956, "Restricted - Possibly"), Eisenhower Library papers, the Johnson Library National Security File and the Nixon Kissinger Office Files. The bundled finding aids did not state a record group for the lots I name only by identifier.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** *Independent measurement of how audiences received the messages.* I searched the 74 East Asia volumes and the three public diplomacy volumes for 1961 to 1972 (77 volumes, about 35,500 documents) for opinion polls and surveys: 31 documents use the wording, and they print the agency's findings in fragments. Two controls ran in the same pass: a word that cannot occur found nothing, and the phrase *department of state* was found in 14,090 documents in all 77 volumes, so the search itself works. The shelf that might hold the research itself is a candidate, not a verified one: the USIA Office of Research and Intelligence's Program Files (identifier 1254251, inclusive 1954 to 1958, coverage 1951 to 1958, 9 linear feet 2 inches, "Restricted - Partly"), and the Office of Policy and Plans Subject Files (identifier 1257679, 1953 to 1971, 47 linear feet, "Restricted - Partly"). Both rest on NARA's creator heading, which has been right about subject only about 56 percent of the time in other checks. *The content of the broadcasts, films and publications themselves.* I ran no search for them; FRUS prints discussion of them, not the items. Candidate shelf: the Voice of America records (identifier 7542975, 1948 to 1959, 6 linear feet 6 inches, "Restricted - Partly"); no series in the bundled finding aids belongs to the agency's East Asia and Pacific area office. *The Asian side of the exchange.* The series prints American reports of what Asian leaders and audiences said; I searched no non-U.S. sources. *The wartime and occupation-era work.* Starting in 1941 would add nine volumes and about 8,700 documents, 50 of which mention the Office of War Information; I did not read them. *South Asia, the 1980s and the Pacific islands* were not searched. The bundled finding aids barely reach shelves from before 1940.

**Scope.** I read 79 volumes, about 35,900 documents: the 74 volumes of the series' East Asia, Pacific, China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and Indochina compilations for 1946 to 1980, plus five public diplomacy volumes (1961 to 1972, 1973 to 1976 and 1977 to 1980). Front matter and editors' notes were excluded from every count, the two second editions are not in the scope, and every figure is conditional on the searchable copy of all 553 volumes as taken on 25 October 2026.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I took it as American persuasion, information, exchange or psychological work addressed to an East Asian or Pacific audience, in the editors' own vocabulary; if you also want aid, military assistance and diplomacy itself treated as attraction, the set grows well beyond the information agency.
- **Who counts as a policymaker.** I counted any U.S. official, including ambassadors, public affairs officers and the agency itself, describing the audience, message or effect; if you mean only Washington decision-makers, the post-level reports that carry most of the effectiveness judgments drop out.
- **Where the region ends.** I included China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia and Indochina, the Pacific compilations and overseas Chinese audiences; adding South Asia or the Pacific islands means new searches, and leaving out Vietnam would remove the largest cluster.
- **How far back to start.** I started in 1946, and the collection's earliest document is from 1950; if you want the wartime Office of War Information and the occupation of Japan, the nine 1941 to 1945 volumes (about 8,700 documents) must be read.
- **How to count the *Oriental mind* wording.** I found 32 documents using it and split them, by my own reading, into 17 that apply it to how American policy or messaging would be received and 15 that do not; a stricter or looser reading of the margin moves that split.

Meanwhile my inclusion rule was: a document in the 79 volumes in which a U.S. official or the information agency describes the audience, the message or method, or the effect of American persuasion, information, exchange or psychological work directed at an East Asian or Pacific audience, chosen by reading.

## Findings

### 1. In 1950 officials judged their own information work weak and aimed at no one in particular

At the February 1950 conference of chiefs of mission in Bangkok, the participants recorded: "Generally agreed USIE materials designed for world-wide or even area-wide distribution not satisfactory." (USIE was the name of the information programme's own output.) (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 15 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d15)) The same document ties American prestige in Asia to showing the American way of life working in Japan, Korea and the Philippines. The report of Ambassador Jessup's 14-nation tour reads: "Ambassador Jessup considered that we are weak on propaganda. He raised the question of whom we are trying to reach and recommended that more thought be given to methods of reaching our audience and to the themes we are trying to put across." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32)) In March 1950 the Department's Far Eastern public affairs officer described a stepped-up programme for Southeast Asia, with a regional production centre planned at Manila; the one sign of effect he offered was more use of the material in the local press (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31)). By 1954 the programme appears as a thin inventory: an account of the Philippines lists four information centres with 13 American and 111 local staff, alongside figures for 122 newspapers and magazines and 18 radio stations, and it states: "During FY 1954 a reduction in the USIS film program by 50% will be necessary because of lack of funds." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d359)) A Psychological Strategy Board paper on Thailand treated overt information as one arm of a wider psychological strategy, to be run through local outlets (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403)).

### 2. Officials assumed an audience that follows the winner, respects resolve and reads generosity as softness

Thirty-two documents in the 79 volumes use some form of the *Oriental mind* or *Asian psychology* wording; 25 use one of the six exact phrases and the other seven use inflected forms such as *Southeast Asian minds*. By my reading, 17 apply the wording to how U.S. policy, aid or messaging would be received, and 15 are other uses, such as an Asian leader's own claim. The report of Jessup's tour records: "Both Pignon, the French High Commissioner for Indochina, and MacDonald, Commissioner for the United Kingdom in Southeast Asia, believe that the Asians are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32)) A circular from the Secretary of State, arguing for the defence of Formosa, put it this way: "it is in the pattern of the Oriental psychology to respect and follow aggressive, resolute and dynamic leadership—to quickly turn on a leadership characterized by timidity or vacillation" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 266 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d266)) A chargé in Saigon wanted aid on strict reciprocity and wrote: "In Asia give-away programs are too frequently mistaken in the oriental mind as meaning that the donor not only has a soft heart but also a soft head." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p2/d992)) Asian leaders turned the formula back on Americans: President Magsaysay told the ambassador that Americans did not understand the Asian mind about gifts to visitors (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 361 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d361)), and Diem was reported convinced that Americans, not being Asians, did not understand the Oriental mind (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Vietnam, Volume I, Document 26 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v01/d26)).

### 3. Colonial memory was treated as a constraint on any message

The audience was assumed to carry a memory of colonial rule, and that assumption shaped how a message was framed and even what the information agency would do. A consul general in Singapore warned: "Tendency in SEA to attribute some “pure nationalism” to Viet Minh aims will make more difficult characterization of invasion as “naked aggression.”" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 1, Document 103 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p1/d103)) The Far Eastern bureau recorded that the information agency had objected to expanding its Malaya programme out of "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism", and that State overrode the objection (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447)). The Deputy Under Secretary weighed a policy by its likely reception: "US support for a Dutch trusteeship would be widely interpreted as evidence of a US desire to maintain Western control over Asian peoples or, to Asian minds, of US “colonialism”." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 271 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d271))

### 4. What officials said drew Asian audiences, and what wore it down

Where they judged the United States admired, they named institutions, culture and single events. In 1963 Ambassador Reischauer judged an astronaut's visit to Japan "what was probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127)) The Tokyo embassy's 1968 stocktaking found: "popular respect for US political institutions, infatuation with many aspects of American mass culture, genuine respect for our intellectual attainments, and visible attractions of the American way of life, have kept America a strongly positive symbol." It then added: "Racial violence and social unrest in America have roused concern over basic stability of American society, made American image a rather less positive political symbol." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 123 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d123)) The agency's public affairs officers for East Asia, meeting at Manila in 1969, reported: "PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US." The same summary records: "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31)) Other documents locate the damage in the programme itself: an ambassador in Rangoon thought the United States too conspicuous in Burma (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60)), and in Indonesia the agency's director reported: "We shall soon have nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106))

### 5. Vietnam turned the information agency into a wartime agency, and its own survey measured falling trust

A 1967 agency report on its work in Vietnam, Thailand and Laos said of its tasks: "Some of these tasks appear to lie outside the Agency’s appropriate sphere of operations." It added: "These three country operations absorb nearly fifteen percent of the Agency’s resources available for overseas programming." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 147 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d147)) The agency's Saigon survey of May 1970 found: "Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys, with only 26 percent of the general public and 23 percent of the college-educated thinking of U.S. as “very dependable” or “rather dependable.”" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88)) In Washington, the agency's account of Senator Church's bill quoted his charge that the agency "is now engaged in a massive campaign, using every tool of the communication arts, to sell the Thieu Government to the people of Vietnam." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 129 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d129)) The agency also described its psychological operations support in Vietnam as short-term, with a plan to hand it to the Vietnamese government (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 58 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58)), and described an information vacuum in Cambodia after the 1970 coup (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 92 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d92)).

### 6. After Vietnam and the opening to China, reassurance became the message, and posts reported that it was not credited

In August 1969 the agency's guidance to its media read: "Peace in Asia—which is a precondition to peace in the world—cannot result primarily from American conceptions but has to involve Asian initiatives and regional and sub-regional arrangements that have an Asian origin." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 30 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d30)) Before the President's trip to Peking the agency proposed to turn a temporary psychological asset into a lasting one (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 146 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146)), while State objected to releasing a film that might send the wrong signal to Peking (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 143 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d143)). In May 1975, after the fall of South Vietnam, the agency's East Asia office abstracted its posts' reassessments. They include: "During this waiting period, programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations." "Thais still see us as friends and admire much in the American system." "The denouement in Indochina has left Hong Kong viewing USG as “very clearly in default.”" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105)) In 1977 the agency's East Asia chief wrote: "I don’t think there should be any serious doubt that this Administration believes shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area is real and necessary." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79)) The reply from Jakarta said: "In general, public diplomacy of the new administration in this area has been effective." It also said: "We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83))

## How this was done

I searched the full text of all 553 volumes, which the searchable copy holds, and read within 79: the 74 East Asia and neighbouring compilations found from their printed titles, and five public diplomacy volumes found by counting the agency's own vocabulary (information agency, information service, information program, psychological strategy, Voice of America, propaganda) volume by volume, then by scanning document headings. Because the series does not use *soft power*, I queried in the editors' vocabulary and tested each search against two controls in the same pass: a word that cannot occur (found 0) and *department of state* (found in 14,251 of 35,874 documents in the reading set). I compared the 79 volumes with 220 other postwar volumes. That showed that *prestige*, *image*, *propaganda* and *oriental* occur at about the same rate in both sets (for example *propaganda* in about 78 documents per thousand against about 70), so they measure the series and not this question, and I did not scope on them; the agency's acronym for its overseas posts (USIS) does discriminate, appearing in about one document in fifty against about one in 180. By decade, the agency's vocabulary appears in about one dated document in thirty-six in the 1950s and about one in twelve in the 1960s; the 1970s rate is nearly as high, about one in thirteen, but nearly half of it comes from one volume and the public diplomacy volumes are inside the set, so the rise is partly built in. I also used semantic search (which finds documents whose meaning resembles a document given to it; it finds roughly nine in ten of the true neighbours, so a missing neighbour is not evidence of absence) on four anchor documents. It led me to the later public diplomacy volumes and so to the May 1975 assessments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105)), which I reached through it and through no text search; the 1977 documents were reached by a heading scan that its results prompted. Every document was pulled whole and its length checked against the database; I read 23 of the 33 collection documents in full and ten in part (a passage window or a leading span), so my reading of those ten is partial. Each quotation above was checked word for word against its document's text. Two covert-rumour documents on Korea were left out as tactics, not soft power. The split of 17 and 15 is my own reading; the exact counts, rates and denominators are in the memo (see the memo).
