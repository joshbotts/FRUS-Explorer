# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly** — The phrase soft power does not occur in the edition, so I read the question through the officials' own terms: information programs, propaganda and psychological operations. On that vocabulary FRUS answers directly, in thirty-one documents from 1948 to 1977. Officials paired sympathy for Asian nationalism with exposure of Soviet imperialism, aimed at educated, influential people (1948, Volume VII, Document 567; 1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386). They believed audiences judged the United States by its actions more than its words (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 251). Early assessments called the effort effective but could not measure it (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). By 1964 the Director of the United States Information Agency (USIA) called information the weakest part of the war in Vietnam (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122). After 1969 its officers held that credibility followed policy (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). Every judgment of effect came from the program's own officers.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** The term does not occur, so I used the officials' own vocabulary of information programs, the Agency's overseas offices, the Voice of America and psychological operations. If you mean attraction through exchanges and libraries alone, the set shrinks to little, because the exchange documents I found only describe. If you mean every form of American influence, it grows to include aid and military documents I did not read for this question. My inclusion rule meanwhile: a document that states an intended message, channel or audience belief for East Asia or the Pacific, or assesses the effect of such messaging.
- **Where the region ends.** I took China, Japan, Korea, Indochina, Southeast Asia and Australasia; adding South Asia would bring India and Pakistan in as audiences, and mainland China as an audience is a thread I did not follow.
- **Who counts as a policymaker.** I included field officers and Agency staff as well as Washington principals; limiting the set to Presidents and Secretaries would remove most of the effectiveness assessments, since the Agency's officers wrote them.
- **Volumes whose titles do not name the region.** I included three public diplomacy volumes, which hold the Manila conference, the 1970 program memorandum and the Vietnam field reviews; leaving them out would thin the account of the late 1960s and early 1970s.

## Findings

### The message was a contrast with Soviet imperialism, aimed at leaders

The earliest documents describe the task as counter-propaganda for educated and influential people. In December 1948 the Department told Ambassador Stuart that the American information office's newsletter was its best way of reaching “the influential middle group of educated Chinese” (1948, Volume VII, Document 567). In November 1949 Ambassador at Large Jessup asked for a larger program in Communist and non-Communist Asia alike:

> A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for, both to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism.
> (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386)

Stuart had already asked in July 1949 to expand the information offices in all Southeast Asian countries (1949, Volume VIII, Document 499). Under Secretary Webb wanted information to travel with military and economic aid (1950, Volume VI, Document 50). By January 1956 an interdepartmental study of Thailand described the Agency's program in these words:

> The USIA program emphasizes comprehensive anti-Communist indoctrination throughout Thailand.
> (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 484)

The tools were broadcasts, libraries, films, newsletters and exchanges. A 1955 report on the foreign information program said the Agency also sought to orient Asian neutralists toward the United States (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190).

### Officials believed the audience trusted deeds over words and heard colonialism in the wrong theme

Officials described Asian audiences as sceptical of American words. A report on Indonesia described a public “which is more affected by our actions than our words and which is hostile to propaganda from any source, especially non-Asian” (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 251). In 1954 the Department rejected a theme for the Agency's use in the Philippines because it recalled the Japanese wartime slogan:

> widespread use in Philippines and elsewhere of slogan “Asia for Asians” would be most unfortunate.
> (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 352)

The fear of seeming to back colonialism also held the Agency back in Malaya. Assistant Secretary Robertson listed among the reasons “a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism” (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 447). Officials also wrote that weak media limited the message in Thailand (1958–1960, Volume XV, Document 548). In 1971 they found the overseas Chinese apolitical and neutral rather than ideological (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149). The vocabulary was the period's own. Acting Political Adviser Sebald reported that many Japanese saw ignorance of “the Oriental” in American methods (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 127). Counselor Mecklin wrote of “Orientals” and the Ngo Dinhs (1961–1963, Volume IV, Document 81). These are officials' beliefs, not findings about Asian peoples.

### Effect was asserted, then judged weak, always by the program's own officers

Among the dated documents in the 77 volumes, those naming the Agency, its overseas offices or the Voice of America were about one in seventy in the 1940s. They were one in forty-four in the 1950s, one in twenty-four in the 1960s and one in seventy-five in the 1970s. The 1940s figure is not a regional rate, because the 1949 China volume supplies close to half of those documents. In 1952 Ambassador Heath answered a request for an assessment of the information program in Indochina:

> All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured.
> (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116)

Money limited the effort. The Agency's services in the Philippines were cut by about two-thirds in fiscal 1954 (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359). By 1962 the Public Affairs Officer in Saigon was asking for a hard reading on how well Vietnamese government propaganda worked in the countryside (1961–1963, Volume II, Document 197). In April 1964 Director Rowan told the President:

> the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare.
> (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 122)

In February 1965 Rowan told President Johnson that efforts to build national unity among South Vietnamese had not progressed to his satisfaction (1964–1968, Volume II, Document 160). The same month he wrote of Indonesia:

> We shall soon have nothing worthwhile left in the way of an information operation.
> (1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Document 106)

### The host government had to be the messenger, and was the weak point

In Vietnam and Thailand the Americans wanted the local government to speak to its own people. In October 1964 an Agency officer reviewing the American information service in Vietnam put the premise this way:

> Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese.
> (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 32)

But the Vietnamese government's service was weak. In January 1962 the Agency's East Asia office told the Ambassador that

> the image of the GVN in the world press can be no better than the face the GVN presents to the public
> (1961–1963, Volume II, Document 16)

where GVN is the Government of Vietnam. In 1968 Agency Director Marks briefed President Johnson on Thailand:

> The Thai Government has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support.
> (1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Document 386)

Washington's response was to put American money and advisers inside the host service. In May 1961 the Agency's Far East office was cranking up a major propaganda campaign on Viet Cong insurgency (1961–1963, Volume I, Document 49). In May 1964 Rowan listed fourteen steps. They included more Agency staff for Vietnam, province advisers, a petty-cash fund and a large expansion of Voice of America broadcasts to the North (1964–1968, Volume I, Document 177). After the 1966 Buddhist crisis, an Agency officer wrote that it had surprised most Americans how much anti-Americanism lay under the surface in Vietnam (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 85).

### After Vietnam, credibility followed policy

From 1969 Agency officers said the audience judged the United States by its policy and its commitments, not by its programs. At the Manila conference of the Agency's public affairs officers for East Asia and the Pacific, the officers reported:

> PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US.
> (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31)

Many of the officers reported uncertainty about the direction of policy and the strength of commitments. In 1970 the Agency's first program memorandum concluded that under the new partnership the United States would have to speak more quietly (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 99). In 1971 Director Shakespeare told Kissinger that opinion surveys confirmed strains in Japan (1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Document 67). After the fall of Saigon the East Asia office abstracted the posts' view:

> During this waiting period, programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations.
> (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105)

In February 1977 the Consulate in Hong Kong judged that the public position of 1975 had failed to reassure the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) or Japan:

> Rather than being reassured, the ASEAN countries and Japan interpreted our apparent unwillingness to come to grips with the “new realities” as another indication that the United States, after twenty years of exaggerating the extent of its interests in Southeast Asia, might now decide it had no interest there at all.
> (1977–1980, Volume XXII, Document 104)

By July 1977 the Agency's East Asia office described the task as shoring up East Asians' confidence in America's staying power (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79).

## How this was done

I worked from the text of the printed documents as the library indexes it, with the editors' footnotes included in the body. I therefore make no claim that depends on whether a phrase sits in a footnote. Because the term soft power finds nothing, I counted the officials' vocabulary instead, by literal spellings of the Agency, its overseas offices and the Voice of America. I ran a positive and a negative control in the same pass. I chose the 77 East Asia and Pacific volumes from their printed titles. I found candidate documents by pattern searches for information agencies, prestige and image, audience terms and effect words, then read the sentences around them. A similarity search ranks documents by how close their meaning is to a chosen document. It showed that I had missed three public diplomacy volumes whose titles do not name the region, and I then searched those in the same way. I retrieved 95 documents whole, read passages from them, and quoted from 31. Each quotation is an exact string from its document. I counted each reprinted memorandum once, citing the regional volume's copy. The similarity search finds about nine in ten of a document's true nearest neighbors, so I make no claim that no similar document exists. Every effectiveness judgment is the program's own officer's, and I found no independent measure.

## Read next

The collection is “Soft power in East Asia and the Pacific: messaging and its limits, 1948-1980.” It is arranged by what officials did, what they believed about the audience, how effective they thought it was, the host government as messenger, and what changed after Vietnam. It closes with the threads I did not follow. Your decisions above would let me recut it, since a narrower or wider definition changes which documents stay. Documents you add from the Agency's research files, or from Japan, Korea and Australasia, would test whether the pattern holds across the region. It rests mostly on Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia plus the Agency's own East Asia office.
