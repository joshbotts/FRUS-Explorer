# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the East Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series never uses the phrase *soft power*. The officials who ran American information work in the region, chiefly the U.S. Information Agency (the Agency) and its posts, described the idea in other words. Their message was contrast with communism and proof of American strength, and after 1975 reassurance, not the appeal of American society. They took the audience to be nationalist, poor and wary of white foreigners, best persuaded “by other Vietnamese” (1964, Public Diplomacy Volume VII, Document 32). Ambassador Reischauer explained Japanese hostility to the bombing of North Vietnam partly by “ostrich-like pacifism” (1965, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). Officials counted output in the 1950s and doubted effect in the 1960s: “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories” (1964, Volume I, Document 197). This rests on about two dozen documents from 1949 to 1978, chosen from about 30,000 in the regional volumes.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The editors name an archive in the source note of a document they print. They name another in a footnote when they cite a file they did not print. I keep these channels apart and add nothing across them. The regional set below means the 1,176 documents in the regional volumes that name the Agency, its posts, the Voice of America or its information and psychological programs.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The term itself is absent. I searched *soft power* as an exact phrase, and as two words within three words of each other, in about 30,000 documents of the 69 regional volumes. I found none. The two words anywhere in one document turned up in about 130 documents, mostly *soft* beside an unrelated *power*, and I did not read them. *Cultural diplomacy* and *Radio Free Asia* were also absent, and *public diplomacy* appeared in two documents. As controls, *Department of State* was found in about 13,500 documents of the same volumes and a nonsense word in none, so the search works. The audience's own voice is also missing. Every view of the audience here is an official's report of it. In the documents I read, the measures of effect are counts of output, film attendance and a few polls, not controlled comparisons. The Agency's own research reports would hold more. The nearest series I could identify is the Office of Research and Intelligence, NAID 1254251, 1954–1958, 9 linear feet 2 inches, access partly restricted. I have not verified what it holds. Exchanges and private channels are thin. *Asia Foundation* was found in a dozen documents, *Peace Corps* in about 60, *cultural exchange* in about 90 and *educational exchange* in about 55, and I did not read them. *Fulbright* appears in about 280, mostly the Senator. I name no shelf for these. Korea after 1960, the Philippines, Indonesia, Australia and New Zealand, and the Voice of America's audience in China are also thin in what I read.

**Scope.** I searched the 69 volumes whose titles name the region, dated 1948 to 1980 and holding about 30,000 documents. I also searched seven volumes of public-diplomacy and information records, holding about 1,600 documents, and took from them only documents whose headings name an Asian or Pacific place or office. I left out volumes on South Asia and the Near East, the China volumes for 1942 to 1947, and every volume's front matter and editorial notes.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** The term does not appear, so I read it as American government work to inform and persuade foreign publics in the region. If you also want aid, exchanges, the Peace Corps or military presence, the set widens far beyond what I read. If you want only talk of attraction or prestige, very little remains.
- **Whose assessment counts.** I included any official of the State Department, the Agency, the National Security Council or an embassy who described the message, the audience or its effect. If you mean only the most senior policymakers, much of the evidence of doubt drops out, because it comes from the Agency's directors and field posts.
- **Where the region ends.** I took the region from volume titles and left out South Asia and the Near East. Including them adds volumes I did not search.
- **Where the period starts.** I began in 1948. An earlier start would bring in the wartime and immediate postwar volumes on China, which I excluded.
- **Enemy propaganda.** I dropped the word *propaganda* from my main search, because in the Korea and Vietnam volumes it mostly describes the enemy's. American propaganda that never uses the words *information program* may therefore be missed.

Meanwhile my inclusion rule was this. A document counts when an American official, writing about American information, cultural or psychological work, describes the message, the audience or its effect. I read each one to confirm that the phrase referred to American government work.

## Findings

### The message was contrast, strength and reassurance, not attraction

Officials built the message from the audience's fears and loyalties, not from the appeal of American society. In 1949 Ambassador at Large Jessup asked for an expanded program in non-Communist Asia. It was to show sympathy with nationalism and to expose Soviet imperialism. He wrote that “A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for” (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386). In August 1955 the Agency's acting director told all posts what to expect after the Geneva summit:

> We can anticipate, too, an upsurge of neutralism. Our information program must energetically combat these tendencies. (1955, Volume IX, Document 189)

In July 1963 Agency guidance on a Chinese nuclear test told posts to stress American and Free World strength. It called for “avoiding a preoccupation with the negative side”, meaning Chinese weakness. The reason was frank: “our credibility on this negative aspect is relatively low” (1963, Public Diplomacy Volume VI, Document 135).

### Officials believed the audience was nationalist, poor, wary of whites and best persuaded by its own people

The joint State and Defense mission that surveyed Southeast Asia in December 1950 judged American information work badly pitched: “At present we are talking over the heads of Southeast Asia, and doing so inadequately” (1950, Volume VI, Document 89). The same report held that white troops would always be associated with colonialism. A 1953 plan for Thailand said that American leadership should be projected with and through local leaders (1953, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). The belief also shaped policy. A 1960 National Security Council paper told officials to keep the number of Americans in each country to a minimum, to head off a reaction to Americans in “relatively privileged positions” (1960, Volume XVI, Document 65). In 1964 a reviewer of the Agency's post in Vietnam wrote that “Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese” (1964, Public Diplomacy Volume VII, Document 32). He judged the civil population “family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times”. These are officials' views of the audience. The audience speaks only through their reports.

### Their own judgments of effect moved from counting output to doubting that messages could outrun events

In the 1950s, National Security Council reports on the foreign information program counted activity. The August 1955 report conceded that “the national press remained generally unreceptive to explanatory material on U.S. policy and Communism”. It also recorded that “a remarkable placement of material on the peaceful uses of atomic energy was achieved” (1955, Volume IX, Document 190). The same series' report of September 1957 hedged on Japan, with a generally favourable public attitude beside rising dissatisfaction (1957, Volume IX, Document 207). By June 1964 the Agency's director told the President that “there is no conceivable information program that will make defeats look like victories, or GVN lassitude appear to be fiery enthusiasm” (1964, Volume I, Document 197). As a measure he offered that Vietnamese film audiences had totalled an estimated 28 million. In January 1966 a summary of the Far East posts said their programs were aimed at elites. It added that budgets made mass efforts impossible (1966, Public Diplomacy Volume VII, Document 73). In August 1966 Director Marks listed the images that hurt American standing over Vietnam. One was “The image of a huge white nation attempting to suppress an Asian struggle for freedom” (1966, Public Diplomacy Volume VII, Document 100). A May 1970 survey of 637 adults in Saigon found that only 26 percent rated the United States a dependable ally. That was the lowest in thirteen surveys (1970, Public Diplomacy Volume VIII, Document 88).

### Officials concluded that the image followed policy and America at home

In May 1965 Ambassador Reischauer sent the only poll then available in Japan. Support for aligning Japan with the free world had fallen from 49 to 40 percent since January. Support for neutralism had risen from 22 to 32 percent. He judged that “the basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run” (1965, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). In June 1968 the embassy in Tokyo reported that “Racial violence and social unrest in America have roused concern over basic stability of American society” (1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 123). The public affairs officers of the region met in Manila in May 1969. Their summary held that “Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers.” It added that the image of the United States “usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations” than on events at home (1969, Public Diplomacy Volume VIII, Document 31). In November 1971 a reviewer judged that the emotional pull of China, though not necessarily the ideological one, had directly affected overseas Chinese communities (1971, Public Diplomacy Volume VIII, Document 149).

### After Vietnam the message became reassurance, and the audience was reported to doubt it

In July 1974 a National Security Council staff member told Kissinger that Asian countries were adjusting well to the Nixon Doctrine (1974, Volume E–12, Document 10). A compilation of post-by-post abstracts of public opinion was made in May 1975, after the fall of South Vietnam. It reported that “The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese since the US withdrawal two years ago.” It added that Hong Kong viewed the United States government as “very clearly in default” (1975, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). In February 1977 a telegram from the Hong Kong consulate said that Japan, and the countries of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, read Washington's reluctance to reassess as a warning. The United States, they feared, might decide it had no interest in Southeast Asia (1977, Volume XXII, Document 104). In July 1977 Smith told Bray that the Administration believed in “shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power” (1977, Public Diplomacy Volume XXX, Document 79). Embassy Jakarta replied in August 1977 that public diplomacy was effective. It said the post still faced a “deeply implanted and persistent feeling” that the United States was withdrawing (1977, Public Diplomacy Volume XXX, Document 83). One document among these opens the audience instead of reassuring it. It is Beijing's report of October 1978 on exchanges and other programs of the International Communication Agency in China (1978, Public Diplomacy Volume XXX, Document 152).

## How this was done

I defined the region from the titles of 69 volumes dated 1948 to 1980. I added seven volumes that gather the government's public-diplomacy and information records, and kept from them only documents whose headings name an Asian or Pacific place or office. In the regional volumes I searched for the names of the Agency and its programs. I then ran ranked searches for the vocabulary of audience belief, effectiveness, neutralism and prestige, and read candidates from each. I did not read the editors' chapter headings before writing the searches.

The search treats word endings loosely, so I checked each phrase against the documents' exact text. The phrase *information program* was present, with spacing and punctuation allowed to vary, in 134 of 135 documents found. *Psychological warfare* was present in all 252 and *psychological strategy* in all 15. A match proves only that the words are there, so I read every quoted document to confirm that the phrase referred to American government information work.

Documents naming the Agency, its posts, the Voice of America or its information and psychological programs were about 33 in every thousand in the regional volumes in the 1950s. They were about 53 in the 1960s and about 19 in the 1970s. Single volumes carry much of each figure. These words do not mark the region by themselves. *Audience* was rarer in the regional volumes (about 8 in every thousand documents) than in the 201 other volumes of the same years (about 12). The Agency's acronyms were only about one and a half times as common there (about 26 against 18 in every thousand). The argument therefore rests on documents read, not on counts.

I retrieved about fifty documents whole from about 1,200 candidates and read the relevant passages of each. I also asked the volumes' index of meaning for the documents closest in content to four anchor documents. It finds about nine in ten of the true nearest documents, so its silence proves nothing. Two of the documents I cite were reached only that way (1974, Volume E–12, Document 10; 1977, Volume XXII, Document 104). Every count rests on the search index of the volumes' text, and I ran no separate spelling-variant count beyond the phrase checks above. I checked that every quotation appears word for word in its document and that every document cited is in the collection.
