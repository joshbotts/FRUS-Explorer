# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The officials in these volumes never speak of "soft power"; they write of the information program, psychological strategy and the image of the United States. In that vocabulary, about 1,400 documents in the region's volumes and the information volumes discuss the effort. Their verdict is consistent: the program reached educated elites but rarely moved opinion against what American policy itself was doing. A 1949 paper called for an expanded program to align the United States with Asian nationalism (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386). A later report on the program conceded that "the trend toward accommodation to Communist pressures in the area continued" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 207). In 1965 Reischauer judged that in Japan "the basic emotional response remains unchanged" (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46). In 1969 the posts' public affairs officers found that the American image "depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations" than on events at home (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** More than half of the 1,400 documents came from the State Department's records, and about one in eight from the Information Agency's own.

It holds the annual reports on the information program, and the editors also cite it unprinted.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The term itself is absent: I searched for the phrase "soft power" across all 553 volumes and found nothing. In the same pass, "Department of State" was found in every volume of the scope, and an impossible test word was found nowhere, so the search was working. Measured effectiveness is largely missing: the Information Agency's country plans and opinion surveys are not among the documents I found, the 1969 Manila conference report is printed only as a summary, and the 1975 post cables only as abstracts. I established this by reading, not by a separate search; the shelf is the Information Agency's records, Record Group 306, which I could trace only to the accessions split across many series. The full round of 1952 embassy replies on the program's effectiveness is missing too. I searched for the phrase "circular telegram 208", the Department's circular asking posts for those replies and found one printed reply, Saigon's (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). The rest are in the State Department's 1950–1954 decimal file, Record Group 59, class 511.

**Scope.** I read the postwar volumes on East Asia, the Pacific, China, Japan, Korea, Indochina and Southeast Asia through the 1977–1980 series, plus the public-diplomacy and information volumes, where a document counted only if it named an Asian place: 81 volumes holding about 35,000 documents. I excluded editorial notes and front matter, the wartime Far East volumes and the 1981–1988 volumes, and I removed documents in which "USI" meant the United States of Indonesia rather than the information service.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** If it means the information and exchange programs, the set is about 1,400 documents; if it also takes in aid, prestige and military reputation, the set grows and loses its edges, and my check of whether the search for "prestige" found the word itself was not valid, so I did not use it. Meanwhile I counted only documents that name the information program, the Information Agency or Service, psychological strategy or warfare, or cultural exchange.
- **Whose beliefs about the audience count.** If only American officials', the South Vietnamese government's claims that Americans could not understand Asians drop out; if any voice the documents record, they stay. Meanwhile I kept them and marked them as reported views.
- **Which years.** Ending in 1980 leaves out the 1981–1988 volumes, which also use this vocabulary; adding them would carry the story past 1980. Meanwhile I stopped at the 1977–1980 volumes.

## Findings

### The program as an alignment with Asian nationalism, judged uphill from the start

Officials framed the early program as identifying the United States with Asian nationalism against communism.

> "A considerably expanded information program both in Communist and non-Communist areas of Asia is called for, both to demonstrate our understanding and sympathy for the nationalist aspirations of the Asian peoples and to expose the menace to these aspirations created by Soviet imperialism." (1949, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386)

Planners insisted on country-by-country treatment: "each nation is an entity basically different from the others and requires special treatment" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). Saigon in 1952 counted film audiences, library visits and English-class waiting lists, and read them as success: "Such demand can only be construed as intense interest in US by educated groups" (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116). The same reply conceded that the rural majority was not a target. The agency's status reports to the National Security Council were blunter: "In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples" (1955–1957, Volume IX, Document 190), USIA being the United States Information Agency.

### Beliefs about the audience: "the Oriental mind"

Officials explained Asian reactions through a stock idea of "the Oriental mind". The phrase is concentrated in the region: 14 of the 25 documents that use it in the whole series fall in the scope, which holds only about one document in nine. From it officials drew rules for presentation. Aid given freely, one warned, looked like weakness:

> "In Asia give-away programs are too frequently mistaken in the oriental mind as meaning that the donor not only has a soft heart but also a soft head." (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992)

Another writer urged that "we must by word and deed do everything within our power to overcome any feeling in the Oriental mind that our interest in Asia is casual, temporary or overshadowed by our interests in other regions" (1952–1954, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 551). Another judged that the "Bulk of Burmese people simply unable understand aid in terms of altruism" (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60). By the late 1950s Diem's government turned the idea back on the Americans. Durbrow reported Diem's conviction: "Not being Asians, Americans do not understand the Oriental mind and point of view" (1958–1960, Volume I, Document 26).

### Vietnam: information as a weapon and a regional liability

In Vietnam the program became an instrument of the war. One official feared the cost to the American image across Asia: "But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me" (1961–1963, Volume I, Document 265). A paper from the Saigon embassy's files asked, "Is it possible, for example, to laugh at the VC, to make them look ridiculous and thus lose face?" (1961–1963, Volume II, Document 197). A later report asked for parity with the fighting:

> "On the American side, these programs to affect what the Vietnamese people think must be given the same status, the same concern, the same adequacy of working resources, as our military and economic operations." (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 37)

### Japan and the elites: persuasion bounded by policy

The program aimed at elites and judged itself by their response. The Operations Coordinating Board's 1954 report on Japan claimed that "Vigorous information programs have been carried on to give the Japanese a better understanding of the United States and of world problems and to combat leftist and neutralist influences" (1952–1954, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 820). The same report admitted "a serious increase in neutralist sentiment, periodic flare-ups of anti-Americanism and an intensification of communist overtures to Japan." A 1963 report on John Glenn's visit to Japan judged that Glenn and his family "were effective demonstration of typical American virtues" (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127). In 1965 Reischauer wrote:

> "Intellectually we have met with some success, and there is a growing awareness of the U.S. point of view, which has perhaps blunted the attack somewhat, but the basic emotional response remains unchanged and is probably unchangeable over the short run." (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 46)

Across the area, "our programs are directed at elite groups: government officials, university and high school teachers, media representatives, top-level business executives" (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 73). The 1969 Manila conference held that "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", and advised: "One caveat: US visibility should be low" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31).

### Credibility after the opening to China and the fall of Saigon

In the 1970s the agency recast its aims around credibility. It saw the President's China trip as a chance "to build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 146). It turned to "the overseas Chinese as a USIS audience" (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149), that is, an audience of the Information Service's posts. After 1975 one post in Singapore proposed programming on "the benefits accruing from the end of our involvement" (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). Kuala Lumpur reported that "Confidence in US economic power, technological and scientific capability, and our leadership role in seeking solutions for world economic and social problems remains." By the late 1970s officials treated Japan as a partner in communication among the industrial democracies, on hard questions: "We are dealing here with gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 2).

## How this was done

I searched the full text of the postwar regional volumes and the information volumes for the period's own terms: the information program, the Information Agency and Service, psychological strategy and warfare, and cultural exchange. The search ignores the difference between singular and plural, so I checked by pattern-matching that each document actually contains one of those terms. That check exposed a false match: "USI" often meant the United States of Indonesia, and I removed the 172 documents that failed it, leaving about 1,400. Two controls ran in the same pass: a common phrase found in every scope volume and an impossible word found nowhere. Among dated documents in the regional volumes, the vocabulary appears in about one in sixty in the 1940s, one in thirty in the 1950s, one in twenty in the 1960s and one in thirty in the 1970s; these rates include the editors' footnotes. I read 121 documents whole and quoted from 23, and I checked every quotation word for word against its document. One document, Saigon's 1952 reply, I first met as a close textual neighbour of the 1969 Manila summary, measured by a comparison of whole documents; it was also in the full-text set, and what I say of it rests on reading it.

## Read next

The collection "Information programs and the American image in East Asia, 1949–1977" holds every document cited here, with the quoted passages marked, arranged in the same order as the findings. Its weakest parts are Korea, the Philippines, Indonesia, Australia and New Zealand, which I reached only through regional reports. Adding documents on those countries, or the Information Agency's opinion surveys from the National Archives, would let the collection test whether the elite model and the "Oriental mind" beliefs held beyond Japan and Indochina.
