# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** About thirty documents, drawn from roughly thirteen hundred that name the United States Information Agency or its overseas posts (called USIS, the United States Information Service) among about 58,000 documents in 105 of the series' 553 indexed volumes, show how American officials assessed their own information, cultural and psychological programs in East Asia and the Pacific between 1931 and 1977. The officials believed that Asian audiences waited to see who was winning, read American action through race and colonialism, and needed reassurance about American staying power. Their strategies were to aim at elites, to rely on personal contact, to keep American visibility low and, late in the period, to adopt Asian-led regional themes. They judged their programs effective mostly on faith and by counting activity: in 1952 the Saigon embassy wrote that all the factors it listed “must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116)). Where the Agency did measure opinion, the results were unwelcome: a 1965 Japanese poll quoted by the American ambassador, the Agency's study of one Vietnamese province in 1964, and its surveys of six countries in 1969 and of eight in 1970 found apathy, small effects and falling confidence (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume II, Vietnam, January–June 1965, Document 172 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v02/d172); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 34 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d34); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 94 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d94)). The Agency's Far East office summarised in 1966 that its programs were directed at elites and that personal contact was its best medium (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73)). This is a guided reading of thirty documents, not an estimate for the whole series; the evidence on effectiveness is the officials' own assessments plus those few surveys, and there is no outcome measure.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Each of these is a search or lookup that was run.

- *The phrase "soft power".* It appears in none of the 321,749 documents of the 553 volumes. The search machinery was checked on the same pass: a phrase every volume should contain, "Department of State", was found in 552 of the 553 volumes (94,049 documents; the one volume without it is outside the scope), and a nonsense string was found in none. That control proves the search works, not that the idea is absent, so I searched the officials' own terms instead (Agency and post names, "information program", "psychological", "prestige", "image").
- *Whether Asian audiences actually held the views the officials ascribed to them.* Nothing in the corpus is the audience's own voice; I searched English text only and did not look for foreign-language enclosures. The only audience evidence is the Agency's own surveys and one Japanese poll, selected by the editors. No shelf for the audience's side is established by the tools used; Chinese-language and Japanese-language sources, which a reader of those languages could consult, are the missing half.
- *The Far East area office of the Agency.* The tools resolve no catalogued series for it. As a control, the same tools do resolve State Department lots (63 D 351, 69 D 260, 93 D 82 and 53 D 413), so the method works for lots of this kind and the gap is in what the tools know of the Agency's records. Of the 14 identifiers in the series list whose creator is an Agency or International Information Administration office, none is for the Far East or East Asia and Pacific area office; the Record Group 306 entry numbers named above are where to ask the National Archives.
- *The period before 1940.* The phrase "Boxer indemnity" turns up in about a hundred documents of the East Asia volumes, mostly from the 1930s, and the collection takes three documents from this period. The tools' archival indexes reach 11 of 695 series before the war, so the shelf that holds those papers cannot be named from here.
- *Korea, the Philippines and Australia*, which appear only in passing, and the question of whether the officials' beliefs about the audience were *accurate*, which no document in the series can settle.

**Scope.** I searched 105 volumes: 99 whose titles name East Asia or the Pacific (they run from 1901 to 1980) and six on public diplomacy or the foreign information program, about 58,000 documents once the volumes' introductions and editors' notes are set aside. I left out volumes on South Asia, the Near East and Europe, and I did not count economic aid or military presence as instruments of persuasion.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** The question uses a later word, and no document does. I took a document as in scope if it (a) assesses the effect, reception or audience of an information, cultural, educational or psychological program aimed at East Asia or the Pacific, (b) states a belief about the regional audience that shaped such a program, or (c) records the program's end. If you also count economic aid and military presence, the set grows far beyond thirty documents and the argument becomes one about influence in general; if you keep to information work only, the 1931 document on schooling drops out.
- **Where the region ends.** I followed the volume titles, so India and the rest of South Asia are out, and Korea, the Philippines and Australia are thinly covered. If you want South Asia in, note that the collection holds one such document, a Vice President's summary of ambassadors' replies that includes how the war looked from India, on which the overview does not rely.
- **What counts as "effective".** I treated an official's judgment and a survey result as two kinds of evidence and kept them apart. If you accept only measured opinion, the answer reduces to three Agency surveys and one poll; if you accept officials' judgments, it widens, but then the claim that programs worked is the officials' own.
- **Where the period starts.** I started in 1931 to catch the Boxer indemnity and the closing of the Information Service in China. If you start when the Agency was founded in 1953, the earliest documents, those from before it existed, fall away, and the 1940s material is only the State Department's earlier posts.

## Findings

### 1. Before an information agency, officials spoke of schools and services, and the evidence is thin (1931-1949)

The three documents from the period before the Agency show how Americans described cultural and educational work in China, not how it was received. In 1931 a Chinese education official cited the Boxer indemnity remission as an American example, and the American consul general said the subject would get “a subject to which special attention would be given”. In 1948 the embassy preferred the Information Service to Fulbright funds as the instrument against anti-American feeling in Chinese universities. When the Service was closed in China in July 1949, the Shanghai consulate protested that the loss meant “a corresponding impoverishment of the cultural and technical resources available to the people of China”. The pre-war set is thin: three documents from a phrase that appears in about a hundred.

> “Mr. Peck referred to the interest taken by the American Government in educational affairs in China, and said that this was a subject to which special attention would be given.”
>
> Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, The Far East, Volume III, Document 939 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v03/d939)

> “Embassy believes USIS (United States Information Service) is better instrument for this purpose and in any event does not see how the Fulbright money could be used unless an outright grant were made to USIS.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 55 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v07/d55)

### 2. 1950-1955: a waiting audience, and success counted as activity

The beliefs the later programs rested on are already stated here. In 1950 Ambassador Jessup reported that the French High Commissioner for Indochina and the British Commissioner for Southeast Asia believed that “the Asians are inclined by their psychology to wait and see who is winning” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d32)); that is the French and British officials' belief, repeated in an American report. A 1953 plan for Thailand treated information as one strand of a political-psychological program that would use the ethnic ties of the Thai peoples across the region (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403)). On effectiveness the Saigon embassy's 1952 answer to a Senate inquiry reasoned that since new customs could be traced to American films it “must be assumed” that films were effective, and it reported film audiences of 75,000 to 100,000 a week and library attendance of about 200,000 a year, counts of activity rather than of changed opinion. In 1955 the Agency's own appraisal for the National Security Council said it had to deal with fears about atomic weapons and with communist propaganda “designed to stir up race hatred”; its strategy was to “orient Asian neutralists toward the U.S.”.

> “All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116)

> “In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190)

### 3. In Vietnam the message became a war program, and the Agency's own research said the message was not the problem (1964-1967)

From 1964 the Agency took on the Saigon government's information effort and the American psychological-warfare effort. Its Director told the President that the weakest part of the war was information and psychological warfare (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122)); in February 1965 he wrote that efforts to build national unity had not progressed to his satisfaction (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume II, Vietnam, January–June 1965, Document 160 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v02/d160)). A field review of 1964 put the problem in the Vietnamese themselves, describing a civil population “family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times”; that is the reviewer's belief, not evidence about Vietnamese. The Agency's own survey in Long An province told a different story, in which insecurity and ineffective government, not the message, kept the population apathetic. By 1967 an internal review argued that some tasks lay beyond the Agency's proper role, noting that in Laos the Service was in effect the Royal Lao Government's ministry of information, and that three country operations took nearly fifteen percent of overseas programming resources (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 147 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d147)).

> “I came to the conclusion that the weakest part of the war operation, both on our part and that of the Government of South Viet-Nam, is in the field of information and psychological warfare.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume I, Vietnam, 1964, Document 122 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v01/d122)

> “The number one problem in Vietnam is motivation.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32)

> “The USIA researchers conclude that the population is largely apathetic and is primarily interested in ending the twenty years of war; they care less as to which side will win, although there appears to be a substantial degree of approval of the Viet Cong.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume II, Vietnam, January–June 1965, Document 172 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v02/d172)

### 4. The audience answered back: race, imperialism and the limits of persuasion (1963-1970)

Officials expected Asian audiences to read American action through race, colonialism and fear of involvement, and a smaller set of documents records what happened. A 1963 memorandum accepted that smaller friendly countries felt taken for granted (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 107 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d107)). In May 1963 the Agency proposed a campaign to “set the record straight on “imperialism” and “neo-colonialism”” against Indonesian-led Afro-Asian propaganda (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 126 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126)); State replied in June that it was questionable whether the United States should publicize its own démarches to Indonesia, because the aid program might be put at risk (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 133 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d133)). In 1964 and 1965 the Agency's libraries in Indonesia were seized, its Director described the situation as “intolerable”, and the Jakarta embassy concluded that the Indonesian government did not want the libraries (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106); Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 109 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d109)). The Agency's Director, Marks, forwarded a newspaper publisher's proposal to show Asians that the United States was not waging a racist war (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 72 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d72)). The measured results were poor: the Japanese poll of 1965 and the Agency's survey after the Cambodia incursion of 1970.

> “Similarly, the number of persons naming the United States as one of their three favorite countries has dropped from 52 percent to 38 percent since last December and those naming the United States as a country they dislike has risen from 4 percent to 8 percent.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Japan, Document 46 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v29p2/d46)

> “The change in confidence in U.S. ability to deal wisely with world problems, from before to after U.S. entry into Cambodia, is the most dramatic trend in the present study.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 94 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d94)

> “Such a note could serve as the opening gun in a campaign to set the record straight on “imperialism” and “neo-colonialism”, two terms which have been overworked in Indonesia recently.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 126 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d126)

### 5. What the field officers said worked: elites, personal contact and the bilateral relationship (1963-1969)

The Agency's Far East office summarised its Public Affairs Officers' replies in 1966: programs were directed at elite groups, and “Personal contact is accepted as our most effective medium”, yet the nature of the programs meant that too little of it was done, and budgets ruled out mass efforts (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73)). At the 1969 Manila conference of the officers for East Asia and the Pacific, they emphasised that the American image depended more on “the state of bi-lateral relations” than on news from within the United States (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31)), and that low visibility mattered. Achievements were thought to move opinion more than messages, but even that was uneven: an astronaut's 1963 visit to Japan was reported by the ambassador as a success (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127)), while the 1969 Apollo survey found the moon landing had “a barely significant effect in Britain, Japan, or the Philippines”. The practitioners' view of what worked rests on their own judgment, which the survey evidence did not always bear out.

> “Personal contact is accepted as our most effective medium, yet the nature of our programs is such that we do too little of it.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73)

> “PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31)

> “The moon landing improved considerably general opinion of the United States in France, but had a barely significant effect in Britain, Japan, or the Philippines.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 34 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d34)

### 6. After Vietnam: Asian initiatives and reassurance (1969-1977)

In August 1969 guidance to the Agency's media treated the Nixon doctrine as a theme and stressed Asian initiatives (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 30 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d30)). A 1971 Agency assessment of overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia described a group with loyalties of its own, in a mood of neutrality rather than ideology (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149)). By 1977 the task had become reassurance. An Agency memorandum of July said the Administration believed that shoring up East Asian confidence in American staying power was “real and necessary” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79)); the State Department asked its posts for views on the future role of public diplomacy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 81 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d81)); and the embassy in Jakarta answered that Indonesians persistently believed the United States was withdrawing and took the new human-rights message as accusatory (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83)).

> “Peace in Asia—which is a precondition to peace in the world—cannot result primarily from American conceptions but has to involve Asian initiatives and regional and sub-regional arrangements that have an Asian origin.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 30 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d30)

> “We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia.”
>
> Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83 (https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83)

## How this was done

I worked from a complete copy of the series' index (553 volumes, checked as complete before anything else was counted) and chose 105 volumes by their printed titles: 99 on East Asia and the Pacific and six on public diplomacy and the foreign information program, which hold about 1,400 documents of which about two in five mention an East Asian or Pacific place. The index is searched by stems, meaning it treats words with the same root as the same word, so a search for the Agency's abbreviation also returned documents about the United States of Indonesia; I therefore re-read all 1,538 candidate hits against the printed words and kept 1,329. Because the Agency's names are far commoner in these volumes than in later volumes outside the scope (about two and a half times the rate), the count measures the topic, but the volumes on public diplomacy are rich in the Agency by construction, and the 1970s figure is half one volume. Chapter headings of the information volumes are years, not themes, so I searched by the Agency's and offices' names and then by words for the region and the audience. I retrieved 62 documents whole, of which thirty went into the collection; I read 19 of the thirty in full and 11 in part. Three of the thirty were reached only by asking the app for documents whose meaning is close to another's (a similarity measure over the whole text) and then reading them. The documents are those the editors chose to print, and the selection rule was not tested. See the memo for the exact counts, rates and denominators. Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this overview is a word-for-word extract from a passage the memo verified against its document, and that every document cited here is in the collection.
