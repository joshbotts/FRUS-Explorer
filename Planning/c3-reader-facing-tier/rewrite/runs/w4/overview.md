# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** From the late 1940s to 1980, the officials whose papers FRUS prints judged America's standing in East Asia less by American culture than by whether Asians believed American commitments would last: Jessup's 1949 outline puts the basic attitude of Asian peoples above the political tide (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386), the information officers who met at Manila in 1969 tied the American image to bilateral relations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31), and the posts reporting after the fall of Indochina in 1975 read credibility as the issue (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). The programmes were aimed chiefly at elites (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73), with the villages argued for in Vietnam (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). Success was judged mostly by outputs — press reprints, column inches, a successful visit — and rarely by measurement; the one printed opinion survey in the set, from Saigon, recorded confidence in the United States at its lowest in thirteen surveys (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). And the messages rested on stated beliefs about a race-conscious, nationalist audience, sometimes called the "oriental mind" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60). The answer rests on 25 documents, chosen from about 2,000 documents in 84 volumes that use the information programme's vocabulary.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Four parts of the question are not in the printed series, each established by a search of the 84 volumes in scope, run with two controls in the same pass — the phrase *Department of State*, which was found in all 84 volumes, and a nonsense string, which was found in none. (a) The term itself: *soft power* occurs in no document; the plain word *soft* occurs in about 350, in other senses (being "soft" on Communism). The question has to be put in the contemporaries' words, which is what this run did. (b) USIA's own audience research: about 160 documents in 36 volumes mention a poll or survey together with the information agency (about 40 of them also mention Japan), but FRUS prints the agency's summaries, not the surveys; the bundled archival data name no USIA series for an East Asian area office, so no shelf for the surveys was found. (c) The Pacific islands: only 6 documents in 5 volumes mention the information agency with Micronesia, Fiji, the Trust Territory, Samoa or Guam — too few to say anything. (d) The measured results of exchanges: about 380 documents in 59 volumes mention Fulbright, about 230 of them with words of evaluation or effect, but none was read, so this overview makes no claim about them. Australia and New Zealand as audiences (about 70 documents) were likewise found and not read.

**Scope.** The run searched 84 volumes — 76 regional East Asia and Pacific volumes covering 1945–1980 and the eight volumes on the information programme and public diplomacy — holding about 39,000 documents, with front matter and editorial notes excluded throughout. Of those, about 2,000 use the information programme's vocabulary, 141 name an information agency or function in their headings, 61 were retrieved and read, and 25 are kept in the collection; the wartime cultural relations programme with China (1942–44) falls outside the period and was excluded.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as *soft power*.** If it means the information and cultural programmes only, this set is close to complete for the printed record; if it also takes in aid, military civic action and exchanges as instruments of attraction, the reading set must grow by hundreds of documents (the Fulbright documents alone number about 380). Meanwhile the run included a document only when it named an information agency, a psychological or propaganda function, public affairs or the coordinating boards, or reached the same matter by a ranked search or a similarity search.
- **Which region.** Counting Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific islands as audiences adds about 70 and 6 documents respectively, not yet read; leaving them out keeps the answer to East and Southeast Asia, which is what the 25 documents cover. Meanwhile the run used the regional volumes' own coverage, including the 1958–60 volume titled *South and Southeast Asia*.
- **Whose assessment.** Restricting *policymakers* to Washington would drop the field reports — from Saigon, Burma, Taiwan and the Manila conference — on which most of the effectiveness evidence rests; keeping them makes the answer a field officers' answer as much as a cabinet's. Meanwhile the run counted any American official, and noted the few passages where an Asian speaker uses the stereotyped phrases.
- **What *effective* means.** If it means what officials claimed, FRUS answers it; if it means what audiences actually thought, FRUS gives one survey series and the rest lies in the agency's research files. Meanwhile the run reports officials' own judgements and says on what evidence each rested.

## Findings

### 1. The image followed the credibility of American commitments

Across three decades the documents locate America's standing less in its culture or its domestic life than in the reliability of its policy. Jessup's 1949 outline set the attitude of Asian peoples above the swings of politics; the 1969 Manila conference of public affairs officers found the image depended on treaties, bases and trade more than on events at home, though failures at home still cost prestige; after Indochina in 1975 the posts read doubt about American intentions; and the administration of 1977–80 aimed its public diplomacy at restoring confidence in American staying power.

> More important than any temporary ebb or flow in the political tide is the basic attitude of the peoples of Asia towards the United States.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The Far East and Australasia, Volume VII, Part 2, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v07p2/d386

> PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31

> We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83

The same thread runs through the 1975 post reports (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105) and USIA's restatement of that aim (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 79, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79).

### 2. Elites first; the villages argued over

The standing strategy was to work through elites and local leaders. The embassy at Saigon in 1952 conceded the rural majority, who were "not a primary target group," to the other side's saturation propaganda; a paper on psychological strategy from 1952–54 would work "with and through indigenous leadership"; and a mid-1960s summary of the area's programmes found elite targeting almost everywhere, against which a paper in the USIA Director's files argued that the war in Vietnam would be decided in the hamlets.

> Yet they are primary target for VM propaganda which follows Soviet line of giving masses saturation treatment.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116

> In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups: government officials, university and high school teachers, media representatives, top-level business executives.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73

> Granted, there is undeniable value in programs aimed at intellectuals, urban leaders and students, but I believe the war is going to be won or lost out in the hamlets, where the VC are.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32

The psychological strategy paper is Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403, which also calls for "suitable information programs for the general public, taking into account local cultural attitudes"; the National Security Council's mid-1950s status reports on the information programme carry the same approach through the region (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190).

### 3. Effectiveness was asserted from outputs, and seldom measured

When officials said a programme worked, their evidence was what went out and was taken up: local press using American material in 1950, Japanese newspapers reprinting about 8,000 column inches a month in the mid-1950s, John Glenn's visit to Japan. Where they doubted, as of Indonesia, they named the odds rather than a measure. Saigon in 1952 said outright that the degree of effect could not be measured. The one survey the set prints, from Saigon in 1970, found 26 per cent of 637 people interviewed rating the United States very or rather dependable — the agency's own figure, not a measurement of this run.

> Major Japanese newspapers continued to reprint from a translated wireless bulletin prepared by USIA in Japan an average of 8,000 column inches a month.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 185, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d185

> All factors recited must be viewed as effective though degree cannot be accurately measured.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116

> Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88

The other documents behind this finding are the 1950 programme for Southeast Asia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31), the later status report that judges Indonesia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 207, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d207) and the report of Glenn's visit (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127).

### 4. Vietnam: the American presence as the message's liability

Seven of the 25 documents concern Vietnam, about a quarter of the set. In them, information officers warn that American actions and the American presence would speak louder than American messages: in 1961 an official feared charges of germ warfare on Asians; in 1962 the Saigon government's image was judged no better than its own conduct; guidance from the Joint U.S. Public Affairs Office in Saigon held that the foreign presence would breed resentment as it grew; and USIA said it had always regarded its psychological operations support in Vietnam as short-term.

> But the spectre of charges that “U.S. imperialists are waging germ warfare on Asians” haunts me.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume I, Vietnam, 1961, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v01/d265

> The American presence is a “foreign” presence that unavoidably generates resentments which are likely to grow as the presence grows.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 48, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d48

> USIA has, from the beginning, considered its psychological operations (PSYOP) support program in Viet-Nam as short-term in nature.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 58, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58

The 1962 judgement on the Saigon government's image is in Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume II, Vietnam, 1962, Document 16, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v02/d16.

### 5. Beliefs about the audience

Officials described an audience that read American motives through its own politics, distrusted altruism, was alert to race, and was proud of its own. Phrases such as *Oriental mind*, *Asian mind* and *Asian mentality* appear in 30 documents in 22 of the regional volumes, most of them from 1949 to 1963, and in most of them it is an American official characterising Asians. A mid-1950s status report records USIA countering propaganda meant to stir up race hatred; a 1947 account of Chinese students found American policy condemned though the American people were not; a USIA analysis of the early 1970s found overseas Chinese proud of Peking's achievements but unwilling to copy its models; and the 1969 Manila officers noted that Japan's own image in Southeast Asia was poor.

> failure or inability explain our motives convincingly to oriental mind

> Bulk of Burmese people simply unable understand aid in terms of altruism.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d60

> In the Far East USIA was forced to deal with mixed emotions regarding the use of atomic weapons against Asiatic peoples, and to counter communist propaganda designed to stir up race hatred.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Foreign Economic Policy; Foreign Information Program, Volume IX, Document 190, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v09/d190

> But American policy toward China, which they conceive to be the product of a stupid or wicked government rather than the will of the American people, is heartily condemned by all student liberals.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947, The Far East: China, Volume VII, Document 245, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v07/d245

The overseas Chinese analysis is Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149.

### 6. China, 1971–1978: restraint and exchange

The opening to Peking made the information agencies weigh what not to say as well as what to show: before the President's visit officials wanted to seem neither soft on Communism nor hostile to Peking, and proposed to turn a temporary psychological asset into a lasting one; by the late 1970s the American mission in Peking saw a real, if revocable, chance to widen communication.

> While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 143, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d143

> The President’s projected visit to Peking presents us with opportunities to build what is now a temporary psychological asset into a long-range one.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 146, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146

> But the Chinese appear to be determined to go down this road, and until the line changes—which it can do although at considerable cost—we have an opportunity to enhance the communication process to a significant degree.

— Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d152

## How this was done

Because *soft power* is a term of 1990, the run first read the FRUS editors' own chapter headings (about 25,000 of them, of which about 150 bore on the subject) to learn the period's words — information policy, the foreign information programme, public diplomacy, cultural relations — and then searched the full text of all 553 FRUS volumes, of which it used 84: the regional East Asia and Pacific volumes for 1945–1980 and the eight volumes on the information programme and public diplomacy. The search index matches a word together with its inflections (*program* and *programs* alike), and every search was run with a phrase sure to be present and a string sure to be absent, to show the search itself was working. The share of regional documents using the programme's vocabulary rose from about 18 in every 1,000 in the 1940s (a figure carried largely by one 1949 volume on the closing of the information offices in Communist China) to about 52 in the 1960s, and fell to about 32 in the 1970s. Hits were read in samples to check that the words meant what was searched for; this caught *USI*, which in 1948–49 means the United States of Indonesia, not the information service, so two volumes of those years were left out of the decade count, and one *Informant Program* counted as an information programme. Documents were gathered by their headings (141 candidates), by a ranked full-text search, and by a measure of similarity in wording between whole documents, which put forward Heath's report from Saigon of 20 September 1952 that no keyword search had found; such neighbours were treated as suggestions to be read, not as evidence. Sixty-one documents were retrieved whole and read, the long ones through the passages around the search words (one proved to be an editorial note and was dropped), and 25 were kept. Every quotation in this overview was checked, character by character, against the text of its document, and every document cited here was checked to be in the collection; both checks passed. The exact counts, searches and denominators behind each figure are in the memo.
