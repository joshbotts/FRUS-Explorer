# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Twenty-six printed documents, dated from 1950 to 1977, show State Department and U.S. Information Agency officials judging how America appeared to East Asian and Pacific audiences, though never under the name "soft power". Early in the period they treated information work as part of a psychological strategy against communism, carried through local voices: a 1953 plan for Thailand spoke of "every suitable operation to prepare the minds and emotions of the people to collaborate in the effort" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). By 1966 a summary of the area's programmes held that "the image of American strength, reliability and friendship is basic to our objectives" and aimed its programmes at elites (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). The officials believed the region judged the United States by its conduct towards each country more than by American life at home: a 1969 conference of the agency's public affairs officers (the senior information officer at each embassy) recorded that "the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations … than on developments within the US" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31). They measured effect by placement in the local press, demand for American material, the reception of a visit and, from about 1970, opinion surveys; after 1975 their assessments turned on whether Asians still believed the United States would stay (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). About three documents in five come from the public-diplomacy volumes; the rest from the East Asia area volumes.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The archival record reaches the historian by two separate routes, which this overview keeps apart and never adds together. *Where the printed documents came from* (each document's source note): (a) the U.S. Information Agency's records, Record Group 306 at the National Archives, Entry A1–42, the Director's Subject Files, 1968–1972 (the largest single source), and Entries UD WW 257, UD WW 193, P–207 and P–104, plus one document whose source note the edition prints as "RG 305", Entry UD–UP 7, which is probably a misprint for Record Group 306; the app's bundled archival indexes give no National Archives identifier for any of these entries, so their identifiers, access status and, apart from A1–42's, their dates could not be established here; (b) the State Department's central files, Record Group 59, under five decimal files: 511.90 (information and cultural relations, Asia, 1950), 511.51G (the same class, Indochina, 1952), 790.5 and 792.5 (internal political and national defence affairs, Asia and Siam, 1953) and 611.98 (political relations with Indonesia, 1962), and the Central Foreign Policy File for two late telegrams; no identifier or access status was resolved for these; (c) the Operations Coordinating Board files, Record Group 59, Lot 61 D 385, which the bundled indexes hold without an identifier; (d) the Kennedy Library (Robert F. Kennedy papers), the Johnson Library (National Security File; Marks Papers) and the Nixon Presidential Materials (National Security Council Files). *What the editors cited in footnotes without printing*: only five such citations in the collection's documents, namely Lot 61 D 385 again; Lot 58 D 726, the Subject Files of the Office of the Officer in Charge for Malaya Affairs (Bureau of Far Eastern Affairs, Office of Southeast Asian Affairs), Record Group 59, Entry UD 53, National Archives identifier 2544398, 1955–1956, five linear inches, access marked "Restricted - Possibly"; the Johnson Library's Marks Papers; the Johnson Library's National Security File, Country File, Indonesia; and the Nixon Kissinger Office Files, Box 86. One further footnote citation, in the 1952 Saigon assessment, was counted but not read, and is not described here.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** First, the term itself: a scan of the full text of twenty-one volumes (the seven information and public-diplomacy volumes and fourteen East Asia volumes, 1950–1980) for "soft power", with hyphen and spacing variants, found it in none; the check phrase "Department of State" was found in every one of the twenty-one, and a made-up string in none, so the scan worked and the absence is real. The officials' words were "image", "psychological" and "public diplomacy", and those terms were used instead. Second, the opinion surveys: a search for survey, poll or public-opinion words together with the agency's names and Asian place names finds them mentioned widely, but the documents read summarise surveys rather than print them; the shelf is the agency's own files in Record Group 306, where one source note names a folder headed "Public Opinion Research". Third, anything after 1980: a check of the fourteen volumes for 1981 onward found none on public diplomacy, Asia, China, Japan, Korea or information, while the same check returned the matching titles for 1942–1980.

**Scope.** The argument rests on twenty-six documents read from the seven information and public-diplomacy volumes and the East Asia area volumes, 1950–1977, out of a search index holding all 553 volumes of the series. Documents about South Asia and the Near East, press relations in Washington, United Nations negotiation and internal reporting procedure were excluded, as were seven documents set aside after reading as off the subject.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** If it means only information, cultural and exchange work, the Vietnam psychological-operations documents fall out and the story becomes one about elites and image; if it includes wartime psychological operations, the counterinsurgency material stays and the audience becomes villagers as well as elites.
- **Where the region ends.** If South and Southeast Asia are read together, the nine South Asia and Near East documents that the word "Asia" also caught come back in and the Indochina weight falls; if East Asia means Northeast Asia alone, most of the 1950s and 1960s evidence, which is Southeast Asian, falls out.
- **Whose assessment counts.** If only Washington's judgements count, the post reports and field surveys become evidence about audiences rather than about policy; if field officers count as policymakers, the 1969 conference and the 1975 reassessment are central.
- **Meanwhile, the rule used:** a document is in if it is an assessment, plan or instruction about U.S. information, cultural, psychological or public-diplomacy work aimed at East Asian or Pacific audiences and was read; it is out if it is mainly about press relations in Washington, United Nations negotiation, internal reporting procedure or South Asia.

## Findings

### 1. In the 1950s, information was a psychological weapon carried by local voices

The earliest planning made information one arm of a strategy against communist expansion, worked through Asian governments and outlets, and tailored country by country: the 1953 Thailand plan held that "each nation is an entity basically different from the others and requires special treatment" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v12p2/d403). Officials claimed early effect from the press: "increased usage of USIE news and feature material in the local press throughout the area" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Volume VI, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v06/d31). The 1952 Saigon assessment, written for a Senate inquiry, read demand for American material as "intense interest in US by educated groups", judged films effective "though difficult to determine degree", said rural villagers were "not a primary target group", and told the programme to "soft-pedal" anything likely to stir "unbridled nationalism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Indochina, Volume XIII, Part 1, Document 116, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v13p1/d116). The limits officials set themselves came from their allies' position as much as the audience's: a 1955 paper on the Malaya programme recorded "a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, Southeast Asia, Volume XXII, Document 447, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v22/d447).

### 2. In the 1960s, the image to project was strength, reliability and friendship, aimed at elites

The 1966 summary put it plainly: "In almost every country of the area, our programs are directed at elite groups" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 73, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d73). Officials judged a visit by its reception: one was called "probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint", because many "seemed, for first time, to appreciate openness of the U.S. space program as compared to Russian" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VI, Public Diplomacy, 1961–1963, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv06/d127). Where the audience was neutral, they counselled restraint: the 1962 plan for Indonesia said "we should refrain from glamorous, immediate-impact actions" and that U.S. actions "must be pegged to a real understanding of and a demonstrated respect for Indonesia’s neutral stance" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XXIII: Southeast Asia, Document 289, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v23/d289).

### 3. Under insurgency, the voice was to be local and the American hand hidden

The officials' beliefs about the audience were bluntest in Vietnam. One review of the programme in Vietnam held that "Vietnamese peasants are, in the main, going to be persuaded by other Vietnamese" and described the population as "family-oriented, selfish and opportunistic even in the best of times" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VII, Public Diplomacy, 1964–1968, Document 32, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv07/d32). Host governments were pressed to speak for themselves: one document held that the Thai government "has not given proper attention to the need for a national information program designed to gain popular support" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVII, Mainland Southeast Asia; Regional Affairs, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v27/d386). The agency said it had treated its psychological-operations support in Vietnam "from the beginning" "as short-term in nature", and by 1970 its surveys showed the rating of the U.S. "as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Documents 58 and 88, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d58 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d88). The 1969 conference generalised the lesson: "Asians are, to varying degrees, America watchers", and "US visibility should be low" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Document 31, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d31).

### 4. Officials recorded where audiences pushed back, in Indonesia, Japan and the Chinese world

The writer of one memorandum called anti-American attacks in Indonesia "a situation that I can describe only as intolerable", a reaction the editors' note records a White House aide judging excessive (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Indonesia; Malaysia-Singapore; Philippines, Document 106, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d106). On Japan in 1971 a request for a review reported that "public opinion surveys and other assessments of Japanese attitudes have tended to confirm the seriousness of these strains" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XIX, Part 2, Japan, 1969–1972, Document 67, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p2/d67). Messaging about China was trimmed so as to offend neither side: "While we do not want to appear as having gone “soft” on Chinese Communism neither do we want to appear anti-Chinese or anti-Peking." Space achievements were proposed as something that "could generate favorable opinion toward the U.S.", and of the overseas Chinese one document reported: "Although the overseas Chinese appear proud of the achievements of Peking, they are reluctant to accept communist political and economic models" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917–1972, Volume VIII, Public Diplomacy, 1969–1972, Documents 143, 146 and 149, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d143, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d146 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917-72PubDipv08/d149).

### 5. After Saigon, credibility itself became the message

The 1975 reassessment, a round-up of the region, found the damage uneven: "The American defeat has been in good part written off by the Japanese", "Thais still see us as friends and admire much in the American system", yet "programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Organization and Management of Foreign Policy; Public Diplomacy, 1973–1976, Document 105, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v38p2/d105). In the late 1970s one memorandum called "shoring up the confidence of East Asians in America’s long-term staying power and positive involvement in the area" real policy, and a reply from the field reported a "deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing”" and a view of the United States as one that "seeks to implant American models on other societies" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Documents 79 and 83, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d79 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d83). Exchanges were redirected accordingly, towards "gut issues rather than some of the frothier aspects of cultural exchange" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXX, Public Diplomacy, Document 2, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v30/d2).

## How this was done

The search ran over a read-only copy of the app's full-text index of all 553 FRUS volumes. Because "soft power" is not the period's term, the scope was built from the officials' own vocabulary: a search of document headings for East Asian and Pacific place names in the seven information and public-diplomacy volumes (forty-two headings, of which nine concerned South Asia or the Near East and were set aside, leaving about one document in fifty of those volumes); a heading search for information, propaganda and psychological terms across the East Asia area volumes, where about three hits in four came from the Vietnam volumes; and a ranked word search of sixteen regional volumes, which found the 1953 Thailand and 1955 Malaya clusters. One document, the 1952 Saigon assessment, was found only by a search for documents whose overall content resembles a given document, and by no word search. Forty documents were retrieved whole; twenty-nine were read in full, seven in part and four not at all, and seven were set aside after reading. A vocabulary count over the full text of twenty-one volumes, with a check phrase that must appear and a made-up string that must not, established which terms measure the subject: "image of the United States" runs about eighteen times as often in the information volumes as in the area volumes, while "propaganda" in the area volumes mostly names the communist side's output and was not used. The figures' exact counts, rates and denominators are in the memo. Every quotation here is an exact passage the memo verified against its document, and every document cited is in the collection; both were checked before finishing.

## Read next

The collection "American soft power in East Asia and the Pacific: how officials judged it, 1950–1977" holds all twenty-six documents with their verified passages, arranged under the five findings. Its gaps are where the historian's additions would count most: the survey reports themselves, from the agency's files in Record Group 306, would test the officials' claims of effect; the years 1956–1961, nearly empty here, may reflect the searches rather than the edition, and a 1960 National Security Council policy paper for Southeast Asia is an unread lead, not in the collection and not used as evidence here; and Korea, Burma, Australia and New Zealand appear only inside the 1975 round-up, so country-level documents for them would widen the regional picture.
