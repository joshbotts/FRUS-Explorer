# How did policymakers assess American soft power in the east Asia and Pacific region? What messaging strategies did they use and how effective did they think they were? What beliefs about the regional audience contributed to those strategies?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series never uses the phrase "soft power": I searched about 36,000 documents in the 79 volumes on East Asia and public diplomacy and found it in none. Officials wrote instead of the information program, psychological strategy and public diplomacy. After touring the region, Jessup judged the United States weak on propaganda (1950, Volume VI, Document 32). In 1969 the information agency's public affairs officers reported that the American image depended on bilateral relations more than on events at home (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31). In 1975 the agency's overseas posts said programs on security lacked credibility until policy was clear (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105). In 1977 Jakarta called the new administration's public diplomacy effective, yet reported a lasting feeling that the United States was withdrawing (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83). Behind these strategies lay a belief that Asian audiences follow the winner, respect resolve, read aid as softness and remember colonialism.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The editors name archival series in the source notes of the documents they printed, and in footnotes to material they left unprinted. A *record group* is the National Archives' numbered grouping of one agency's records, and a *lot file* is a State Department office's papers retired under a lot number. Access statuses are the National Archives catalogue's. Where I could not match a series to the catalogue, I say so.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** The phrase "soft power" is not in the series. I searched it in the 79 volumes and in 220 other volumes dated 1946 or later, about 84,000 documents, and found it in none. The same search found "department of state" in 14,251 of the first set's documents, and a nonsense string in none, so the search works. Independent measurement of how audiences received the messages is also missing. In 77 of the volumes (the East Asia compilations and three public diplomacy volumes), only about thirty documents mention an opinion poll or survey; I searched "public opinion poll" and "opinion survey", and the search treats "poll" and "polls" as the same word. The agency's research reports might be on a shelf the finding aids call Program Files, Office of Research and Intelligence, 1954 to 1958, identifier 1254251, Restricted - Partly, but nothing confirms that it fits. The content of broadcasts, films and publications is described in the documents I read, not printed, and I ran no search for it. Candidate shelves are Records Relating to the Voice of America, Broadcasting Service, 1948 to 1959, identifier 7542975, and Subject Files, Office of Policy and Plans, 1953 to 1971, identifier 1257679, both Restricted - Partly. The catalogue gives these shelves an agency creator, and I verified neither as on topic. No series in my data is the agency's East Asia area office. I did not read the wartime information work, though fifty documents in nine volumes for 1941 to 1945 name the Office of War Information. The Asian side of the exchange appears only as US officials relayed it. Korea, Japan and Australia and New Zealand are thinly covered by what I read. I did not search South Asia, the Pacific islands or the 1980s.

**Scope.** I used 79 volumes, about 36,000 documents: the East Asia, Pacific, China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, Indochina and Vietnam compilations from 1946 to 1980, and the public diplomacy volumes that cover Asia. I left out front matter and editors' notes, the wartime volumes, second editions of volumes, and South Asia.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as soft power.** I took the officials' own terms, public diplomacy and the information program, so if you mean attraction by any means, exchanges, aid and culture with no information purpose would add documents.
- **Whose assessment counts as policymaker.** I included embassy and agency officers who wrote assessments, so if you want only the President, the secretaries and their senior staff, I would need to select the documents again.
- **How to read effectiveness.** I took officials' own judgments as the answer, so measured reception stays open until you add the agency's research reports.
- **The region.** I followed the series' own East Asia and Pacific volumes, so Burma is in and South Asia is out.
- **The start date.** I began in 1946, so starting in 1941 would add nine wartime volumes of about 8,700 documents.

Meanwhile my inclusion rule was this: a document in which a US official describes the audience, the message or method, or the effect, of American persuasion, information, exchange or psychological work directed at an East Asian or Pacific audience, selected by reading.

## Findings

### The 1950 effort was judged weak and badly aimed by its own officials

A conference of chiefs of mission (the heads of American missions abroad) at Bangkok in February 1950 found worldwide information material unsatisfactory. It asked for material aimed at specific groups in simple language. State's Far Eastern public affairs officer then reported that the program for Southeast Asia was stepped up (1950, Volume VI, Document 31). His evidence of effect was greater use of the material in local newspapers. A regional production center at Manila was planned. After a tour of fourteen countries, Jessup raised the question of whom the United States was trying to reach. The French High Commissioner for Indochina and the British Commissioner in Southeast Asia believed that Asians wait to see who is winning. Papers of 1952 to 1954 describe the same effort as psychological strategy. The one for Thailand relied on information programs through local outlets (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 403). I read the early verdict as weak aim, thin evidence of effect and shrinking money.

> Generally agreed USIE materials designed for world-wide or even area-wide distribution not satisfactory. (1950, Volume VI, Document 15)

> Ambassador Jessup considered that we are weak on propaganda. He raised the question of whom we are trying to reach and recommended that more thought be given to methods of reaching our audience and to the themes we are trying to put across. (1950, Volume VI, Document 32)

> During FY 1954 a reduction in the USIS film program by 50% will be necessary because of lack of funds. (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 359)

### Officials believed Asian audiences follow the winner, respect resolve and read aid as softness

Officials wrote of an "Oriental mind" or "Asian mind" as a working theory of the audience. I searched six such phrases, among them "Oriental mind", "Asian psychology" and "Asiatic mind", in the 79 volumes. I found 32 documents that use the wording, counting forms such as "Asian minds". In seventeen of them the wording describes how American policy, aid or messaging would be received. In fifteen it has other uses, such as an Asian leader's own claim, a remark about negotiators or praise of a general. The split is my reading, and some cases are marginal. The belief shaped strategy. General Ridgway wanted Americans to overcome any feeling that US interest in Asia was casual or temporary (1952–1954, Volume XIV, Part 2, Document 551). In Burma, Ambassador Sebald said Americans were too conspicuous and that most Burmese could not understand aid as altruism (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 2, Document 60). A Secretary of State circular used the belief to argue for defending Formosa. The chargé in Saigon argued that aid should come on terms of strict reciprocity. Asian leaders turned the formula on Americans. President Magsaysay said that Americans misjudged the importance of gifts to visitors. Diem was convinced that Americans, not being Asians, did not understand the Oriental mind (1958–1960, Volume I, Document 26).

> it is in the pattern of the Oriental psychology to respect and follow aggressive, resolute and dynamic leadership—to quickly turn on a leadership characterized by timidity or vacillation (1950, Volume VI, Document 266)

> In Asia give-away programs are too frequently mistaken in the oriental mind as meaning that the donor not only has a soft heart but also a soft head. (1952–1954, Volume XIII, Part 2, Document 992)

> Said further Americans do not understand Asian mind when they fail realize importance gifts to friendly visitors (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 361)

### Officials assumed a colonial memory that limited what could be said

A consul general in Singapore warned that anti-colonialism might blur what an invasion of Laos meant to Southeast Asians. He asked for a coordinated psychological offensive. The Far Eastern bureau recorded that the information agency objected to expanding its Malaya program, partly for fear of seeming to support colonialism. State overrode the objection. The Deputy Under Secretary warned that support for a Dutch trusteeship of New Guinea would be read by Asians as colonialism. In each case officials weighed a message or policy by the reception they expected. I found these cases by reading, so they show the belief at work, not how common it was.

> Tendency in SEA to attribute some “pure nationalism” to Viet Minh aims will make more difficult characterization of invasion as “naked aggression.” (1952–1954, Volume XII, Part 1, Document 103)

> a fear of placing the U.S. in the position of appearing to support colonialism (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 447)

> US support for a Dutch trusteeship would be widely interpreted as evidence of a US desire to maintain Western control over Asian peoples or, to Asian minds, of US “colonialism”. (1955–1957, Volume XXII, Document 271)

### What drew Asian audiences, and what wore it down, in the officials' accounts

Where officials say the United States was admired, they point to institutions, mass culture, the American way of life and single events. Ambassador Reischauer rated a 1963 visit by the astronaut John Glenn to Japan the most successful American visit for the public image. Where they say the image slipped, they point to security politics, Tet and American disorder. The agency's director reported that Indonesian pressure had left the information service in Indonesia nearly without a program (1964–1968, Volume XXVI, Document 106). The public affairs officers of 1969 judged that bilateral issues counted for more than the messages. I read that as a judgment that messaging could not repair doubt about policy.

> what was probably the most successful visit by an American, from a public image standpoint (1917–1972, Volume VI, Document 127)

> popular respect for US political institutions, infatuation with many aspects of American mass culture, genuine respect for our intellectual attainments, and visible attractions of the American way of life, have kept America a strongly positive symbol. (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 123)

> Racial violence and social unrest in America have roused concern over basic stability of American society, made American image a rather less positive political symbol. (1964–1968, Volume XXIX, Part 2, Document 123)

> PAOs emphasized that the image of the US usually depended more on the state of bi-lateral relations (security treaty, Okinawa, Korean military modernization, trade restrictions) than on developments within the US. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 31)

### Vietnam turned the information agency into a wartime agency

The agency's own planners judged that some of its tasks in Vietnam, Thailand and Laos lay outside its proper role. It treated its psychological operations support in Vietnam as short-term, and it planned to hand its Vietnam work to the Vietnamese government (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 58). It set objectives for the Nixon-Thieu meeting at Midway as strengthening a public image (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 24). After the 1970 coup in Cambodia it reported that the Lon Nol government had little to counter Communist propaganda (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 92). Senator Church charged that the agency was selling the Thieu government to the Vietnamese public. Among dated documents in the 79 volumes, about one in thirty-six in the 1950s named the agency or its services, such as its overseas information service or the Voice of America. About one in twelve did so in the 1960s. The 1970s rate is nearly as high, but nearly half of those documents come from one public diplomacy volume, so part of the rise is built into my choice of volumes.

> Some of these tasks appear to lie outside the Agency’s appropriate sphere of operations.
>
> These three country operations absorb nearly fifteen percent of the Agency’s resources available for overseas programming. (1917–1972, Volume VII, Document 147)

> Rating of the U.S. as a dependable ally of SVN reached the lowest point in thirteen Saigon surveys, with only 26 percent of the general public and 23 percent of the college-educated thinking of U.S. as “very dependable” or “rather dependable.” (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 88)

> is now engaged in a massive campaign, using every tool of the communication arts, to sell the Thieu Government to the people of Vietnam. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 129)

### After Vietnam and the China opening, reassurance became the message

From 1969 the line was partnership and Asian initiative. The China opening then constrained messages. State objected to releasing an agency film on Tibet before the President's visit to Peking, because it would send the wrong signal (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 143). The agency proposed to turn "a temporary psychological asset" into a lasting one on that trip (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 146). It also looked closely at the overseas Chinese as an audience, who were proud of Peking's achievements but reluctant to accept communist models (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 149). Its East Asia deputy thought the Communists more alert to the cultural approach (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 127). After 1975 posts reported that audiences doubted American staying power, whatever the programs said. In 1977 the agency's East Asia chief pointed to Secretary Vance's speech as evidence that policy was meant to shore up confidence (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 79). Holbrooke then asked chiefs of mission how public diplomacy should serve policy in Asia (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 81). Jakarta added that the human-rights message was read as accusatory.

> Peace in Asia—which is a precondition to peace in the world—cannot result primarily from American conceptions but has to involve Asian initiatives and regional and sub-regional arrangements that have an Asian origin. (1917–1972, Volume VIII, Document 30)

> During this waiting period, programs on U.S. security relations in Asia lack credibility and may in fact do damage to our relations. (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105)

> The denouement in Indochina has left Hong Kong viewing USG as “very clearly in default.” (1969–1976, Volume XXXVIII, Part 2, Document 105)

> In general, public diplomacy of the new administration in this area has been effective.
>
> We still face deeply implanted and persistent feeling that new administration is “withdrawing” or, at least, “reducing priority” in East Asia. (1977–1980, Volume XXX, Document 83)

## How this was done

I took the East Asia compilations from the volumes' printed titles, and I added the public diplomacy volumes after counting information vocabulary by volume and reading the editors' headings. I searched in the editors' vocabulary, because the series says information program, psychological strategy and public diplomacy, not soft power. I ran the term searches with two controls: a nonsense string that should find nothing, and "department of state", which should find something. I also tested words that might mislead. "Prestige", "image", "propaganda" and "oriental" turn up at about the same rate in 220 other volumes, so I did not scope by them, while the agency's overseas service name, USIS, turns up nearly four times as often in the 79 volumes. I read about ninety documents in full or in part and chose the ones cited, so the sample is not random. Vietnam-era material dominates because the agency's own records are best printed there. I reached the May 1975 assessments by a search for documents close in meaning to a 1969 conference summary, not by word search, and treat such neighbours as suggestions. I checked every quotation against the text of its document and every cited document against the collection.

## Read next

The collection is *East Asia and the Pacific: how U.S. officials judged their persuasion, 1950-1977*. It holds the documents cited here, with the passages quoted. Adding Japan, Korea and Australia and New Zealand documents would test whether the pattern holds where my reading was thin. Adding the wartime Far East volumes and the Japan occupation's information and education work would extend it before 1946. Adding the agency's audience-research reports and the shelves named above would move it from officials' judgments toward measured reception.
