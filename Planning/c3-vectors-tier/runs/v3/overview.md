# How have U.S. policymakers' thinking about Cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows five distinct ways of thinking, each tied to an era. In the 1870s and 1880s officials treat Cuba as a rich neighbour that should become independent and must not pass to another European power; security is not yet the vocabulary, commerce is. In 1897–98 the case for war rests on sugar, American capital, disease and disorder as much as on the Maine. From the Platt Amendment of 1901 to the abandonment of the right to intervene in 1934, security (naval stations, intervention) and prosperity (sugar, tariffs, loans) are handled together in treaties. From the Second World War to the late 1950s Cuba is a hemisphere-defence and sugar-supply problem. From 1959 the threat is defined by communism, Soviet weapons and subversion, and Cuban prosperity becomes something to be withheld; after the missile crisis it is analysed as a managed problem whose economic leverage is modest. The argument rests on 23 printed documents, drawn from about 4,900 documents in the editors' own Cuba chapters and about 13,900 documents that mention Cuba anywhere, in the 553 volumes indexed. The documents are cited under each finding below.

Ball (Lot 74 D 272, entry A1 5175, identifier 614703, 1961–1966, unrestricted); the files on Operation Mongoose kept by the Coordinator of Cuban Affairs (Lot 66 D 501, entry UD-UP 293-B, identifier 648748, 1963–1965, unrestricted); the Special Assistant on Communism's subject files (Lot 62 D 24, entry A1 3148, identifier 2363836, 1958–1961, partly restricted); the national-security files of the Kennedy Library (collection identifier 1164), the Johnson Library (567979), the Carter Library's national security adviser's office (560131), and the Eisenhower and Nixon materials, all under the libraries' own access rules; and Central Intelligence Agency files, for which the data holds no shelf. *Series the editors cite in footnotes without printing*: the Executive Secretariat's records relating to National Security Council policy papers (Lot 66 D 95, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted, a lot the National Archives splits with a second series, Subject Files, identifier 2839193); the Coordinator of Cuban Affairs' Records Relating to Cuba (Lot 73 D 191, entry A1-5758, identifier 1040618, 1957–1975, partly restricted, 27 linear feet); the Records Relating to Sugar (Lot 65 D 212, entry UD-UP 215, identifier 27022874, 1955–1962, fully restricted); the correspondence with foreign heads of state (Lot 66 D 204, entry A1 1590, identifier 2538452, 1953–1964, access possibly restricted); and again the Kennedy, Carter and Johnson national-security files. For the years before 1910 the editors cite no unprinted series in the index, so the shelf is the National Archives' microfilmed Record Group 59 series: the despatches of the U.S. ministers to Spain (1792–1906) and to Cuba (1902–1906), the despatches of the U.S. consuls in Havana (1783–1906), and the Cuban legation's notes (1844–1906).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** First, the phrase 'national security' as a description of Cuba's importance: I searched every document that mentions Cuba, Cuban or Havana in all 553 volumes for the phrase, and before 1940 the phrase appears in only eight such documents, none of which uses it about Cuba's bearing on American safety (they concern wartime bond acts, disarmament talks, the Paris peace conference and a Colombian coast-guard request); the search could return hits (it returns about 2,600 in all, almost all after 1950), a search for a phrase that should be everywhere returned documents in every decade, and a search for a nonsense string returned none. The earlier thinking is therefore read under the officials' own words: independence, defence, commerce, sanitation. Second, any measurement of Cuba's effect on American prosperity: I searched the Cuba documents of 1910–1935 for the words American investments and American capital; one document gives a total, and it is the Cuban ambassador's claim of 1929, not an American estimate. The shelf that holds the figures is the U.S. Tariff Commission's report of 1929 on the effects of the reciprocity treaty (which that note names) and the sugar records above. Third, Cuban and Spanish views, which appear only as American officials report them; they are not in FRUS and not in the bundled shelf lists. Fourth, anything after January 1989: the library holds two documents that mention Cuba dated 1989, both from January, and none later. Fifth, Congress, the sugar lobbies and public opinion appear only through officials' accounts; I did not try to measure them.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series as indexed, 311,429 documents once front matter, editors' notes and the duplicate second editions are set aside: 13,899 of them mention Cuba (in 481 volumes) and 4,897 sit in the editors' own Cuba chapters (62 volumes, 1900–1980). Everything below is conditional on this library, which holds every volume.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as 'Cuba's impact.'** I used the editors' own Cuba chapters for trends from 1900 and any mention of Cuba for the nineteenth century, when the volumes are arranged by correspondent and have no Cuba chapters. If you want only Cuba-specific documents, the 1870s–1890s findings thin to a handful; if you want every mention, about three in five of those documents mention Cuba fewer than five times.
- **'National security' or its equivalents.** The phrase is almost absent before the 1940s. I read independence, defence, protection of citizens and commerce as the earlier equivalents; if you hold the phrase to its modern sense, the answer to the first half of the question is that the thinking begins in the 1940s, and the nineteenth-century material becomes a different question.
- **'Prosperity.'** I took it to mean trade and sugar, American investment and claims, and (later) economic pressure on Cuba. If you mean Cuban prosperity as the Americans' aim, the 1934 trade agreement and the 1960 plan to weaken the economy are the poles; if you mean American prosperity, the sugar supply and the investments are.
- **Where the series ends for this topic.** The library's last Cuba material is January 1989. A later period needs a different source.

Meanwhile the inclusion rule was: every document the argument rests on, taken from the editors' Cuba chapters wherever they exist and from text searches where they do not, and nothing else.

## Findings

Before the six findings, one measurement across all of them. The vocabulary shifts sharply across the series. The phrase 'national security' appears in none of the Cuba chapter documents from 1900 to 1916 and in a single one from the 1910s (a bond act); in almost none of the 1940s; in about one in twenty of the 1950s; in about one in three of the 1960s; in about two in three of the 1970s; and in nearly nine in ten of the 1980s. That is not a Cuba finding: documents mentioning Cuba use the phrase about as often as the whole series does in the same decade (about one in three in the 1960s in both). Sugar, by contrast, is a Cuban theme: it appears in about two in five chapter documents of the 1940s, about one in ten in the 1960s and 1970s, and in none of the 44 chapter documents from the 1980s. Communism, the Soviet Union or missiles appear in about one in twenty of the 1930s chapter documents, one in three in the 1950s and nearly two in three in the 1960s, although the whole series uses those words in about half of all its documents of the 1960s, so the Cuban excess is real but moderate.

### 1. A rich neighbour that must be independent and stay out of European hands, 1874–1886

In February 1874 Secretary of State Fish told the new minister in Madrid that Cuba "is the largest insular possession still retained by any European power in America. It is almost contiguous to the United States." He called the state of Cuba "the one great cause of perpetual solicitude" in American foreign relations, and argued that independence was inevitable and right (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, Mr. Fish to Mr. Cushing, 6 February 1874, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552). In 1881 Secretary Blaine, writing about Hawaii, called Cuba "That rich island, the key to the Gulf of Mexico, and the field for our most extended trade in the Western Hemisphere" and "a part of the American commercial system" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1881, Mr. Blaine to Mr. Comly, 1 December 1881, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d405). The same year-by-year concern shows in routine trade reporting: in 1886 the legation in Madrid noted that the United States "buys about 95 per cent, of the sugar exported from Cuba" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1886, Mr. Curry to Mr. Bayard, 22 October 1886, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1886/d414). Security is described through geography, trade and the exclusion of a rival, not through the modern vocabulary.

### 2. The sugar bowl and the case for war, 1897–1898

On 5 October 1897 Minister Woodford told the German ambassador in Madrid that "the United States does not seek to annex Cuba or to establish a protectorate over Cuba," and then listed what Cuba meant: its sugar plantations "are as necessary to the food supply of the United States as are the cotton fields of India and the cotton and wheat fields of Egypt and the grain fields of Russia to England and to other countries". He added that the American cane crop "does not furnish more than one-tenth of the sugar we consume", that American investments in plantations, mines and railways were being destroyed, and that yellow fever came from Cuba (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, Mr. Woodford to Mr. Sherman, 5 October 1897, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/d498). The President's message to Congress in April 1898 added injury to American commerce and a threat to peace as grounds: "The right to intervene may be justified by the very serious injury to the commerce, trade, and business of our people," and "The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, the President's message on Cuba, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). Prosperity and security are argued together, with public health beside them.

### 3. Security and prosperity written into one treaty system, 1904–1934

The treaty of 1904, whose proclamation reprints the Platt Amendment, put a naval-station clause beside the intervention right: Cuba would lease land for naval stations "to enable the United States to maintain the independence of Cuba, and to protect the people thereof, as well as for its own defense," and consented to American intervention "for the preservation of Cuban independence" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1904, proclamation of the treaty with Cuba, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d207). In 1930 Secretary Stimson, with Cuba in turmoil, told the press that "A great many people seem to think that the Platt Amendment gives us a protectorate over the internal affairs of Cuba" and that this was not the policy (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume II, Memorandum of Conference by the Secretary of State with the Press, 2 October 1930, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v02/d747). In 1934 Hull's instruction on the trade agreement said the new treaty, "eliminating the permissive right of intervention by this Government, has removed a chronic source of friction between our two countries," while the sugar duty cut was meant to help the Cuban people and open markets for American farm and factory goods (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 127, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d127). The right of intervention was given up in the same year as the sugar tariff was cut, and Hull presents the two as parts of one policy that pairs political understanding with profitable commercial relationships. I also read Ambassador Welles' 1933 telegram, which still treats intervention as a live obligation (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 299, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d299), and the Cuban ambassador's note of 1929 claiming nearly 1,500 million dollars of American capital in Cuba (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1929, Volume II, Document 924, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1929v02/d924); that figure is Cuba's claim, not an American estimate.

### 4. Hemisphere defence, bases and sugar as a 'strategic concept,' 1941–1958

When the Navy wanted to enlarge the Guantánamo naval station in 1941, the State Department objected that the principle of leased areas under American jurisdiction "could not be applied to other countries of the Hemisphere" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1941, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v07/d119). By December 1944 the Joint Chiefs wanted "a defense facility in western Cuba under the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States Government" after the war (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 878, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d878), and Cuba's president refused: "President Grau, as a matter of sovereignty, is adamant on the proposition that there shall not be a lease of the bases to anyone" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 741, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d741). Prosperity was argued in strategic terms too: in 1954 the Secretary of State defended Cuba's share of the American sugar market by pointing out that Cuba "has been a reliable expansible source of sugar in both war and peace, a strategic concept which should not be jeopardized" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 342, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d342). In 1958 State and the Joint Chiefs were already defining Cuban internal stability as part of hemispheric defence (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 70, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d70).

### 5. Communism, missiles and economic denial, 1959–1963

In March 1959 the Director of Central Intelligence described "Fidel Castro, the instigator of the present unsettled situation," as "advocating a neutralistic bloc in Latin America, while actually supporting Communist-dominated revolutionary groups" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05/d112). In April 1960 a State Department official concluded: "it follows that every possible means should be undertaken promptly to weaken the economic life of Cuba." He named the sugar legislation as the principal instrument (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 499, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d499): prosperity, once a shared interest, had become a weapon. The October 1962 transcripts show how contested the security case was even inside the government: McGeorge Bundy asked, "What is the strategic impact on the position of the United States of MRBMs in Cuba? How gravely does this change the strategic balance?" and Secretary McNamara answered that the Chiefs said "substantially" but that "My own personal view is, not at all" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d21). The NSC minutes of four days later record the President's view that the missiles had "an entirely different impact throughout Latin America" than the planes (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d34), and a December 1963 memorandum put the minimum aim of policy as "a Cuba which poses no threat to its neighbors and which is not a Soviet satellite", with isolation and covert measures as the main weapons (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, Document 729, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d729). The danger, as officials stated it, was political and regional as much as strategic.

### 6. A managed problem, 1969–1981

A 1969 interdepartmental study defined the issue in a sentence: "Cuba has been a double-barreled problem for the U.S.: —as a base for Soviet military power, and —as an exporter of revolutionary doctrine and violence to Latin America." It ranked preventing strategic weapons as the only vital interest (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–10, Documents on American Republics, 1969–1972, Document 205, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d205). In 1977, when the Carter administration weighed normalization, the Treasury Secretary advised that "The political significance of both sides of the trade issue far outweighs economic effects." He added that American claims for expropriated property came to about two billion dollars (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d21). In January 1981 Secretary Haig advised the new President that "The intent of the 1962 Understanding was to prevent Cuba from becoming a threat to the security of the US—and to countries friendly to us in the Hemisphere" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1981–1988, Volume III, Soviet Union, January 1981–January 1983, Document 5, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v03/d5).

## How this was done

I counted, decade by decade, the documents that mention Cuba, Cuban or Havana (about one in fifteen of the dated documents in the 1870s, one in thirteen in the 1890s, one in forty-five in the 1940s, one in nine in the 1960s), and separately took the 4,897 documents the editors themselves placed in chapters titled Cuba. I read the chapter headings first and searched with the editors' own wording, tested the common word groups against a nonsense string and a phrase that should appear everywhere, and checked each search's wording against the actual text of the documents (the index treats related word forms as the same word, so every phrase count was re-checked by reading the literal words in the text). I retrieved 23 documents whole, read the relevant parts of each, and quoted 35 passages. I also used the numerical fingerprints that summarise each document's meaning: I found the nearest-neighbour documents of six anchor documents, compared the fingerprints of whole volumes, read the map of document clusters, and checked for reprints; two documents in this overview (the December 1963 memorandum and the March 1959 intelligence memorandum) were reached that way and by no word search, and a reprint of the Blaine document of 1881 in an 1894 appendix was detected and set aside. About five in six of the Cuba chapter documents from 1950 on sit in one cluster on the map, which is consistent with the idea that the postwar Cuba problem was a single subject, but the map is a projection and I use it only to explore. Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo verified against its document, and that every document cited here is in the collection.
