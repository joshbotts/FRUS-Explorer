# How have U.S. policymakers' thinking about Cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Across the whole printed series — all 553 volumes, of which about 6,700 documents fall under the editors' Cuba headings or name Cuba in their titles — the documents show the argument moving through four registers. In the 1870s Cuba mattered as a neighbour whose war, slavery and commerce spilled onto the United States: Secretary Fish wrote that the island was "Commercially, as well as geographically" closer to the United States than to Spain (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, Document 552, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552). By 1897–98 the same disorder had become a threat to American property and to domestic peace, "a constant menace to our peace" in the President's 1898 message (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, the President's message, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). From 1902 to the Second World War the security case was a protectorate's — intervention rights, Guantánamo, wartime air bases — and the prosperity case was sugar and tariffs, with Washington arguing in 1933 that better trade would calm Cuban politics (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 241, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d241). After 1959 the two were fused and inverted: Cuba became a Soviet-aligned base for revolution and missiles, and the economy became a weapon to be turned against it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 517, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d517; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d34). By 1974 a State Department memorandum could say flatly that "The United States has no vital interests in Cuba" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Document 278, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d278), and the Carter documents treat Cuba as a bargaining problem, Africa and refugees (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Documents 19 and 98, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d19 and https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d98).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Series the printed documents came from* (about 5,900 of the 6,700 carry a source note): the Department of State's Central Decimal File in Record Group 59, chiefly the classes for Cuba's internal political affairs, its finances, its agriculture (sugar) and U.S.–Cuban commerce, 1910–1963 (the bundled data name no identifier for the decimal file series itself); in RG 59 the lot files Records Relating to the Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962–1963 (entry A1 3053, NARA identifier 27022950, partly restricted), Records of Under Secretary George W. Ball, 1961–1966 (A1 5175, 614703, unrestricted), Files Pertaining to Operation Mongoose, 1963–1965 (UD-UP 293-B, 648748, unrestricted), the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs' Subject Files, 1959–1962 (A1 3147, 2068466, partly restricted), the Special Assistant on Communism's Subject Files, 1958–1961 (A1 3148, 2363836, partly restricted), and Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council, 1948–1961 (A1 1583E/F, 2838992, partly restricted); and, outside RG 59, the Kennedy Library's National Security Files (collection 1164; Country Files 1961–1963, 193236), the Johnson Library's National Security File (567979; Latin America Country Files, 567985) and the Eisenhower Library's Whitman File (probably Papers as President, 1124), for which the bundled data give no access status; the Carter Library's National Security Affairs files did not resolve to an identifier. For the nineteenth century the shelves are RG 59's microfilmed Despatches from U.S. Consuls in Havana, 1783–1906 (file unit 196006797), Notes from the Cuban Legation, 1844–1906 (183303950), and Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Cuba, 1902–1906 (177380758), with no entry or access data bundled. *Series the editors cited without printing* (about 680 documents carry such citations, none before December 1910): RG 59's Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, 1947–1979 (A1 1586E, 2839192, partly restricted), the most-cited lot; Presidential and Secretary of State Correspondence with Foreign Heads of State, 1953–1964 (A1 1590, 2538452); the Secretary's and Under Secretary's Memorandums of Conversation, 1953–1964 (A1 1566, 602231, unrestricted); Records Relating to Cuba, 1957–1975, of the Coordinator of Cuban Affairs (A1-5758, 1040618, partly restricted); and, again, the Missile Crisis and Ball lots and the Kennedy, Johnson and Carter national security files. Two lots that the documents cite did not resolve to any series.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** *"National security" as the policymakers' own concept*: I searched the phrase in all 553 volumes. It appears in about 1,040 of the Cuba documents, but in only about 47 of them in the officials' own prose; in the rest it is the name of an archive or of the Council (the Kennedy Library's "National Security Files"). Its rate among the 1960s Cuba documents is no higher than in the whole 1960s corpus, so the phrase measures the series, not Cuba. The controls held: the phrase "Department of State" was found in all but one of the 553 volumes, and a nonsense term in none. *"Prosperity"*: in no decade do more than 22 Cuba documents use the word, so it is not how anyone argued. The period's own words (commerce, property, sugar, tariffs, reciprocity) carry the economic case instead. *The 1880s and the Ten Years' War seen from Havana*: the editors filed this in the Spain chapters, and the Cuba headings hold almost nothing for 1870–1889. The shelf is the Havana consular despatches named above. *Public, congressional and business opinion, and Cuban sources*: FRUS prints government records. It cannot show what sugar interests, Congress or Cubans thought, except where a document quotes them.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series were searched; the working set is about 6,700 documents under the editors' Cuba headings or with Cuba in their titles, from the 1860s to 1980, plus a second set of about 470 Cuba-heavy documents of 1865–1889 drawn from the Spain chapters. Front matter, editorial notes and the second editions of two volumes were excluded.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Does "Cuba policy" include the Spain chapters of 1865–1897?** Including them (as I did, as a separate set) puts the 1870s among the densest decades for Cuba; excluding them makes the 1870s and 1880s look nearly empty, an artefact of where the editors filed the documents.
- **Do multi-country headings count?** The First World War headings that list Cuba with other states inflate the 1910s defence counts; dropping them would sharpen the 1940s and 1960s contrast. I kept them and flagged the effect.
- **Is "security" the officials' word or yours?** If the question means the officials' own concept, the evidence before 1945 must be read through words like "menace", "peace", "intervention" and "naval station". If it means the analyst's category, the frame counts below are the measure. I used the second meanwhile.
- **Does a Cuban voice count as evidence of U.S. thinking?** The 1902 message of the Cuban President shows the sugar-and-tariff bargain from the other side; I cite it as context only.

## Findings

### 1. In the 1870s Cuba was a neighbour's war, a slave society and a market

Among the Cuba-heavy documents of the 1870s, about half speak of insurrection and about one in three of commerce. About one in four speak of slavery or emancipation, a concern that fades almost to nothing by the 1890s (see the memo). In the mid-nineteenth-century annuals Cuba came up more often, relative to the size of each decade, than in any decade before the 1960s. The arguments are geographic and commercial, and the slavery question is framed by American race politics.
- "With the Spanish islands of Cuba and Porto Rico we maintain, in spite of their adverse legislation, a large commerce by reason of our necessities and of their proximity." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1870, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1870/d197
- "a question touching so large a number of colored people dwelling almost within sight of our southern boundary" — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1873, Part I, Volume II, Document 50, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1873p1v2/d50
- "Commercially, as well as geographically, it is by nature more closely connected with the United States than with Spain." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, Document 552, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552

### 2. In 1897–98 disorder became a danger to property and domestic peace

In the 1890s Cuba documents, American citizens, property and capital appear in about one in five, and insurrection in more than a quarter. Washington argued that a war on the country's doorstep threatened both investment and the tranquillity of American politics, and that this justified intervention.
- "Not only are our citizens largely concerned in the ownership of property and in the industrial and commercial ventures which have been set on foot in Cuba through our enterprising initiative and sustained by their capital, but the chronic condition of trouble and violent derangement in that island constantly causes disturbance in the social and political condition of our own people." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, Document 490, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/d490
- "The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace, and entails upon this Government an enormous expense." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, the President's message, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83

### 3. 1902–1945: a protectorate's security and a sugar economy

The economic vocabulary shifts to sugar, tariffs and reciprocity. In the 1940s more than a third of the Cuba documents mention sugar, and about one in five mention strategic or defence matters. Security meant treaty rights and bases. In 1933 the Department presented trade as the cure for Cuban unrest and its own pressure as a way to avoid intervention. The 1934 treaty dropped the intervention right but kept Guantánamo. In 1945 the ambassador valued a wartime air base above all others in the Caribbean.
- "The immediate remedy would be to obtain a satisfactory reduction in the American tariffs" — the Cuban President's message, enclosed in Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1902, Document 294, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1902/d294
- "a speedy improvement in economic and commercial conditions in Cuba would result in an immediate allaying of popular unrest and of political agitation" and "They are, on the contrary, measures intended to prevent the necessity of intervention." — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 241, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d241
- "the stipulations of that agreement with regard to the naval station of Guantánamo shall continue in effect." — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d170
- "The military advantages to the United States of access to the Batista base, at the very least on conditions permitting unlimited use at all times during the life of the convention, are of course very great." — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 745, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d745

### 4. 1959–1963: Cuba as a Soviet base, and the economy as a weapon

The 1960s are the peak: about 114 of every 1,000 documents of the decade name Cuba, and about 72 of every 1,000 sit under a Cuba heading (a quarter of those in one microfiche supplement). Communism or the Soviet Union appears in about three in five of these documents, strategic or defence matters in about three in eight, and missiles in about one in five. Prosperity now ran the other way: officials planned to use sugar and oil to make Cubans suffer under Castro.
- "use judiciously selected economic pressures at those points where Castro’s domestic and international policies have weakened Cuba’s economic structure in order to engender more public discomfort and discontent" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 517, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d517
- "Since we have crossed one Rubicon with our sugar and oil actions, should we not consider other economic measures that could have a significant impact on Castro’s regime?" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 567, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d567
- "there is no doubt that Cuba is being used as a base for export of the communist-fidelista revolution." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume X, Cuba, January 1961–September 1962, Document 202, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10/d202
- "This route is aimed at preventing any addition to the strategic missiles already deployed to Cuba and eventually to eliminate these missiles." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d34

### 5. 1974–1980: a declining stake, a bargaining chip and refugees

By the mid-1970s the economic denial programme had outlived its purpose in the Department's own telling. Under Carter the embargo was a lever for Cuban restraint in Africa and for human rights. Refugees and exiles, rare before 1959, appear in about one in five of the 1960s documents and about half of the 1980 documents (one volume only; see the memo).
- "The United States has no vital interests in Cuba." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 1, Documents on Mexico; Central America; and the Caribbean, 1973–1976, Document 278, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d278
- "The embargo was our biggest bargaining chip, and we should not “puncture” it without getting some commitment to international restraint." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 19, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d19
- "trying to control the flow of Cuban refugees into the United States." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 98, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d98

## How this was done

I worked from a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer index covering all 553 volumes. I built the set from the editors' own chapter headings that name Cuba, plus documents whose titles name it. Each decade was measured against the number of dated documents in that decade, with front matter and editorial notes removed. Each theme was counted by exact word patterns over the document text, because the index's search folds different words together; for example, it treats "communism" and "communication" as the same word. The counts include editors' footnotes, which I could not separate from the documents. Two words from the question, "national security" and "prosperity", failed a test against the whole corpus and were set aside as measures. I resolved archival sources through the app's bundled finding-aid data. I also used the app's document-similarity vectors to find neighbours of key documents; two of the cited documents, the 1874 Fish instruction and the 1974 Rush memorandum, were found that way and by no word search. A neighbour of this kind is a reading lead, not evidence, and both were read before being cited. I read 22 documents whole and quote 16 of them. I checked that every quotation here and every excerpt in the collection was verified in the memo against its document's text, and that every document cited here is in the collection.
