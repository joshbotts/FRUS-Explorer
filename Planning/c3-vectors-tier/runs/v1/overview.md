# How have U.S. policymakers' thinking about Cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly** — The printed record answers the question across the whole span, and what it shows is a change in which stake leads. From 1874 to 1898 officials argued from neighbourhood and trade: Cuba was “almost contiguous to the United States” and could afford “a large and lucrative market” (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, Document 552, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552), and the war message of April 1898 set “the commerce, trade, and business of our people” beside “a constant menace to our peace” (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, the President's message to Congress, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). Under the terms of 1902 and the treaty of 1934 the two stakes were written into instruments, an exclusion of rival powers and a naval station on one side and a preferred sugar market on the other (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1902, Document 273, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1902/d273; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d170). By 1951 a State Department statement held both in one sentence, calling Cuba “of great strategic importance” for its place on the sea lanes and as the source of sugar in two wars (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 440, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d440). After 1959 the market became a weapon; in October 1962 one of the President's advisers said the problem was “a domestic, political problem” rather than a military one; and from 1969 an interagency study ranked only “strategic weapons” as a vital interest (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–10, Documents on American Republics, 1969–1972, Document 205, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d205), so that in 1979 a President could say of a Soviet brigade in Cuba “It presents no direct threat to us” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume I, Foundations of Foreign Policy, Document 129, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v01/d129). This rests on 22 documents chosen from about 4,100 in which Cuba is named five or more times, among about 12,300 documents that name it at all in the 553 volumes indexed.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest** — The two channels below are separate counts and must not be added together. *Channel one, where the printed documents came from* (the roughly 4,100 documents that name Cuba five or more times): for 1900 to 1957, most came from the Department of State's central decimal file in Record Group 59, whose Cuba file class (837) covers political affairs, financial conditions and cane sugar for 1910 to 1949 (the catalogue entry and NARA identifier of the decimal file itself are not in the data the app ships); for 1958 to 1989, State lot files in Record Group 59, among them Records Relating to the Cuban Missile Crisis (entry A1 3053, NARA identifier 27022950, 1962–1963, partly restricted), the Secretary's and Under Secretary's Memorandums of Conversation (entry A1 1566, identifier 602231, 1953–1964, unrestricted), the Bureau of Inter-American Affairs Subject Files kept for the Special Assistant on Communism (entry A1 3148, identifier 2363836, 1958–1961, partly restricted) and the Operation Mongoose files of the Coordinator of Cuban Affairs (entry UD-UP 293-B, identifier 648748, 1963–1965, unrestricted), together with the presidential libraries (Kennedy and Johnson national security files, Carter national security affairs, Nixon NSC files, Eisenhower's Whitman File) and CIA files. *Channel two, what the editors' footnotes cite without printing* (621 of the same 4,100 documents carry at least one such citation): Department of State central-file classes, the lot of Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted; NARA also assigns the lot a second series, Subject Files, entry A1 1586F), the Missile Crisis, Special Assistant on Communism and Memorandums of Conversation lots already named, Records Relating to Sugar (entry UD-UP 215, identifier 27022874, inclusive dates 1955–1962 with coverage from 1932, fully restricted), and the Carter, Kennedy, Johnson and Nixon files. For 1870 to 1899 neither channel says anything: the index records a source for 1 of 309 documents, and the footnote channel has no entry before December 1910.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS** — Four parts of the question lie outside what these data hold. (a) *How much Cuba actually contributed to American prosperity or endangered American security*: FRUS shows what officials said, not the trade and investment measures behind it; the data I used do not index the Commerce or Treasury trade statistics that would show it, so I name no shelf for them. (b) *The nineteenth-century record behind the despatches*: for 1870 to 1899 the printed documents have almost no recorded sources and no footnote citations, so the shelf has to be inferred from the National Archives' country series for the State Department, *Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Spain, 1792–1906* (file-unit identifier 177380726, series 603720), *Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Cuba, 1902–1906* (177380758), and the notes from the Spanish and Cuban legations (183303928, 183303950); their access status is not in the bundled data. (c) *The years after 1988*: the 177 indexed documents dated in the 1990s include none that names Cuba, so the series as indexed ends where the question's present begins; a test search for a nonsense word returned nothing and a search for “Department of State” returned documents in every one of the 553 volumes, so the absence is not a failure of the search. (d) *Documents about Havana that never use the word Cuba*, 1,137 of them across four periods, and the phrase “national security” itself: before 1947 only 10 documents pair it with Cuba, and in the short stretches of text around the phrase that I examined it appears in the name of a statute, a Cuban declaration or unrelated business; after 1946 the phrase is mostly the name of the National Security Council (10 of 40 sampled documents use it in any other sense), so I read the older and the newer language by a wider vocabulary of defence, strategic position, menace, rivals and bases. The volumes print the executive branch's side of the correspondence; congressional debate, press opinion and Cuban government archives are other collections.

**Scope** — The 553 volumes indexed, dated 1870 to 1988 by each document's own date, apart from the two second editions that duplicate first editions, and leaving out front matter and editors' notes: about 12,300 documents name Cuba, and about 4,100 name it five or more times. The 22 documents in the collection are those on which the argument rests and nothing else.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What *Cuba's impact* covers.** I counted documents that name Cuba; if you also want Caribbean and hemispheric policy that never names the island, the set grows and the 1900–1939 findings gain weight; I used the narrow rule meanwhile.
- **What counts as national security before 1947.** I read defence, strategic position, rival powers, bases and “menace” as the older language; if only the phrase counts, there is almost no evidence before the Second World War (10 documents).
- **What counts as prosperity.** I took both American commerce and investment and Cuban economic stability as an American interest (the 1952 position paper treats the second as the first); if you want only American gain, the 1940s and 1950s findings thin out.
- **Who counts as a policymaker.** I took every author in the printed record, from presidents to chargés; if you want only cabinet-level voices, the nineteenth-century findings rest on Fish and the war message alone.
- **Whether 1959–1960 is a break or a re-use of old levers.** The sugar quota and tariff were named as leverage in 1948 and used in 1960; calling this a continuity of instruments and not a rupture of thought changes the headline of Finding 4.

The inclusion rule used meanwhile: a document that names Cuba, Cuban or Cubans five or more times, dated 1870–1988, not front matter, not an editor's note, not a second edition whose first edition is also indexed.

## Findings

### 1. Before 1898: a rich neighbour, and the worry was who held it

Hamilton Fish's instruction of February 1874 is the clearest statement in the 1870s of why Cuba mattered, and it is a statement about proximity and trade. Cuba was the largest possession still held by a European power in America, nearly contiguous, a market, and the source of “perpetual solicitude”; what Fish wanted was to be delivered from the hazard of complications on the side of Spain or of Cuba, and the daily business was citizens' claims. By 1897 the argument was told in losses: the American minister in Madrid, explaining the American position to the German ambassador for transmission to Berlin, said that the country depended on Cuba for sugar, that American property there lay idle, and that the trade in sugar, tobacco and American goods had been destroyed, while disclaiming any wish to annex the island. In April 1898 the President's message gave four grounds, and the third and fourth were commerce and the nation's peace and expense.

> “It is almost contiguous to the United States.” — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, Document 552, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d552
>
> “our great commerce with Cuba has thus been practically destroyed” — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, Document 498, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/d498
>
> “The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace, and entails upon this Government an enormous expense.” — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, the President's message to Congress, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83

### 2. 1902 to 1934: security by exclusion, prosperity by preference

The terms the President sent to Congress in March 1902 forbade Cuba to let any foreign power gain a foothold, and the same message closed on legislation to stimulate commerce between the two countries. In August 1917 the State Department told the Cuban minister that Cuba's greatest aid in the war would be economic rather than military, and said it would hesitate to approve a loan unless the damaged Cuba Railway was restored, so that sugar and manganese could be made readily available. In 1933 the Secretary of State still told his new ambassador that policy was governed by those 1903 articles (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 241, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d241); the treaty of 1934 ended the right to intervene but kept the naval station at Guantánamo, and the trade agreement three months later called itself a continuation of the reciprocity of 1902 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 154, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d154).

> “nor in any manner authorize or permit any foreign power or powers to obtain, by colonization or for military or naval purposes or otherwise, lodgment in or control over any portion of said island” — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1902, Document 273, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1902/d273
>
> “Cuba’s greatest aid to the United States in the prosecution of the war would be economic rather than military” — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1918, Document 333, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1918/d333
>
> “the stipulations of that agreement with regard to the naval station of Guantánamo shall continue in effect” — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d170

### 3. 1941 to 1952: defence and sugar in one sentence

In the war and after it the two stakes stop being argued separately. The ambassador in Havana urged aid in 1941 for “economic and defense reasons”; a 1948 department memorandum, written before Castro, stated the leverage plainly, that by tariff or quota the United States could plunge Cuba into poverty; and the 1951 statement put the strategic position, the naval base and sugar into one policy, with hemispheric security the first objective. A 1952 position paper went further and called Cuban economic and political stability a United States interest in its own right (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 331, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d331).

> “our interest in the Cuban situation for economic and defense reasons is such” — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1941, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 134, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v07/d134
>
> “we could, by manipulation of tariff or quota affecting sugar, plunge the entire island into poverty” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 420, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d420
>
> “Cuba, lying 90 miles to the south of Key West, Florida, is of great strategic importance to the US both because of its location with respect to the Panama Canal and the sea lanes of the Caribbean and because in two wars it has been the major and most readily-expandible source of sugar” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 440, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d440

### 4. 1959 and 1960: the market becomes a weapon

When the Department judged Castro's government hostile, the instruments of prosperity became instruments of security policy. In September 1959 the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs listed the sugar quota among the “retaliatory instruments” likely to be used; at a special National Security Council meeting on 22 June 1960 the Treasury and State proposed cutting Cuba's preferential quota by a fifth to a quarter; and five days later the Assistant Secretary recorded that nearly a year earlier the Department and the CIA had decided to devise means to bring about Castro's overthrow (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 535, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d535). The Secretary of Defense then asked in July what further economic measures could follow, once “one Rubicon” had been crossed.

> “our retaliatory instruments, such as GATT and the sugar quota, are likely to be used” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 362, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d362
>
> “Amendment of the preferential sugar quota act to provide for a reduction from 20 to 25 per cent of Cuba’s quota.” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 532, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d532
>
> “Since we have crossed one Rubicon with our sugar and oil actions” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 567, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d567

### 5. October 1962: was it geography, or politics?

The two transcripts of 16 October 1962 judge the threat in different ways within a few hours. In the morning the Secretary of State called a missile base in Cuba “intolerable and not acceptable”; in the evening the President, arguing that it hardly matters whether a warhead comes from the Soviet Union or from ninety miles away, said that geography “doesn’t mean that much”, and the Secretary of Defense said he did not think there was a military problem at all, only a domestic, political one. That matters for the question: at the point of greatest physical danger, at least one senior voice weighed the danger as a political one. The transcripts are unscripted, and the second is long and partly unintelligible.

> “this kind of a base is intolerable and not acceptable” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 18, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d18
>
> “I don’t think there is a military problem here.” and “Geography doesn’t mean that much.” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d21

### 6. From 1969: a ranked list, then a test of Moscow's conduct

After the crisis the documents rank interests. A July 1969 interagency study put the exclusion of strategic weapons first and called improved relations only a “subsidiary and lesser” interest; in 1977 the State Department's list of what the United States wanted of Cuba was restraint abroad, against a Cuban wish for access to American markets; in 1979 the President told the nation that the Soviet brigade was no direct threat and no nuclear one; and a 1981 intelligence paper treated Cuba mainly as a sore spot in the Soviet-American relationship (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1981–1988, Volume III, Soviet Union, January 1981–January 1983, Document 10, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v03/d10). The share of documents that use defence or Soviet vocabulary close to a mention of Cuba rises from between one in five and one in three before 1958 to about four in five from 1958; the share that uses sugar, trade or investment vocabulary close to a mention of Cuba falls from seven in ten in 1940–1957 to one in two.

>
> “to gain access to U.S. markets and technology to stimulate its economy” — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 18, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d18

## How this was done

I searched the full-text index of 553 volumes for the word Cuba and its adjective, which the index treats as one family of words, and checked that the match was literal in 39 of 40 evenly spaced sample documents (all 40 when plurals are allowed); a nonsense word returned nothing and “Department of State” appeared in all 553 volumes. I excluded front matter, editors' notes and the two second editions, dated each document by its own date and not its volume's year, and counted how many documents per thousand in each decade named Cuba, from about 62 per thousand in the 1870s, to 17 in the 1940s, to 114 in the 1960s. I then scanned the 4,100 documents that name Cuba five or more times for security and prosperity words within about 250 characters of each mention, and read about 65 documents in full or in windows to choose the 22 on which the findings rest. The app's meaning-based layer, a numerical fingerprint of each document that lets a document be compared with every other without a search term, was used as a second finder: the fingerprints put the 1874 instruction nearest the Cuba despatches of 1897 and 1898, and put the 1951 statement next to a 1948 memorandum and the 1969 study; on the app's map, 2,982 of 6,124 documents naming Cuba from 1958 on sit in a single Cuba cluster, and none of the 1,240 from 1870 to 1899 do, so the map could not be used to follow the question across 1958. Every number here is a count of a named set in the library of 553 volumes; the exact counts, shares and queries are in the memo. I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is a passage the memo verified against its document, and that every document this overview cites is in the collection.
