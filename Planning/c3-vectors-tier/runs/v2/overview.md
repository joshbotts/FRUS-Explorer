# how have u.s. policymakers' thinking about cuba's impact on national security and prosperity changed since the 1870s?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The printed record shows the stakes moving, not just growing. In the 1870s–1890s Cuba mattered as a disordered neighbor whose wars damaged American commerce and drew the United States toward intervention: Hamilton Fish's 1870 report on trade with Spanish America (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1870, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1870/d197), James G. Blaine's 1881 instruction calling Cuba "the key to the Gulf of Mexico" (1881, Document 405, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d405), and President McKinley's war message of April 1898 (1898, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83). From 1901 to the 1930s security and prosperity were folded into one guardianship: a right to intervene for "a stable government," naval stations "for its own defense," and Cuban peace as the condition of Cuban prosperity (1902, Document 313; 1903, Document 331; 1906, Part I, Document 427). From the 1930s to the 1950s the hinge was sugar: Sumner Welles in 1933 and John Foster Dulles in 1954 tied the sugar quota at once to Cuban stability, American exports and "a strategic sugar reserve" (1933, Volume V, Document 281; 1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 343). After 1959 the frame became military and ideological — Cuba as a possible Soviet base and an exporter of revolution — and by 1977 a Treasury Secretary could write that the trade issue's political significance "far outweighs economic effects" (1961–1963, Volume X, Document 202; Volume XI, Document 34; 1969–1976, Volume E–10, Document 205; Volume XII, Document 211; 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Documents 21 and 98). These rest on a set of about seven thousand documents the editors filed under Cuba, from 130 volumes, 1849 to 1980.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from* (the editors' source notes): before 1950 overwhelmingly the State Department's Central Decimal File in Record Group 59, under the Cuba files for political affairs (837.00), financial conditions (837.51), sugar cane (837.61351) and U.S. trade (611.37…), with the nineteenth century in the microfilmed Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Spain, 1792–1906 (NARA file unit 177380726) and to Cuba, 1902–1906 (177380758) and the Notes from the Spanish Legation, 1790–1906 (183303928); after 1958 the presidential libraries' national security files (Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon, Carter), CIA records, and State Department lot files in RG 59 — Records Relating to the Cuban Missile Crisis (lot 65D438, entry A1 3053, NAID 27022950, 1962–1963, partly restricted), Records of Under Secretary George W. Ball (lot 74D272, A1 5175, NAID 614703, 1961–1966, unrestricted), Files Pertaining to Operation Mongoose (lot 66D501, UD-UP 293-B, NAID 648748, 1963–1965, unrestricted), the Inter-American bureau's Subject Files (lots 62D24, A1 3148, NAID 2363836, 1958–1961, and 62D418, A1 3147, NAID 2068466, 1959–1962, both partly restricted), and the Policy Planning Council's NSC records (lot 62D1, A1 1583E/F, NAID 2838992, 1948–1961, partly restricted). *What the editors cited in footnotes without printing*: chiefly decimal-file numbers (about 340 documents, 1913–1962), the same presidential-library files, Records Relating to NSC Policy Papers (lot 66D95, A1 1586E, NAID 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted), Presidential and Secretary of State Correspondence with Foreign Heads of State (lot 66D204, A1 1590, NAID 2538452, 1953–1964, possibly restricted) and Records Relating to Sugar (lot 65D212, UD-UP 215, NAID 27022874, 1955–1962, recorded as fully restricted). The two lists are kept apart because they answer different questions (see the memo).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Three things. First, anything after 1980: searching every volume for Cuba, Cuban, Havana or Habana after January 1981 finds a few hundred passing mentions in thirteen Reagan-era volumes but not one document with Cuba in its heading, and the series' coverage stops in 1991; the Reagan Library's national security files are the shelf, which the offline tools cannot resolve. Second, a direct measure of "national security" thinking: the phrase almost never occurs in these documents before the 1930s, and after 1959 it is mostly the name of an institution (the National Security Council) and appears no more often in the Cuba documents than in the corpus at large, so it was dropped as a test. Third, the commercial interests' own voice — planters, refiners, investors — reaches FRUS only through State Department correspondence; the nearest bundled shelf is the Department's Records Relating to Sugar, 1955–1962, and nothing bundled reaches the nineteenth-century trade. (Fish's best-known Ten Years' War instruction of November 1875 was also not found: the only Cuba document of that month is a different Fish instruction on the *Virginius* case.)

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series are in this library; the working set is the 7,013 documents the editors filed under a Cuba heading (plus, for 1861–1894, when Cuba sat under "Spain," documents naming Cuba at least three times), dated 1849–1980, with front matter, editorial notes and the two duplicate second editions excluded. Every count is about that set and that library.

## Decisions this needs from you
- **What counts as "a Cuba document."** I used the editors' filing (their Cuba chapters and volumes); switching to "any document that mentions Cuba" would roughly double the set and pull in Cold War documents where Cuba is one item among many, which would make the 1960s–80s look still more security-minded.
- **Whose thinking.** The set mixes presidents, secretaries, ambassadors, consuls and intelligence estimates; restricting to Washington-originated papers would sharpen "policymakers" but would drop most of the nineteenth-century evidence, which is diplomats' despatches.
- **Where "prosperity" lives.** It can mean American trade and investment, Cuba's own prosperity as a condition of order, or (after 1960) economic pressure as a weapon; I treated all three as the prosperity thread, and separating them would change the 1900s–1930s reading most.
- **The end date.** The question says "since the 1870s," but FRUS has no Cuba compilation after 1980; I stopped there rather than argue from passing mentions.

## Findings

### 1. 1870s–1898: a neighbor's disorder as a cost to commerce and a "menace to our peace"
In the earliest decades Cuba's documents speak of insurrection and intervention about three times as often as the corpus at large (about half the Cuba documents of the 1870s, against about one in seven), and of ships and naval matters far more often — the *Virginius* affair dominates. Commerce runs through every argument, framed by proximity and necessity rather than by danger. Blaine's 1881 instruction put the strategic and commercial images in one sentence; McKinley's 1898 message justified intervention by injury to trade and by the expense of permanent watchfulness.
- "With the Spanish islands of Cuba and Porto Rico we maintain, in spite of their adverse legislation, a large commerce by reason of our necessities and of their proximity." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1870, Document 197, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1870/d197
- "That rich island, the key to the Gulf of Mexico, and the field for our most extended trade in the Western Hemisphere is, though in the hands of Spain, a part of the American commercial system." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1881, Document 405, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1881/d405
- "The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace, and entails upon this Government an enormous expense." — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1898, President McKinley's message to Congress of April 11, 1898, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/ch83 (see also Secretary Sherman's instruction of July 1897, 1898, Document 490, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1898/d490)

### 2. 1901–1933: security and prosperity fused in a guardianship
After independence the two halves of the question were written into one instrument: the conditions Washington put to the Cuban convention reserved a right to intervene for "a stable government" that would protect property, and the 1903 lease of coaling and naval stations was justified both by Cuban independence and by American "defense." Theodore Roosevelt's 1906 letter, on the eve of the second intervention, made Cuban peace and prosperity the test of Cuban independence. In this band the editors' headings file about two documents in five under political order and intervention, and in the 1910s Cuba's documents talk about property and claims nearly twice as often as the corpus.
- "Cuba consents that the United States shall reserve and retain the right to intervene for the preservation of the independence of Cuba, and the maintenance of a stable government that will duly protect life, property, and individual liberty" — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1902, Document 313, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1902/d313 (quoted in the Cuban commission's report enclosed in the despatch)
- "To enable the United States to maintain the independence of Cuba, and to protect the people thereof, as well as for its own defense, the Cuban Government will sell or lease to the United States the lands necessary for coaling or naval stations" — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1903, Document 331, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1903/d331
- "This nation asks nothing of Cuba, save that it shall continue to develop as it has developed during these past seven years" — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1906, Part I, Document 427, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1906p1/d427

### 3. 1933–1958: sugar as the hinge of stability, exports and wartime supply
From the Good Neighbor years to the eve of the revolution the economic thread dominates: the editors' own headings put about two Cuba documents in three under economic subjects (sugar crops, trade agreements, credit), and in the 1940s over half the Cuba documents use trade-and-sugar vocabulary against about one in five in the corpus. Welles argued that a sugar settlement would steady Cuba and recover an export market; in 1941 the Department valued a defense agreement for its political effect more than for the base; in 1954 Dulles tied the sugar quota to Cuban stability, U.S. exports and Cuba's role as "a strategic sugar reserve." Strikingly, the Cuba documents of the 1950s use Cold War vocabulary less often than the corpus does.
- "We will likewise regain an exceedingly important market for our exports." — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 281, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d281
- "a cooperative defense agreement with the Cubans is nevertheless essential for its effect on United States relations with Cuba and the other American republics." — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1941, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1941v07/d119
- "It would result in corresponding reductions in the considerable United States exports to Cuba and would jeopardize the concept of Cuba as a strategic sugar reserve." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 343, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d343

### 4. 1959–1970: a Soviet base and an exporter of revolution
After Castro the vocabulary of the Soviet Union, communism and missiles appears in about two Cuba documents in three (against about one in two in the corpus), and naval and strategic terms in about two in five (against one in four). The May 1961 task force rated a strategic base only a "remote possibility"; eighteen months later Kennedy distinguished bombers he could live with from missiles that changed the balance; by 1969 the planners called Cuba a "double-barreled problem," and in 1970 the Joint Chiefs saw the Cienfuegos submarine facility as the gravest current challenge. Economic policy is still present, but as a weapon — the editors head one 1960 chapter "a policy to weaken the Cuban economy."
- "There is the remote possibility of an attempt to convert Cuba into a Russian base for strategic attack on the United States." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume X, Cuba, January 1961–September 1962, Document 202, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10/d202
- "In his view the existence of fifty planes in Cuba did not affect the balance of power, but the missiles already in Cuba were an entirely different matter." (the President, at the National Security Council, October 20, 1962) — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XI, Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v11/d34
- "Cuba has been a double-barreled problem for the U.S.: —as a base for Soviet military power, and —as an exporter of revolutionary doctrine and violence to Latin America." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–10, Documents on American Republics, 1969–1972, Document 205, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d205 (also: "The latest and perhaps the most serious challenge to US security interests is occurring in Cuba." — 1969–1976, Volume XII, Soviet Union, January 1969–October 1970, Document 211, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v12/d211)

### 5. 1977–1980: trade as leverage, migration as a domestic security problem
In the Carter documents the embargo is weighed for what it buys politically, not for what trade would earn; the prosperity thread has become a bargaining chip. In 1980 the Mariel boatlift brought Cuba's impact home as a problem of borders and federal coordination. The word "prosperity," distinctive in Cuba documents before 1959, is no more common in them than in the corpus afterward (thin counts; see the memo).
- "The political significance of both sides of the trade issue far outweighs economic effects." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 21, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d21
- "the influx of Cuban and Haitian refugees requires the highest degree of coordination of the Federal government’s domestic resources" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIII, Mexico, Cuba, and the Caribbean, Document 98, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d98

## How this was done
I read the editors' chapter headings across all 553 volumes to find where they file Cuba (under "Spain" until 1895, in a Cuba chapter of the annual volumes from 1902, in dedicated Cuba volumes from 1958), took every document in those sections plus, for 1861–1894, documents naming Cuba at least three times, and dated each by its own date rather than its volume's. For each decade I counted how many of these documents use six word families (security; strategic and naval; Soviet, communist and missile; trade and sugar; property and claims; insurrection, revolution and intervention) and compared each with the same count in a random sample of 300 documents from the same decade, so that a word that is simply getting commoner everywhere is not read as a change in thinking about Cuba; the question's own phrase "national security" failed that comparison and was dropped. These are counts of words in whole documents, editors' footnotes included, not of arguments. A scan of the original XML for every spelling showed "Habana" more common than "Havana," and the searches were corrected for it, with a control phrase found in 552 of 553 volumes and an impossible string found in none. The quotations were found by ranked full-text searches and one through the app's document-similarity vectors (the 1969 study was the near neighbor of the 1961 task force paper), and every quotation was checked word for word against its document. The archival series were resolved through the app's bundled finding aids. Every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was verified in the memo against its document, and every document cited here is in the collection; I checked both before finishing.
