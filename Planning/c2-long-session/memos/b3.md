# Scoping memo — fishing rights and territorial waters in FRUS

**To:** the historian
**Question:** How did the United States handle disputes over fishing rights and territorial waters with other governments?
**Status:** scoping pass. Nothing here is a research finding about US policy; it is an account of what the corpus holds, what to ask it, and what the two terms in your question do and do not measure.

---

## 1. Headline

**The corpus holds this question densely, continuously, and in two vocabularies that do not
overlap.** It is a first-rank subject of the annual volumes (1870s–1920s), it thins to a
background hum in the war-and-Cold-War decades, and it returns as a named standing chapter — *Law
of the Sea* — from 1952 to 1988.

**The trap is the phrase "territorial waters".** It is not a fisheries term. It is a term of art
that four unrelated dispute families share, and in this corpus it is barely more diagnostic of your
question than "high seas" is. Details in §4; the consequence is that you should scope on the
editors' own chapter formulas and on the State Department's own file numbers, not on the phrase.

## 2. What the corpus holds — the editors' own headings

guessing at phrasing, then hand-separated true hits from lookalikes.

**The single most useful thing in this memo:** *Law of the Sea* is a modern phrase. It appears as a
chapter heading first in the 1952–54 volume and never before. If you search the corpus for your
subject in your own vocabulary you will find the last forty years of it and miss the first ninety.
Conversely, *fur seal*, *Behring Sea*, *modus vivendi*, *inshore fisheries* and *Newfoundland
fishery question* are dead vocabulary after 1930.

## 3. What I measured — document counts and their shape

I built a union query over the vocabulary the headings taught me, deliberately **excluding**
`"high seas"` and `"freedom of the seas"` (see §4):

```sql
SELECT c.volume_id, c.document_id, substr(d.date_iso,1,4)
FROM frus_documents f JOIN document_cache c ON c.rowid=f.rowid
JOIN document_dates d ON d.volume_id=c.volume_id AND d.document_id=c.document_id
WHERE frus_documents MATCH 'body_text : ("territorial waters" OR "territorial sea" OR
  "three mile limit" OR "marine league" OR "law of the sea" OR "continental shelf" OR
  "fur seal" OR "pelagic sealing" OR "fishing vessels" OR "fishing vessel" OR "fishing rights" OR
  "fishery rights" OR "coastal fisheries" OR "inshore fisheries" OR "twelve mile limit" OR

  "fishery convention" OR "seal fisheries" OR "fishing boats" OR "fishing craft")'
```

## 4. The false-friend test, and why "territorial waters" fails it

Both terms in your question are supplied vocabulary, so both had to be priced against a baseline.
I split the corpus into the 86 heading-defined volumes (69,839 documents) and everything else
(236,780 documents) and measured each term's share on both sides.

| term | in on-topic volumes | in the rest | enrichment |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fisheries` | 923 of 69,839 (1.32%) | 964 of 236,780 (0.41%) | **3.25×** |
| `fishing rights` | 82 (0.117%) | 124 (0.052%) | 2.24× |
| `high seas` | 620 (0.888%) | 1,024 (0.432%) | 2.05× |
| `territorial waters` | 612 (0.876%) | 1,052 (0.444%) | 1.97× |
| *control:* `the government` | 18,073 (25.9%) | 50,334 (21.3%) | 1.22× |
| *control:* `department of state` | 14,491 (20.8%) | 78,927 (33.3%) | 0.62× |

The controls establish the baseline band: a term that is measuring the corpus rather than the
question lands between about 0.6× and 1.2×. `fisheries` clears it decisively. **`territorial
waters` sits at 1.97× — statistically above baseline, but no better than `high seas` at 2.05×,
which I had already set aside as a neutrality-and-blockade term.** A phrase that is exactly as
enriched as a term you rejected is not carrying your question.

**Where the phrase actually goes.** Its largest concentrations, by volume:

| volume | hits | what the volume's own chapter heading says it is about |
|---|---|---|
| `frus1923v01` | 62 | *"Prevention of the illegal importation of liquor into the United States"* — Prohibition rum-running and the twelve-mile hovering treaties |
| `frus1964-68v29p1` | 51 | *"Pueblo Crisis"* — a seized US intelligence ship off North Korea |
| `frus1949v09` | 36 | China: recognition, the Nationalist port closure and blockade |
| `frus1918Supp01v02` | 31 | wartime neutrality; Norway mining its own waters |
| `frus1914Supp`/`1915Supp`/`1916Supp` | 49 | belligerent warships "hovering" off neutral coasts |

**Four unrelated dispute families share the phrase: fisheries, wartime neutrality, Prohibition
smuggling, and Cold War naval incidents.** Only the first is your question. This is confirmed
independently at the archival level in §6: the second-largest decimal file in my hit set is
`811.114` (50 documents) — *United States, Traffic in liquors* — which is Prohibition, and
`POL 33-6 KOR N-US` (87 documents) is the Pueblo.

**Twenty-five chapter headings I rejected as lookalikes**, all matching a naive pattern: *Chinese
Maritime Customs* (repeated across `frus1931-41v01`, `frus1935v03`, `frus1937v03`, `frus1938v03`,
`frus1942China`, `frus1943China`), *Allied Maritime Transport Council*, *United States Maritime
Commission*, *Maritime Province* (Siberia), *Third International Conference on Maritime Law*
(salvage and collisions), *United Maritime Authority*, and — my favourite — *"Counterfeiting the
American Consular Seal"* (`frus1906p2`), which a `seal` search returns. The bundled authority file
adds two more: the collections named for *Adrian S. Fisher* and *Fisher Howe*.

**Recommendation:** do not scope on `territorial waters`. Scope on the heading formulas in §2 and
the file numbers in §6, then use `territorial waters` only inside that scope.

**Three results change how you should search.**

1. **The numeral form wins for the twelve-mile limit: 436 numeral against 144 spelled — 75% of the
   evidence.** A search for "twelve-mile" alone finds a quarter of it. For the three-mile limit the
   split runs the other way (529 spelled, 222 numeral, 30% numeral), so neither convention is safe
   on its own; count both, every time.
2. **The acronym outnumbers the phrase for the EEZ** (117 vs 87) and is a substantial supplement
   for the Law of the Sea (`LOS` 816 against `law of the sea` 2,258, and `UNCLOS` a further 88).
   The commission acronyms are effectively absent: `ICNAF` 7, `INPFC` 4, `IATTC` 1. If you want
   the commissions, search their spelled names.
3. **`LOS` carries a documented false positive and I checked it rather than assuming.** 5 of its 40
   volumes are pre-1950 (`frus1864p4`, `frus1868p2`, `frus1884`, `frus1886`, `frus1902app2`), and
   the hits there are Spanish: `CESAREO DE LOS SANTOS`, `POR LOS HERMANOS DE ORGA`. In the modern
   volumes the token is genuine and TEI-glossed (`<gloss target="#t_LOS1">LOS</gloss>`). Restrict
   the acronym to post-1950 volumes.

I did **not** separate document text from footnotes on either surface. The index's `body_text`
contains both, and my TEI scan is over the whole tag-stripped file, apparatus included. Every
density figure in this memo therefore blends the diplomats' language with the editors'.

## 7. What I would search for, in order

1. **Scope by chapter heading, not by phrase.** Take the 86 volumes and 136 headings in §2 as the
   spine. They are the editors' own repeating formulas and they cost nothing to reproduce.
2. **Then scope by file number.** `.628`, `.0145`, `.627`, `.0144` for 1910–49; `711.428` for the
   Canadian file; `399.731` for Geneva 1958; `POL 33-4` and `POL 33-6` for 1963 onward. These are
   the Department's own categories and they do not drift with wording.
3. **Only then use free text**, and use every variant: spelled *and* numeral mile-limits, singular
   *and* plural *territorial sea(s)*, and the acronyms restricted to post-1950 volumes.
4. **Treat `territorial waters` as a filter, never as a finder** (§4).
5. **For the nineteenth century, expect the corpus to be a selection** and plan on the
   chronological despatch series (§6) for the remainder.

## 8. What I did not do, and what would be wrong to conclude from this memo

- **I have not read the documents.** Nothing here says how the United States *handled* anything.
  The title of this memo is a scoping question; the historical question is untouched.
- **I did not separate body text from footnotes** on either surface (§5). Every density figure
  blends the diplomats with the editors, and the editorial apparatus grows steeply after 1945 —
  which is exactly the period where the density curve turns back upward. Some part of the 1950s–70s
  rise in §3 may be annotation rather than documents. That is a TEI question I did not answer.
- **The heading scope under-captures the nineteenth century.** Only 1,434 of my 3,567 hit documents
  fall inside the 86 heading-defined volumes; 2,133 fall outside, and the outside set is real
  material — `frus1887` (30), `frus1890` (26), `frus1891` (54) all carry Bering Sea and fisheries
  correspondence filed under a *country* heading rather than a topical one, which my two-level
  structure walk did not reach. The heading list is a high-precision spine, not a complete scope.
- **Volume-scope archival ranking does not work for this question, and I checked.** Running the 86
  on-topic volumes through `collection-usage-index.json` returns 740.00119 (WWII), 893.00 (China),
  893.51, 320, 811.20 — the archival profile of big annual volumes that happen to contain a
  fisheries chapter, not of the chapter. **Use the document-grain `document_sources` route in §6A,
  never the volume rollup.** (54,871 source notes sit in those 86 volumes; a few hundred are yours.)
- **NARA's creator attribution is not verified here.** I report titles and NAIDs from the bundle; I
  did not check a single creator heading, and the house rule that creator attribution has been
  measured at 56% precision applies to every row in §6.
- **Person coverage is no help on this subject.** The tagged-person tables reach only 287 of 552
  volumes and 88% of their tags sit in 1950-or-later books — so Bayard, Phelps, Blaine, Root and
  Elihu Root's Hague counsel are effectively invisible to that route.
- Figures are conditional on this index (552 volumes, complete at the volume level), with front
  matter, editorial notes and two duplicate second editions excluded.
