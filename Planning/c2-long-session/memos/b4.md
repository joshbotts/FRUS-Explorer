# Scoping memo — The Department of State and international narcotics control before 1970

**To:** the historian
**Re:** what the FRUS Explorer corpus (552 volumes, 316,839 indexed documents) can and cannot
support on this question
**Status:** scoping pass, not research. Every number below is reproducible from the queries in §7.

---

## 1. Bottom line

The corpus is **good** for this question — better than I expected — but the material is
concentrated at both ends of the period and nearly absent in the middle, and it is easy to
retrieve wrongly.

Three findings you should have before you decide whether to commit:

1. **FRUS printed dedicated narcotics compilations from 1906 to 1963.** Forty-six sections
   across 35 volumes carry a chapter heading naming opium or narcotics, holding **487 pre-1970
   documents** (455 once one false heading is removed — §6). This is not a topic you have to reconstruct from scattered mentions; the editors
   treated it as a standing subject head for six decades.
2. **The density is strongly front-loaded.** Of 296 pre-1970 documents that discuss drugs
   substantively (five or more drug-word occurrences), 187 fall in 1900–1939. The 1950s hold
   **five**. The chronology of the printed record does not track the chronology of the problem.
3. **The institutional answer is visible in the sources themselves, and it reverses.** In 1908
   the Secretary of State personally convened the Shanghai Opium Commission. By 1961 State's role
   was to instruct a Treasury official (Anslinger) who was negotiating for the United States,
   and to sit on an interdepartmental committee chaired by Treasury at the White House. No
   dedicated State narcotics office appears anywhere in the corpus's source notes until an
   S/NM officer is named on a memorandum of 22 December 1969 — the last month of your period.

## 2. What I searched for, and why

I approached this on three independent handles so that no single retrieval failure could hide
the material.

**(a) Vocabulary.** `narcotic*`, `opium`, `opiate`, `heroin`, `morphine`, `cocaine`, `coca`,
`marihuana`/`marijuana`, `cannabis`, `hashish`, `poppy`, plus the period's own euphemisms —
`habit-forming drugs`, `dangerous drugs`, `illicit traffic`. The FTS5 index uses a Porter
stemmer, so `narcotic` retrieves `narcotics` and `drug` retrieves `drugs` — but it does not
fold `opium`/`opiate`, so both are needed. Raw counts across the whole corpus: opium 1,060
documents, narcotic\* 871, drug\* 1,131, heroin 192, cocaine 106, morphine 102.

**(b) Compilation structure.** FRUS is organised into named chapters, and `volume_structures`
stores that outline as JSON with the document ids under each heading. Searching chapter *titles*
finds what the editors themselves thought was a narcotics compilation — a far higher-precision
signal than searching text.

**(c) Archival filing.** Source notes carry the Department's own file numbers. This turns out to
be the sharpest instrument available, because the Department had a classification for this
subject. From the Department's published 1910–1949 schedule (bundled as
`decimal-class-labels.json`, sourced to *RG 59 Department of State Classification of
Correspondence, August 1910 – December 1949*): **class 8 = "Internal Affairs of States",
subject suffix `.114` = "Traffic in liquors and habit-forming drugs."** So `893.114` is
China–narcotics, `891.114` is Iran–narcotics, `890.114` is Southeast Asia. Class 5 is
"Congresses and Conferences", and **`511.4*`** is the U.S. file on the international opium
conferences and the League committee. Two further homes: **`500.C1197`** (the League's Advisory
Committee on Traffic in Opium) and, after the 1950 renumbering, **`341.9`**. After the 1963 move
to subject-numeric filing the topic sits under **`SOC 11-5`** and then **`INCO-DRUGS`**.

**(d) The bundled subject index.** There is a *Narcotics* subject group of eight terms, and it
works — but I nearly reported the opposite because of a scripting error on my side. See §6(3);
it matters for how much weight you put on any negative result I report.

Two things follow. First, before 1969 the Department had a *subject class* for narcotics but no
narcotics *office*. The earliest appearance of one in this corpus is a clearance line — "Harry
Schwartz (**S/NM**)" — on an NSC memorandum of **22 December 1969**; the earliest citation to an
S/NM *file series* is **1972**, and to a lot file under that name **1976**. Treasury's own
records (RG 170) are cited four times in the whole corpus, the earliest in 1973. Second, the
1967–69 filing of drug traffic under `INCO` (International Commerce) is itself evidence about how
the Department understood the problem on the eve of the Nixon initiative.

### 3.4 Four documents that anchor the arc

I read these; they are the shortest route to seeing the shape of the answer.

- **`frus1908/d72`** — Secretary Root to Ambassador Reid, 7 May 1908, repeated to Paris, Berlin,
  The Hague, Tokyo, Peking and Lisbon. Root names Shanghai and 1 January 1909 for the commission,
  says the President will appoint up to three commissioners, and states the object: "to devise
  means to limit the use of opium in the possessions of that country" and to suppress traffic
  among its nationals. This is State as convener and author of the international regime.
- **`frus1934v01/d599–d601`** — the Secretary to League Secretary-General Avenol on the draft
  Convention for the Suppression of the Illicit Traffic in Dangerous Drugs, and a follow-up
  telegram to Geneva ("For Fuller") reasoning about the Sixth Amendment and the impracticality of
  transporting foreign witnesses to a U.S. grand jury. State as the brake, on legal-constitutional
  grounds.
- **`frus1944v02/d905`** — Hull's press statement of 3 July 1944 on H.J. Res. 241, declaring the
  resolution "in line with the long-standing opium policy of the United States" and calling for
  production limited to medical and scientific need. Alongside it in the same compilation: Hull
  to Stimson, a memorandum by the Chief of the Division of Far Eastern Affairs, and exchanges
  with the Netherlands, British and Chinese embassies on abolishing opium monopolies in the
  reoccupied colonies. State using the war to force a policy on allied colonial powers.
- **`frus1961-63v25/d327`** — Mulliken (IO/OES) to Gardner, 10 April 1961, minuting the
  Interdepartmental Committee on Narcotics at the White House: chaired by Assistant Secretary of
  the Treasury Flues, with HEW, Defense, Justice, the White House staff, and the Bureaus of
  Narcotics and Customs; State represented by IO, L/T and OES; Anslinger briefing on the Single
  Convention he had just negotiated. State as one voice among seven, supplying legal and
  UN-machinery expertise.

Note that the 1961–63 compilation is filed not under a region or a global issue but under
**Organization and Administration of Foreign Policy** — the editors' own judgement that by then
narcotics was a question about *who in Washington handles this*.

## 4. The arc the evidence supports

I would frame the pre-1970 story in four phases, all of which this corpus can document:

1. **1878–1905, consular and treaty work.** Opium appears in despatches from Peking (Seward,
   Angell) and Tokyo (Bingham) to Evarts and Blaine, around the 1880 commercial treaty with China
   and Japanese regulation. 21 substantive documents in the 1880s. Not yet a policy; a treaty
   clause and a consular nuisance.
2. **1906–1919, State invents the international regime.** The Shanghai Commission and the three
   Hague conferences are the densest run in the whole pre-1970 record (242 compilation documents
   in twelve volumes). The Department convenes, sets the date and place, appoints commissioners,
   seeks the appropriation, and circulates the powers. This is the strongest claim the corpus can
   support and the best-documented.
3. **1923–1939, League machinery and American ambivalence.** Delegation reporting from the Opium
   Advisory Committee, the 1931 Geneva Limitation Convention and Bangkok, sustained pressure on
   Persia and on Japanese-occupied China — but also, in 1934, the Department declining a draft
   convention on constitutional and practical grounds. A story about a non-member of the League
   working through the League's committees.
4. **1944–1963, hand-off.** State's 1944 wartime initiative against colonial opium monopolies is
   the last time it clearly leads. Thereafter the record thins to almost nothing in the 1950s and
   resumes in 1961 with State servicing a Treasury-led negotiation. The transfer of the lead is
   the finding — and it is visible in the filing, the compilation placement, and the attendance
   list of one meeting.

## 5. Where the corpus will fail you

- **The 1950s are nearly empty, and there is no dedicated compilation between 1947 and 1961.**
  Five substantive documents (≥5 drug words) across the entire decade, 24 with two or more,
  against 51 and 113 in the 1930s. This is *not* an artefact of volume availability: the corpus
  holds 108 volumes covering the 1950s. It is editorial selection.
  What survives is scattered and comes through a different channel — for example
  `frus1955-57v27/d81`, Dulles to Eisenhower, 5 July 1955, on the "Italian Narcotics Situation"
  and Ambassador Luce's alarm, sourced not to the central files but to the **Eisenhower Library,
  Whitman File**. Expect the 1950s evidence to be in presidential libraries and personal files
  rather than in RG 59 decimal classes, and search accordingly.
  I do *not* claim the 1953 Opium Protocol or the founding of the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs
  are unprinted — both are named in documents here (the Commission in `frus1945v01`, `frus1945v02`,
  `frus1946v01` and `frus1947v08`; the Protocol in the 1955 Dulles memorandum and repeatedly in
  the 1961–63 compilation). What is missing is a compilation *about* them. Establishing whether
  they were deliberately omitted or printed under a heading my filters missed is item 2 in §8.
- **1964–1968 is eight documents.** I listed them: three on Iran (1964), one Far East action
  memorandum (1965), two on Laos (1966), one from Treasury (1966), one NSC paper (1968). Nothing
  with a narcotics chapter heading exists for those years at all.
- **The Federal Bureau of Narcotics is barely present.** "Anslinger" appears in 18 documents
  corpus-wide. RG 170 (the Bureau's own records) is cited four times, the earliest in 1973. If your question is
  really about the *U.S.* narcotics apparatus rather than about *State*, FRUS is the wrong
  archive and you would want RG 170 directly.
- **Domestic enforcement is out of frame by design.** FRUS prints foreign-relations documents.
  Harrison Act administration, Prohibition-era enforcement, and the Bureau's domestic work appear
  only where a foreign government is on the other end.

## 6. Four traps I hit, so you don't

**(1) `811.114` is the liquor file, not the drug file.** The `.114` suffix means "Traffic in
liquors *and* habit-forming drugs", and for the United States the file is overwhelmingly
Prohibition. Measured: **269 documents cite `811.114`; 147 mention liquor; 8 mention any drug.**
The country-arranged `.114` files for China, Iran and Southeast Asia are the reverse (44 of 46
`893.114` documents mention drugs). Filter `.114` by country, or check the text.

**(2) One compilation heading is false.** `frus1952-54v08` has a chapter titled "UNITED STATES
RELATIONS WITH TURKEY: … AND THE PROBLEM OF ILLICIT TRAFFIC IN NARCOTICS". It contains 32
documents. **Not one of them contains the word narcotics, opium, drug, poppy, traffic or
smuggling.** It is a composite heading whose narcotics strand was not printed. Excluding it, the
compilation set is 455 documents of which 425 (93%) name a drug. If you build a corpus from
chapter titles, verify the contents.

**(3) I got the subject taxonomy wrong on the first pass, and the way I got it wrong is worth
recording.** The bundled `document-subject-index.json` vocabulary is a list of *objects*, not
strings; my first filter tested each entry as a string, silently matched nothing, and I was about
to report "this corpus has no narcotics subject." It does. There is a **Narcotics** group of eight
subjects, with these corpus-wide document frequencies — Opium 1,016, Heroin 175, Narcotics
control 168, Cocaine 100, Morphine 99, Narcotics traffic 69, Drug industry 8, and Narcotics
itself 631 — and it is a fourth usable handle. Restricted to pre-1970 dates the eight together
return **1,016 distinct documents** (coincidentally the same figure as Opium's corpus-wide
frequency), against my text search's 1,089.
The two agree closely for a reason the artifact discloses in its own provenance: the subjects were
assigned by "case-insensitive string matching of subject names and variants, NOT semantic
analysis … treat as recall-oriented candidates rather than ground truth." So it corroborates the
text search rather than supplying independent evidence — but it is a much faster way to pull the
set, and its per-decade profile (159 in the 1900s, 163 in the 1930s, 68 in the 1950s, 64 in the
1960s) matches §3.1 closely enough to trust either.

**(4) The person index will not help you here.** The corpus's person tables cover only volumes
published from about 1983 onward. Hamilton Wright and Stuart J. Fuller — State's two central
narcotics officials of the 1910s and 1930s — have no entry at all. Anslinger has one, recorded as
"Anslinger, **Harvey** J.", with 6 mentions in 1 volume, against 18 documents that name him in
full text. Search names as text, not through the person tables.

**Two smaller ones.** `Ibid.`-style source notes and pre-1910 documents print their file number
in the body (`File No. 774/197A`, `500.C1197/618`) rather than in a separate source note, so
class-based queries under-count the early material. And `document_dates` buckets a span-dated
document by its start date, so a chapter straddling a year boundary can land a year early.

## 7. Reproducing everything above

Open read-only: `sqlite3 -readonly /Users/jbotts/frus-analysis/frus-copy.db`
(the `-uri` flag this machine's sqlite3 does not accept; `-readonly` does the same job).

```sql
-- 3.1  graded drug-word counts by decade (regex grading done in Python; SQL gives the ≥1 set)
WITH hits AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT volume_id, document_id FROM frus_documents
  WHERE frus_documents MATCH
    'body_text:(narcotic OR opium OR opiate OR heroin OR morphine OR cocaine OR marihuana OR marijuana OR cannabis OR hashish OR poppy)')
SELECT (CAST(substr(d.date_iso,1,4) AS INT)/10)*10 AS decade, COUNT(*)
FROM hits h JOIN document_dates d USING(volume_id,document_id)
JOIN document_cache c USING(volume_id,document_id)
WHERE c.is_front_matter=0 AND d.date_iso < '1970-01-01'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;

-- 3.2  the compilations: chapter headings naming the subject
SELECT volume_id, structure_json FROM volume_structures
WHERE structure_json LIKE '%arcotic%' OR structure_json LIKE '%pium%'
   OR structure_json LIKE '%rug%';
-- then walk sections[].subsections[] recursively for a title matching
--   /narcotic|opium|heroin|marihuana|cannabis|\bdrugs?\b/i and take its documentIds

-- 3.3  archival footprint
SELECT s.decimal_class, COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT s.volume_id),
       MIN(substr(d.date_iso,1,4)), MAX(substr(d.date_iso,1,4))
FROM document_sources s LEFT JOIN document_dates d USING(volume_id,document_id)
WHERE s.decimal_class LIKE '511.4%' OR s.decimal_class LIKE '341.9%'
   OR s.decimal_class LIKE '500.C1197%' OR s.decimal_class LIKE '%.114%'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 2 DESC;

-- 6(1)  the liquor trap
SELECT SUM(lower(c.body_text) LIKE '%liquor%')   AS liquor,
       SUM(lower(c.body_text) LIKE '%narcotic%'
        OR lower(c.body_text) LIKE '%opium%')    AS drugs,
       COUNT(*)                                  AS total
FROM document_sources s JOIN document_cache c USING(volume_id,document_id)
WHERE s.decimal_class LIKE '811.114%';           -- liquor 147, drugs 8, total 269

-- 6(2)  the false Turkey chapter
SELECT COUNT(*),
       SUM(lower(body_text) LIKE '%narcotic%' OR lower(body_text) LIKE '%opium%'
        OR lower(body_text) LIKE '%drug%'     OR lower(body_text) LIKE '%poppy%')
FROM document_cache WHERE volume_id='frus1952-54v08'
  AND CAST(replace(document_id,'d','') AS INT) BETWEEN 459 AND 490;   -- 32 / 0

-- 3.1 cross-check: the bundled Narcotics subject group (vocab indices 107,114,264,321,364,395,415,481
--     -- read them from document-subject-index.json's vocab[], matching t['s']=='Narcotics')
SELECT (CAST(substr(dd.date_iso,1,4) AS INT)/10)*10 AS decade,
       COUNT(DISTINCT r.volume_id||'/'||r.document_id)
FROM document_subject_refs r JOIN document_dates dd USING(volume_id,document_id)
JOIN document_cache c USING(volume_id,document_id)
WHERE r.subject IN (107,114,264,321,364,395,415,481)
  AND c.is_front_matter=0 AND dd.date_iso < '1970-01-01'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;                            -- 1,016 documents pre-1970
```

Working files I left in this directory: `narc_sections.tsv` (every narcotics-titled section with
its document ids), `peryear.csv`, `docs_by_vol.csv`.

## 8. What I would do next, in order

1. **Read the 296-document substantive set end to end.** It is small enough to do properly and it
   is the whole evidentiary base. Start with the 143 Shanghai Commission documents (frus1908–1911)
   and the 57 China/Manchuria documents (frus1934v03–1939v04).
2. **Settle the 1950s absence.** Establish whether the 1953 Opium Protocol and the early UN
   Commission on Narcotic Drugs are genuinely unprinted, or printed under headings my filters
   missed. A negative finding here is publishable in its own right, and it is cheap to establish:
   the 1952–54, 1955–57 and 1958–60 subseries are 80 volumes and every one of their chapter
   outlines is in `volume_structures`. Pair that with a sweep of the presidential-library
   sources — `document_sources.repository` — since the one 1950s document I found came from the
   Eisenhower Library's Whitman File rather than from RG 59.
3. **Follow the file numbers out of FRUS.** `511.4*`, `500.C1197`, `893.114`, `341.9`, then
   `SOC 11-5` and `INCO-DRUGS` are live NARA search keys for RG 59. The printed record is the
   index; the file series is the archive.
4. **Decide whether the question is about State or about the United States.** If the latter, RG
   170 and Anslinger's own papers are the centre of gravity and FRUS is a supporting source. If
   the former — and the corpus is unusually well suited to it — the thesis is available and
   documented: *State built the international narcotics regime between 1906 and 1919, worked it
   through League machinery with reservations until 1939, made one last attempt to lead in 1944,
   and by 1961 had become the diplomatic service of somebody else's policy.*
