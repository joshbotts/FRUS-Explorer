# Scoping memo — State and international narcotics control before 1970

**To:** the historian
**Re:** what the FRUS Explorer corpus holds on the Department of State's role in international
narcotics control before 1970
**Status:** scoping pass. Everything below is measured against the local index and the bundled
reference data; nothing is drawn from secondary reading. Queries are given so you can re-run them.

---

## 1. Bottom line

The corpus is unusually good for this question, and better than the raw keyword counts suggest,
because the FRUS editors themselves treated narcotics as a named topic and built **47 dedicated
compilation sections** for it between 1906 and 1963. Those sections hold **498 documents**. Around
them sits a wider penumbra of **1,082 pre-1970 documents across 210 volumes** that mention opium
or a narcotic by name.

The material is not evenly spread. It falls into five clearly separated phases, with a **near-total
gap between 1948 and 1960** and an **institutional watershed at 1969**. The corpus will support a
well-evidenced narrative of what State did — convene, negotiate, instruct, and report — and it will
document, in the Department's own words, that State understood itself as the *originator* of the
international control regime. It will not support claims about enforcement, about what Treasury's
narcotics bureau actually did, or about the 1950s.

---

## 2. What I searched for, and what I actually measured

I used three independent routes and then checked whether they agree. They do, which is the main
reason I trust the shape below.

**Route A — full text.** FTS5 over `body_text`. Note that the index uses a Porter stemmer, so
`narcotic` and `narcotics` are the same query (871 documents), as are `drug`/`drugs` (1,131) and
`opium`/`opiums` (1,060). Corpus-wide term frequencies from `frus_documents_vocab`:
`opium` 1,060 docs / 6,876 occurrences; `narcot*` 871 / 3,397; `heroin` 192; `cocain*` 106;
`morphin*` 102; `opiat*` 27.

Union of `opium OR narcotic OR cocaine OR heroin OR morphine OR cannabis OR marihuana OR
marijuana`, **restricted to documents dated before 1970: 1,082 documents in 210 volumes.**

**Route B — the editors' own subject taxonomy.** The bundled `document-subject-index.json` carries
a curated *Narcotics* family under "Global Issues": Opium (1,016 docs corpus-wide), Heroin (175),
Narcotics control (168), Cocaine (100), Morphine (99), Narcotics (631), Narcotics traffic (69),
Drug industry (8). These are subject ids 107, 114, 264, 321, 364, 395, 415, 481 in
`document_subject_refs`, and the table's document-frequencies match the JSON exactly.
**Pre-1970: 1,016 documents.**

**Route C — volume structure.** I walked every volume's `structure_json` looking for compilation
headings that name the topic. **47 sections, 498 documents, all of them covering years before
1970.** Widening the pattern from `opium|narcotic|dangerous drug|habit-forming` to include a bare
`drug` adds 52 sections / 704 documents, but the difference is entirely accounted for: 2 sections
are Nixon-era (below) and 3 are false positives I checked by hand (§6).

**Convergence.** Pre-1970, Routes A and B agree on 982 documents; A finds 100 that B does not, B
finds 34 that A does not. 434 of the 498 compilation documents also contain a keyword — the other
64 are covering notes and enclosures inside a narcotics compilation that never use the word. So a
reasonable working universe is **~1,100 pre-1970 documents, with a well-defined core of ~500.**

## 3. The shape of the evidence: five phases

Documents inside the 47 dedicated compilations, by decade of document date:

| decade | docs in dedicated narcotics compilations |
|---|---|
| 1900s | 136 |
| 1910s | 107 |
| 1920s | 70 |
| 1930s | 107 |
| 1940s | 26 |
| 1950s | 32 |
| 1960s | 20 |

Keyword hits per year across 1944–1969 show the trough plainly: 1944 = 28, then 6–15 a year to
1953, then **1954 = 3, 1955 = 6, 1956 = 8, 1957 = 6, 1958 = 0, 1959 = 2, 1960 = 1**, then 1961 = 15
(the Single Convention), then back to 1–8 a year until **1969 = 28**.

### Phase 1 — treaty enforcement, c. 1880–1905
No dedicated compilation, but real material scattered through the annual volumes. The recurring
subject is the **1880 treaty with China (Article II)** and the **Act of 23 February 1887**, which
between them barred American citizens from the China opium trade, plus consular questions about
what that prohibition covered (e.g. Denby to Bayard, 1887, on whether *storage* of opium by an
American firm was forbidden), Chinese lekin and import duties on opium, and opium smuggling in the
Pacific. The 1894 Hawaii appendix (`frus1894app2`) carries 40 subject-tagged documents about the
Honolulu opium-licensing scandal — relevant to the politics, not to State's diplomacy.

### Phase 2 — State convenes the regime, 1906–1915
This is the strongest and best-organised body of evidence in the corpus, and it is the heart of the
answer. Nine compilation sections across `frus1906p1`, `frus1907p1`, `frus1908`, `frus1909`,
`frus1910`, `frus1911`, `frus1912`, `frus1913`, `frus1914`, `frus1915` — 13 + 37 + 58 + 27 + 54 +
4 + 7 + 6 + 15 + 7 documents plus the circulars.

The origin is documented step by step. In July 1906 the Acting Secretary instructs Minister Rockhill
in Peking to report on the opium trade; in September 1906 he forwards **Bishop Brent's letter to the
President** to Ambassador Reid in London, arguing that the acquisition of the Philippines had made
the United States an interested party; by January 1907 the Secretary reports that Britain and Japan
have responded favourably and that instructions have gone to France, Germany and the Netherlands
asking whether they would join a conference. The result is the Shanghai Opium Commission (1909) and
then the Hague conferences of 1912, 1913 and 1914 with their convention and protocols.

The Department's own later summary of this is worth having: in a June 1943 letter to Secretary of
War Stimson, the Secretary of State dates the Department's role from the moment "the Government of
the United States took the initiative in bringing about the first international conference on the
subject of narcotic drugs" at Shanghai in 1909 (`frus1944v02/d895`).

**Role in this phase: initiator and convener.** State drafted the invitations, ran the soundings,
and appointed and instructed the delegations.

### Phase 3 — participation in a League regime the United States had not joined, 1923–1936
Eleven compilation sections: 1923 (Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium, 14 docs), 1924 (Second
Opium Conference, 26), 1924/1925 (Persian Gulf opium exports with Britain, 6 + 10), 1925 (Canada
extradition), 1926, 1928, 1929, 1931 (**Limitation Convention, 30 docs**, and the Bangkok Conference
on the Suppression of Opium Smoking, 8), 1932, 1934 (a compilation whose own title records the
United States' *disinclination* to join a draft illicit-traffic convention), 1935, 1936.

The 1923 instruction to the American delegation (Congressman Stephen G. Porter, Bishop Charles H.
Brent, Dr Rupert Blue) is explicit that the United States attends "in a consultative capacity"
because it is party to the Hague Convention of 1912, not because it is a League member. This is the
period's distinctive problem and the corpus documents it directly.

Running in parallel from 1934 is a China/Far East series — "Problem of controlling the traffic in
opium and narcotic drugs in China, including Manchuria and Jehol" — which recurs in 1934, 1935,
1936, 1937, 1938 and 1939, and by 1938–39 is filed under the *Undeclared War Between Japan and
China*. Narcotics here is an instrument of the Japan question, not a public-health question.

### Phase 4 — war and immediate post-war, 1943–1947
Three sections (`frus1944v02` comp21, 20 docs; `frus1944v05`; `frus1945v02` comp23, 3 docs) on the
United States' initiative to get the colonial powers to suppress opium smoking in territories
liberated from Japan; plus `frus1947v08` ch62 on Mexican–US illicit traffic (3 docs). Small, but
coherent, and the 1943 Hull-to-Stimson letter is the pivot document.

### Phase 5 — the UN period, 1961–1963
One compilation, `frus1961-63v25` ch9, titled simply **"Narcotics"** (20 docs) inside the
*Organization and Administration of Foreign Policy* volume — which is itself a statement about where
the editors thought the subject belonged. Its contents are the negotiation of the **1961 Single
Convention on Narcotic Drugs**, US delegation reports to the 16th, 17th and 18th sessions of the UN
Commission on Narcotic Drugs, and reporting from the embassies in Turkey, Greece and the UK.

The single most useful document for your question is `frus1961-63v25/d326`: **Secretary Rusk's
instructions to the U.S. Representative to the Single Convention conference — and the Representative
is Harry J. Anslinger, Commissioner of Narcotics in the *Treasury* Department.** The source note
records that the instructions were drafted by an officer in IO/OIC and cleared by the Assistant
Secretary of State for International Organization Affairs. That is the division of labour in one
document: substantive expertise and the delegation chair sit in Treasury; the instruction, the
clearance and the diplomatic channel sit in State, inside the international-organizations bureau
rather than in any narcotics office.

### The watershed at 1969 — and why "before 1970" is the right cut
There is no narcotics office in State before 1969. The evidence is in the volumes' own abbreviation
lists (`terms` table): **`S/NM` — "Office of the Coordinator for International Narcotics Matters" /
"Special Assistant to the Secretary of State for Narcotics Matters" — appears only in 1969–76
volumes.** So do `BNDD`, `CCINC` (Cabinet Committee on International Narcotics Control), `INCB` and
`INCP`. Before 1969 the Department handles narcotics through whichever geographic or functional
bureau owns the file; after 1969 it has a named coordinator reporting to the Secretary.

For contrast, the two Nixon-era compilations — `frus1969-76ve01` ch3 "U.S. Policy Towards
International Production and Trafficking in Illegal Drugs" (85 docs) and `frus1969-76ve03` ch4
"Drug Control, 1973–1976" (60 docs) — together hold **145 documents**, i.e. about 29% as many as
all 47 pre-1970 sections combined, for 8 years against 58. The 1970s are where the archive gets
dense. Your period is the prehistory, and the corpus is honest about that.

## 4. The archival routes — how to widen the search beyond keywords

Of the 498 compilation documents, 473 carry a parsed source row. Their provenance is remarkably
tidy, and these file designators are better search keys than any English word:

| era | file | what it is | docs |
|---|---|---|---|
| pre-1910 | **Numerical File case 774** | the opium-conference case file ("File No. 774/18–19") | the whole 1906–09 run |
| 1910–49 | **511.4A** (and 511.4-A, 511.4A1, 511.4A2A, 511.4A5, 511.4A6, 511.4R1) | class 5 = Congresses and Conferences, country 11 = United States, .4 = the narcotics conference series | 129 |
| 1910–49 | **500.C1197** | the League of Nations Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium | 33 |
| 1910–49 | **893.114 / 890.114 / 891.114** | ".114 = Traffic in liquors and habit-forming drugs" applied to China, the Far East and Persia | 58 |
| 1940s | **890.114 Narcotics/N** | the wartime opium-suppression file | — |
| 1950s–63 | **341.9**, **345.2** | UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs; UN Drug Supervisory Body | 13 |
| 1958–60 | **892.53** | "Narcotics traffic in Thailand" (named in `frus1958-60v15`'s Sources list) | — |
| post-1963 | **SOC 11–5** | subject-numeric: social conditions, traffic in narcotics (+ country suffixes) | — |
| 1970s | **Lot 81 D 327** | IO/T/HNP files — conference notebooks on US narcotics policy | — |

The `.114` reading comes from the bundled `decimal-class-labels.json`, which carries the 1910–49
State classification schedule and glosses subject suffix `114` as *"Traffic in liquors and
habit-forming drugs"*.

Practical suggestion: query `document_sources.decimal_class LIKE '511.4%'` and
`LIKE '500.C1197%'` and `LIKE '%.114%'` rather than searching prose. That is how you find the
covering notes and enclosures that never say "opium".

## 5. What the corpus will and will not support

**Will support**
- A documented account of State's convening role in 1906–1915, at instruction-by-instruction grain.
- The peculiar posture of 1923–36: a non-member of the League sending consultative delegations to
  its opium machinery, and declining to sign some of its conventions.
- The recurrent linkage of narcotics to China policy, and after 1937 to the Japan question.
- The 1961 Single Convention negotiation, and the State/Treasury division of labour, from
  instructions and delegation reports.
- Change in institutional location over time, evidenced from the volumes' own abbreviation lists.

**Will not support**
- **Anything about enforcement.** These are the Department's papers. Anslinger appears in 18
  documents corpus-wide and has 6 tagged mentions in one volume; the Federal Bureau of Narcotics
  by that name appears in 2. Treasury's own files are not here.
- **The 1950s.** Only **26** documents in the whole corpus are dated 1954–1960 and mention a
  narcotic (1954 = 3, 1955 = 6, 1956 = 8, 1957 = 6, 1958 = 0, 1959 = 2, 1960 = 1), and they are
  incidental mentions inside Vietnam, Burma, Iran and Israel compilations, not a policy record.
  This is almost certainly an artefact of what the FRUS editors compiled for 1955–57 and 1958–60,
  not evidence that the Department did nothing.
- **Quantities, prices, seizures.** The printed record is diplomatic correspondence.
- **The internal working papers behind positions.** Pre-1945 volumes print despatches and
  instructions; the memoranda-of-conversation and staff-paper style that would show how a position
  was formed only becomes common in the post-1945 volumes, and this topic has few of those.

## 6. Traps I hit, so you don't

1. **`811.114` is Prohibition, not narcotics.** It looks like the obvious US narcotics file (class 8
   internal affairs, country 11 = US, .114 = traffic in liquors *and* habit-forming drugs), and it
   is the largest `.114` class in the corpus at 268 documents — but of those, 141 mention "liquor"
   and only **8** mention opium or a narcotic. It is the 1920s rum-running correspondence with
   Britain. Do not fold it in.
2. **"Single Convention" is a false friend.** 67 documents match the phrase; most use it in the
   ordinary sense of *one* convention (e.g. 1884, 1923, 1932 disarmament). Only the 1961–63 volume
   means the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs.
3. **Bare `drug` is unusable.** It matches 1,131 documents, including two whole compilations that
   are not about narcotics: `frus1915Supp`/`frus1916Supp` "Importation of drugs" (17 docs — German
   pharmaceuticals, salvarsan and novocaine, held up by the Allied blockade, file 300.115) and
   `frus1920v02` "appointment of a dye expert" (44 docs — the dyestuffs trade).
4. **Porter stemming.** `narcotic` and `narcotics` are one query; you cannot separate them, and
   `opiate` does not stem to `opium`.
5. **The person tables are useless for this period.** They cover only 287 of 552 volumes, and
   essentially none before 1950 — the volumes that carry Phases 1–4 have no keyed names index at
   all. Searching prose for "Brent" (1,187 documents) or "Porter" (1,504) returns overwhelmingly
   other people. Identify delegates from compilation headings and instructions, not from the
   person layer.
6. **Subject tags include topic-adjacent material.** The 40 tagged documents in `frus1894app2` are
   about opium licensing as Hawaiian domestic politics.

---

## 7. Suggested next steps

1. Read the 498 compilation documents. That is a tractable corpus (2.1 million characters, mean
   4,194 characters a document) and it is the spine of the study.
2. Then sweep by file number — `511.4*`, `500.C1197*`, `*.114`, `774/*`, `341.9`, `345.2`,
   `SOC 11-5` — to pick up the material the editors filed elsewhere.
3. Treat 1948–1960 as a research question about *the volumes*, not about the policy: check the
   Sources sections of the 1952–54, 1955–57 and 1958–60 volumes for narcotics files that were
   surveyed but not compiled (`frus1958-60v15` lists `892.53: Narcotics traffic in Thailand`, so
   at least one editor looked).
4. If the argument turns on the 1970s watershed, the two Nixon "Global Issues" e-volumes give you
   the comparison set at 145 documents.

## Appendix — reproducing the measurements

Open read-only: `sqlite3 -readonly /Users/jbotts/frus-analysis/frus-copy.db`

```sql
-- Route A: keyword universe, pre-1970
WITH h AS (SELECT DISTINCT volume_id, document_id FROM frus_documents
  WHERE frus_documents MATCH
  'body_text: (opium OR narcotic OR cocaine OR heroin OR morphine OR cannabis OR marihuana OR marijuana)')
SELECT COUNT(*), COUNT(DISTINCT volume_id)
FROM h JOIN document_dates d USING(volume_id,document_id) WHERE d.date_iso < '1970';
-- 1082 | 210

-- Route B: the editors' Narcotics subject family, pre-1970
WITH s AS (SELECT DISTINCT volume_id, document_id FROM document_subject_refs
           WHERE subject IN (107,114,264,321,364,395,415,481))
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM s JOIN document_dates d USING(volume_id,document_id) WHERE d.date_iso < '1970';
-- 1016

-- Route C: walk volume_structures for compilation headings matching
--   opium|narcotic|dangerous drug|drug traffic|habit-forming|coca leaf
-- -> 47 sections, 498 documents (script, not SQL)

-- the 811.114 trap
SELECT CASE WHEN c.body_text LIKE '%opium%' OR c.body_text LIKE '%narcotic%' THEN 'narcotics'
            WHEN c.body_text LIKE '%liquor%' THEN 'liquor' ELSE 'neither' END, COUNT(*)
FROM document_sources s JOIN document_cache c USING(volume_id,document_id)
WHERE s.decimal_class LIKE '811.114%' GROUP BY 1;
-- liquor 141 | narcotics 8 | neither 120

-- the institutional watershed
SELECT volume_id, term, definition FROM terms WHERE term IN ('S/NM','CCINC','BNDD','INCB');
-- every row is a 1969-76 volume
```

Key documents cited above, by id: `frus1906p1/d332`, `frus1906p1/d336`, `frus1907p1/d117`
(the convening sequence); `frus1923v01/d35` (Porter/Brent/Blue instruction);
`frus1924v01/d57` (US suggestions to the Second Opium Conference); `frus1931v01/d537`
(Limitation Convention); `frus1944v02/d895` (Hull to Stimson, the Department's own origin story);
`frus1944v02/d901` (opium in liberated territory, China); `frus1961-63v25/d326`
(Rusk's instructions to Anslinger).
