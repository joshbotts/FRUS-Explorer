# Scoping memo — fishing rights and territorial waters in the FRUS corpus

**To:** the historian
**Re:** *How did the United States handle disputes over fishing rights and territorial waters with other governments?*
**Status:** scoping pass. Nothing here is a finding about the past; everything is a finding about what the corpus holds and how reliably it can be searched.

---

## 1. Short answer

The corpus holds a great deal on this question — enough for a monograph — and it is unusually
well-shaped for it, because for most of the relevant period the FRUS editors **filed this material
under their own topical headings**. I can point you to **127 compilations and chapters across 85
volumes whose titles are explicitly about fisheries, sealing, whaling, territorial waters, the
continental shelf or the law of the sea**, containing **1,957 documents**. A wider keyword sweep
returns 5,177 documents across 480 of the 552 volumes.

The topic is also unusually **badly served by the obvious search methods**, and in a direction that
matters: both the keyword route and the app's subject tags systematically miss the nineteenth-century
material, which is where the topic is densest. I measured this rather than assuming it — see §5.

---

## 2. The shape of the coverage

I ran a composite phrase query (`fisheries`, `"fishing rights"`, `"fishing vessels"`,
`"fishing grounds"`, `fishermen`, `"territorial waters"`, `"territorial sea"`, `"fur seal"`,
`"law of the sea"`, `"continental shelf"`, `"contiguous zone"`, `"innocent passage"`, `"three-mile"`,
`"marine league(s)"`, `"maritime jurisdiction"`, `"jurisdictional waters"`, `"pelagic sealing"`,
`"exclusive economic zone"`) over the full-text index: **5,177 hits**, 1.6% of the 316,839 documents.

Raw counts mislead here, because the corpus is wildly uneven by decade (the 1940s alone hold 24% of
all documents). Normalised — hits as a share of that decade's documents — the topic is a **U**:

| decade | documents | hits | rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1860s | 11,250 | 190 | 1.69% |
| 1870s | 5,798 | 249 | **4.29%** |
| 1880s | 6,472 | 292 | **4.51%** |
| 1890s | 9,712 | 330 | 3.40% |
| 1900s | 9,925 | 192 | 1.93% |
| 1910s | 30,370 | 383 | 1.26% |
| 1920s | 19,733 | 346 | 1.75% |
| 1930s | 39,197 | 382 | 0.97% |
| 1940s | 75,680 | 542 | **0.72%** |
| 1950s | 46,506 | 595 | 1.28% |
| 1960s | 29,817 | 566 | 1.90% |
| 1970s | 23,264 | 742 | **3.19%** |
| 1980s | 6,123 | 235 | **3.84%** |

Two peaks — the Atlantic-fisheries and Bering-Sea decades, and the Law-of-the-Sea decades — with a
long trough in between when the series is dominated by war and grand strategy. If you work from raw
hit counts you will conclude the topic is a modern one; it is not.

## 3. What is actually in there — from the editors' own headings

This is the most valuable thing I found, and it is worth more than any keyword count: I walked
`volume_structures` (the TEI section tree for all 552 volumes) and pulled every compilation, chapter
and subchapter whose **title** names the topic. That is the FRUS editors' own topical filing, and it
gives you a ready-made table of contents. Abridged, in order:

**Anglo-American / Canadian inshore fisheries, 1872–1930.** The single longest thread.
`frus1872p1` "Fisheries in waters adjacent to the British North American possessions" ·
`frus1880` "Alleged Outrage upon American Fishermen" (Fortune Bay) · `frus1885`, `frus1886`
(38 docs of correspondence with American fishing interests) · the Newfoundland fishery question
running as a named chapter through `frus1905`, `frus1906p1` (70 docs), `frus1907p1`, `frus1908`,
`frus1909` · `frus1909` "Special agreement … relating to North Atlantic coast fisheries" and
`frus1910` "The North Atlantic Coast Fisheries Arbitration — Decision of the Permanent Court of
Arbitration at The Hague" · then the interwar Canadian sequence: halibut convention 1923
(`frus1923v01`, 16 docs), its 1928–30 replacement, the Fraser River sockeye salmon convention,
lobster and port privileges, Missisquoi Bay.

**Bering Sea fur seals and North Pacific sealing, 1888–1911.**
`frus1888p2` "Papers relating to the seizure of British sealing-vessels in Bering Sea" (68 docs) ·
`frus1894app1` "Enforcement of regulations respecting fur seals" (144 docs — a whole appendix volume
compilation) · "Protection of the fur seal" as a named chapter in 1895, 1895p2, 1896, 1897 (70 docs) ·
`frus1911` the four-power fur seal convention · its attempted revision by Japan in 1926 and 1928, and
Japan's abrogation in 1940–41.

**Whaling and sealing claims against Russia.** `frus1902app1` is an **entire appendix volume** on this
(202 documents; 25% of it matches the topic query outright), organised as numbered arbitration cases —
the *Cape Horn Pigeon*, the *James Hamilton Lewis*. Preceded by "Arbitration of claims of American
sealing and whaling vessels v. Russia" chapters in `frus1900`.

**Territorial-sea limits as a legal question, 1904–1936.**
`frus1912` "Extension by Russia of the three-mile limit of territorial waters to twelve miles for
customs purposes" (22 docs) · `frus1914Supp` "The question of the limits of territorial waters — The
Italian six-mile rule" · `frus1930v01` the Hague Codification Conference (24 docs) · `frus1935v01`
"Iran's attempt by unilateral action to extend its exclusive jurisdiction" · `frus1935v04` and
`frus1936v05` on Ecuadoran fishing regulations · `frus1936v01` "Refusal of the British Government to
recognize the right of the United States to exercise control outside [territorial waters]".

**Wartime neutrality and territorial waters, 1914–1918.** A distinct sub-thread where the question is
security, not fish: "Actions of belligerent warships outside territorial waters — Hovering"
(`frus1914Supp` 15 docs, `frus1915Supp` 20 docs, `frus1916Supp` 4 docs), "the Mining by Norway of Its
Territorial Waters" (`frus1918Supp01v02`, 18 docs).

**Japan and the Alaska fisheries, 1935–1939.** "Disinclination of Japan to negotiate a convention …
regulating fisheries off the coast of Alaska" (`frus1935v03`, `frus1936v04`), then "Representations to
Japan in regard to regulation of fisheries off the coast of Alaska" (`frus1937v04`, 40 docs;
`frus1939v02`, 37 docs).

**Whaling as a treaty subject, 1937–1946.** International Conference for the Regulation of Whaling
1937, the 1938 protocol, informal conferences 1939, 1943, 1944, 1945, and the 1946 Washington
conference that produced the ICRW.

**The 1945 turn.** `frus1945v02`, "Formulation of United States policy on the resources of the
Continental Shelf and on coastal fisheries" — **35 documents**, running from Ickes to Roosevelt in
June 1943 through the Legal Adviser's memoranda to `d794`, "Press Release Issued by the White House,
September 28, 1945": the Truman Proclamations. This is the internal paper trail of the moment the
United States stopped protesting unilateral extensions and made one.

**The consequences of 1945.** Mexico's continental-shelf policy and its effect on US–Mexican fisheries
(`frus1946v11`), US–Mexican fisheries conflict (`frus1947v08`), Costa Rican fisheries legislation,
"Policies of the United States and certain other American Republics with respect to the continental
shelf" (`frus1948v09`), the northwest Atlantic fisheries convention (`frus1949v04`).

**Law of the Sea as a standing policy file, 1952–1988.** "United States policy regarding the law of
the sea" (`frus1952-54v01p2`, 28 docs) → `frus1955-57v11` (29) → **`frus1958-60v02` "LAW OF THE SEA"
(108 docs**, UNCLOS I and II) → `frus1961-63v25` (20) → `frus1969-76ve03` "Law of the Sea" (54) and
`frus1969-76ve01` "Oceans Policy" (113) → **`frus1981-88v41` "Law of the Sea" (89 docs)** plus
"International Regulation of Whaling Practices" (48). The Reagan-era volume is the densest in the
corpus on this topic: 125 of its 376 documents (33%) match.

**Adjacent, and you must decide whether it is in scope.** A large body of material concerns
*navigation through straits* rather than fishing or the breadth of the territorial sea: the Turkish
Straits and Montreux (`frus1934v02`, `frus1936v03`, `frus1945v08`, `frus1946v07`, and the Potsdam
volumes), the Straits of Tiran and Gulf of Aqaba (`frus1955-57v17`, 206 documents in one subchapter),
the Taiwan Strait crises, and the *Pueblo* seizure. The Pueblo material is filed by the department
itself under a territorial-waters file number (see §6), so it is arguably core rather than adjacent.

## 4. What I would search for

Three routes, in this order of trustworthiness.

**(a) Section titles, not text.** Walk `volume_structures` and match section titles. This is the
highest-precision instrument in the corpus for this question and it works across the whole period,
because *both* editorial regimes label topically — the 19th-century omnibus volume names a chapter
"Protection of the fur seal", the modern single-subject volume names a compilation "Law of the Sea".

**(b) Full text, with the vocabulary widened for period.** The phrase list in §2 is a starting point.
It must be extended with the language the sources actually used:
`Behring` (see §5), `sealing act`, `seal skins`, `schooner`, `modus vivendi`, `marine league`,
`headland`, `Fortune Bay`, `Bay of Fundy`, `Gulf of St. Lawrence`, `Grand Banks`, `Cape Horn Pigeon`,
`James Hamilton Lewis`, `Paris Tribunal`, `hot pursuit`, `Santiago Declaration`, `cod war`,
`tuna boats`, `200-mile`, `UNCLOS`.

**(c) The subject taxonomy**, as a supplement only. The bundled vocabulary has directly usable terms
under an *International Law* category: `Territorial waters` (1,622 documents), `Law of the sea` (588),
`Territorial sea limit` (347), `Continental shelf` (268), `Innocent passage` (172), `Whaling industry`
(96), `Offshore oil` (46), `Exclusive economic zone` (25), `Fishing agreement` (22), `Fishing limit`
(10), `Commercial whaling` (27), `Sea bed mining` (2). Union: 2,611 documents.

## 5. What I measured — and the three warnings that come out of it

I treated the 1,957 documents inside the editors' own topical compilations as a **gold set** and asked
what each search route recovers from it. This is the part of the memo I would most want you to check.

| route | recall of the gold set | pre-1920 | 1920 onward |
|---|---|---|---|
| composite keyword query (§2) | 1,265 / 1,957 = **65%** | **49%** | 77% |
| topic subject tags (§4c) | 689 / 1,957 = **35%** | **10%** | 54% |
| (any subject tag at all) | 1,406 / 1,957 = 72% | — | — |

**Warning 1 — the search tools fail exactly where the topic is densest.** A keyword sweep loses half
the pre-1920 material, and the subject tags lose nine-tenths of it. This is not because those
documents are untagged in general (75% of the whole corpus carries some subject tag, evenly across
decades); it is because the *vocabulary* is a modern-diplomacy vocabulary. "Territorial waters" is
applied as a subject 16 times in the 1860s and 213 times in the 1910s. Sampled, the misses are plainly
on-topic — Bayard on the North Pacific sealing act, Gresham to Pauncefote on the Paris award
regulations, the Newfoundland modus vivendi correspondence — they simply say "the schooners", "the
regulations", "the act", never "fishing rights".

**Warning 2 — the search index's stemmer is dangerous on this vocabulary.** The full-text index uses
Porter stemming, so:

- `fishing` = `fish` = `fishes` — one and the same query, 7,283 hits, which includes fish as
  provisions and, memorably, **Hamilton Fish**, Secretary of State 1869–1877, whose correspondence
  headings are all over the 1870s volumes.
- `sealing` = `seal` = `seals` = `sealed` — 5,162 hits, conflating fur seals with sealed envelopes and
  the Great Seal.

Neither term is usable bare. Use quoted phrases (`"fur seal"`, `"fishing vessels"`), which are exact.

**Warning 3 — the archaic spelling is the majority spelling.** `"Behring Sea"` returns **217**
documents, `"Bering Sea"` **342**, and `Behring` on its own **360**. A search for the modern spelling
alone silently drops most of the 1888–1897 dispute.

**A fourth, smaller point — phrase hits are not topic hits.** `frus1969-76v42/d14` matches
`"territorial waters"` because Le Duc Tho tells Kissinger "We have no ships in U.S. territorial
waters." Real phrase, not a maritime dispute. Of the 5,177 keyword hits, only 2,452 are also
subject-tagged for the topic; treat the keyword set as a candidate pool, not a result.

department's own file numbers cluster tightly, which means you can go from this corpus to a NARA pull

## 7. The answer-shape the corpus supports

I want to be careful: what follows is the pattern of the editors' *headings and file numbers*, not a
verified account. But the corpus is organised in a way that makes a clear argument available, and it
is worth stating so you can test it:

1. **Adjudication.** The United States repeatedly put fisheries and sealing disputes to third parties —
   the Paris Tribunal (1893), the Hague Permanent Court of Arbitration (North Atlantic Coast
   Fisheries, 1910), the Russian whaling and sealing claims commission (1902). Three of these have
   dedicated FRUS appendix volumes or named chapters, i.e. the department wanted the record public.
2. **Modus vivendi and bilateral convention** as the working instrument between arbitrations — the
   Newfoundland modus vivendi 1906–09, the 1911 four-power fur seal convention, halibut 1923, Fraser
   River sockeye 1930.
3. **Protest and reservation of rights** against other states' unilateral extensions — Russia's
   twelve-mile customs zone (1912), Iran (1935), Ecuador (1935–36), Mexico's shelf decree (1945–46).
4. **Then, in 1945, the reversal**: `frus1945v02`'s 35-document compilation shows the United States
   preparing and issuing exactly the kind of unilateral extension it had spent forty years protesting,
   with the Legal Adviser's memoranda in the file.
5. **Then multilateral codification** as damage control and as policy — The Hague 1930, UNCLOS I
   (1958), II (1960), III (1973–82) — ending in `frus1981-88v41`'s 89 documents on the decision not to
   sign.
6. **Throughout, a second and separate meaning of "territorial waters"** — belligerent hovering,
   mined neutral waters, the Turkish Straits, Tiran, the Taiwan Strait, the *Pueblo* — where the
   dispute is about warships, not fish. The department filed some of it under the same numbers.

## 8. How to check me

Everything above is reproducible from the read-only database and the bundled JSON. The load-bearing
steps:

```sh
DB="file:/Users/jbotts/frus-analysis/frus-copy.db?mode=ro"

# §2 — the composite query (5,177)
sqlite3 "$DB" "select count(*) from frus_documents where frus_documents match
 'fisheries OR \"fishing rights\" OR \"fishing vessels\" OR \"fishing grounds\" OR fishermen
  OR \"territorial waters\" OR \"territorial sea\" OR \"fur seal\" OR \"law of the sea\"
  OR \"continental shelf\" OR \"contiguous zone\" OR \"innocent passage\" OR \"three-mile\"
  OR \"marine league\" OR \"maritime jurisdiction\" OR \"pelagic sealing\"
  OR \"exclusive economic zone\"';"

# §5 warning 2 — the stemmer collapses these to one term
for q in fish fishing fishes seal sealing seals sealed; do
  sqlite3 "$DB" "select '$q', count(*) from frus_documents where frus_documents match '$q';"
done          # -> 7283 for every fish form, 5162 for every seal form

# §5 warning 3 — spelling
sqlite3 "$DB" "select count(*) from frus_documents where frus_documents match '\"behring sea\"';"  # 217
sqlite3 "$DB" "select count(*) from frus_documents where frus_documents match '\"bering sea\"';"   # 342

# §3 — the editors' own headings (127 sections / 1,957 documents)
#   parse volume_structures.structure_json, walk sections + subsections recursively,
#   match titles against:
#   fisher(y|ies)|fishing|fur seal|sealing[- ]vessel|law of the sea|continental shelf|
#   whaling|territorial water|halibut|sockeye|seabed|oceans policy

# §6 — provenance
sqlite3 "$DB" "select decimal_class, count(*) from document_sources
  where decimal_class in ('711.428','399.731','811.0145') group by 1;"

# §3 — the Truman Proclamation anchor
sqlite3 "$DB" "select header from document_cache
  where volume_id='frus1945v02' and document_id='d794';"
```

Three checks I would run before trusting any of this further, and have not:

- **My gold set is not independent of route (a).** It is built from section titles, so route (a)
  recovers it by construction and routes (b)/(c) are being scored against a target that favours the
  editors' vocabulary. The recall percentages in §5 are therefore a fair comparison of (b) against (c),
  and a fair *absolute* warning about pre-1920 recall, but they are not evidence that (a) is complete.
  A hand-read sample of, say, 200 random pre-1920 documents would give an unbiased base rate.
- **I have not read the documents.** Every characterisation in §7 comes from headings, file numbers and
  the first 200 characters of a handful of documents.
- **Person-based approaches will not work on the early material.** Separately in this session I
  established that the corpus's person index exists for only 287 of 552 volumes, essentially all
  covering 1950 onward: 0% of the 1860s–1900s volumes and 7% of the 1870s carry one. You cannot ask
  "who handled the fisheries question" of the Bayard or Blaine period through the person tables. The
  document text and the section headings are the only handles there.

One last practical note: the database contains 6,556 machine-generated document summaries and only
**8** hand-written notes. 227 of the summaries mention this topic. Those are derived text and should
not be counted as corpus evidence or quoted as such.
