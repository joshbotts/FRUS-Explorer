# Scoping memo — fishing rights and territorial waters in the FRUS corpus

**To:** the historian
**Re:** How did the United States handle disputes over fishing rights and territorial waters
with other governments?
**Status:** scoping pass. Nothing below is a finding about the past; it is a finding about what
the corpus can be made to yield, and how to get at it.

---

## 1. Bottom line

The corpus supports this question **unusually well**, for a reason that is worth stating up front:
fisheries and territorial-waters disputes are one of the very few subjects that FRUS's editors
tracked *continuously and by name* from the 1870s to the 1980s. I found **130 volumes containing
238 editorial section headings** that name the topic — from "Fisheries in waters adjacent to the
British North American possessions" (1872) through "Newfoundland fishery question" (1904–1912),
"Fisheries and related questions (Canada)" (1918–1930), "United States policy regarding the
delimitation of the territorial sea" (1950–51), to "Law of the Sea" (1958–60, 1961–63, 1973–76,
1981–88). That editorial thread is the single most valuable thing here, and it is not reachable
by keyword search of document text — it lives in the volume table-of-contents structures.

Full-text search finds **4,493 documents** on a broad twelve-term query, about **1.4% of the
corpus**. That number is a ceiling with maybe 65–75% precision, not a corpus of on-topic
documents. The editorially-headed sections give roughly **1,800 documents that the editors
themselves grouped under the topic** — a much cleaner starting corpus.

Two things you should know before designing anything around this:

* **"Territorial waters" is not a fisheries term in the mid-twentieth century.** Only 533 of the
  2,473 documents matching a territorial-waters phrase also mention fishing. The rest are
  neutrality law (WWI belligerent warships "hovering" outside the three-mile line), submarine
  access, and seizure incidents. The largest single cluster of "territorial waters" documents in
  the 1960s is **the USS *Pueblo* crisis** — genuinely a territorial-waters dispute, entirely not
  a fisheries one.
* **The corpus thins out exactly where the question climaxes.** The 1977–80 subseries has no
  dedicated Law of the Sea compilation, and 1964–68 has none either. UNCLOS III ran 1973–1982;
  FRUS covers its Nixon-Ford half (1969–76 volumes E-1 and E-3, 167 documents) and its Reagan
  endgame (1981–88 vol. XLI, 89 documents) but has a hole across the Carter negotiations.

I ran everything against the SQLite index at `/Users/jbotts/frus-analysis/frus-copy.db`
(read-only), plus the bundled `manifest.json`, `document-subject-index.json` and
`decimal-class-labels.json`. Four independent routes into the topic, deliberately, because each
one has a different failure mode.

**Route 1 — full-text search (`frus_documents`, FTS5, porter stemming).**
A warning that cost me two queries: the tokenizer stems, so `fishing`, `fished`, `fisher` and
`fish` are **one token** (7,283 documents — far too broad, it catches fish as food and as cargo),
while `fisheries` and `fishery` stem to a different token (1,969). Any query written naively will
either over- or under-shoot by a factor of four. The queries I settled on:

## 3. What the corpus holds — the editorial spine

FRUS volumes carry a table of contents with **compilation headings written by the editors**. I
parsed all 552 `volume_structures` JSON trees and matched section titles against
`fisher|fishing|seal|whal|territorial water|territorial sea|law of the sea|three-mile|continental
shelf`. Result: **238 matching sections in 130 volumes** on a loose pattern; **134 sections in 87
volumes covering 1,817 documents** after removing obvious homonyms (Marine Corps, consular seal,
"sealed"). These are not keyword hits. They are the editors saying *this block of documents is
about this dispute*.

The thread, as the headings themselves tell it:

**(a) The North Atlantic inshore fisheries, 1870–1912 — Britain/Canada/Newfoundland.**
`frus1872p1` "Fisheries in waters adjacent to the British North American possessions" (7 docs);
`frus1880` "…Alleged Outrage upon American Fishermen" (16); `frus1885` "Agreement… Respecting the
Fisheries" (10); `frus1886` "Correspondence… with parties interested in American Fishing Vessels"
(38); `frus1904`–`frus1912` a run of "Newfoundland fishery question" sections (`frus1906p1` alone
has 70 documents), ending at `frus1910` "The North Atlantic Coast Fisheries Arbitration — Decision
of the Permanent Court of Arbitration at The Hague". A complete arc from seizure incidents through
modus vivendi to arbitral award, in the editors' own arrangement.

**(b) The Bering Sea fur-seal controversy, 1886–1904.** The densest nineteenth-century block.
`frus1891` (95 Bering Sea documents), `frus1894app1` — an entire appendix volume whose title runs
"Enforcement of Regulations Respecting Fur Seals" (144 documents under that heading),
`frus1888p2` "Supplement C: Papers relating to the seizure of British sealing-vessels in Behring
Sea" (68), `frus1895p1` "Protection of fur seals in Bering Sea" (76), `frus1897` (70), `frus1896`
(30), then the Russian side — "Arrest of American sealers on Robben Island" (1896, 1897) and
`frus1902app1`, **a whole volume titled "Whaling and Sealing Claims Against Russia, On Account of
Arrest and Seizure of the American Vessels 'Cape Horn Pigeon,' 'James Hamilton Lewis,'
'C. H. White,' and 'Kate and Anna'"** (202 documents, the whole volume). If you want a case study of how the United
States converted a seizure dispute into an arbitration, this volume is the file.

**(c) Territorial-sea doctrine before it was called that, 1904–1930.**
`frus1912` "Extension by Russia of the three-mile limit of territorial waters to twelve miles for
customs purposes" (22 docs) — a direct nineteenth-century ancestor of the 200-mile fight.
`frus1914Supp`–`frus1918Supp01v02`: "Actions of belligerent warships outside territorial waters —
Hovering", "The question of the limits of territorial waters — the Italian six-mile rule", "the
Mining by Norway of Its Territorial Waters". **This is where 'territorial waters' becomes a
neutrality-law term and stops being a fisheries term** — the single most important thing to
understand before reading any aggregate count. `frus1930v01` "Conference for the Codification of
International Law, The Hague, 1930" (24 docs) is the first attempt to codify the limit.

**(d) The interwar conservation treaties, 1918–1944 — Canada and Japan.** Twenty-odd headings, a
year-by-year serial: halibut (1923 convention, `frus1923v01` 16 docs; renegotiation 1928, 1929,
1930), Fraser River sockeye salmon (1928–1937), Missisquoi Bay seine fishing (1927–1929), fur seals
(the 1911 four-power convention, Japan's 1926 and 1928 revision proposals, its 1940 abrogation
notice, the 1942 US–Canada replacement), and international whaling (London 1937, protocol 1938,
1943 British proposal, London 1944, London 1945, Washington 1946 — the founding of the IWC). And
running through the late 1930s: **`frus1935v03`/`frus1936v04`/`frus1937v04`/`frus1939v02`
"Disinclination of Japan to negotiate a convention… regulating fisheries off the coast of Alaska"**
(12 + 10 + 40 + 37 documents) — the Bristol Bay salmon dispute, one of the better-documented
bilateral fisheries confrontations in the corpus and a strand of the pre-Pearl-Harbor
deterioration.

**(e) The 1945 hinge.** `frus1945v02` **"Formulation of United States policy on the resources of
the Continental Shelf and on coastal fisheries"** (35 documents) — the internal drafting of the
Truman Proclamations. Then the reaction, compiled country by country: Mexico's shelf policy
(`frus1946v11`, `frus1947v08`), Costa Rican fisheries legislation (`frus1947v08`), El Salvador's
definition of its territorial sea (`frus1950v02`), and `frus1948v09` "Policies of the United States
and certain other American Republics with respect to the continental shelf". The corpus documents
both the American act and the Latin American answer that produced the 200-mile claim.

**(f) The Law of the Sea, 1950–1988.** A named, continuous compilation across eight subseries:

| subseries | heading | docs |
|---|---|---|
| 1950 | "United States policy regarding questions pertaining to the delimitation of the territorial sea" | — |
| 1951 | "…delimitation of the territorial sea and related matters" | 9 |
| 1952–54 | "United States policy regarding the law of the sea" | 28 |
| 1955–57 | "United States Policy Regarding the Law of the Sea" | 29 |
| 1958–60 | "UNITED NATIONS > LAW OF THE SEA" | **108** |
| 1961–63 | "Law of the Sea" | 20 |
| 1964–68 | *— none —* | 0 |
| 1969–72 | "Documents on Global Issues > Oceans Policy" (vol. E-1) | **113** |
| 1973–76 | "Documents on Global Issues > Law of the Sea" (vol. E-3) | 54 |
| 1977–80 | *— none —* (11 scattered documents in vol. I) | ~11 |
| 1981–88 | "Global Issues II > Law of the Sea" (vol. XLI) | **89** |
| 1981–88 | "Global Issues II > International Regulation of Whaling Practices" | 48 |

Note also `frus1969-76ve02` "Seabed Arms Control" (76 docs) — adjacent, and a reminder that the
seabed was simultaneously an arms-control problem.

The 1981–88 volume covers the decision itself: its own preface reads "The decision not to sign the
Law of the Sea Treaty required a year and a…", and documents 114–116 are the sign/don't-sign
review. The Reagan refusal is documented; the Carter negotiation that produced the text is not.

## 4. The archival route

If the object is to go past the printed volumes to the files, the corpus hands you the finding aid.

**The State Department decimal classification** (bundled at `decimal-class-labels.json`, the
1910–1949 schedule) assigns this subject three keys, composed as *class + country + suffix*:

* `8**.628` — **Fisheries**
* `8**.627` — **Fur seals**
* `8**.0145` — **Territorial waters. Marginal sea**

`document_sources.decimal_class` records the class the FRUS editors cited for each printed
document, so you can go straight from a class to the printed documents drawn from it and thence
to the file. Counts in this corpus:

**Read the third column, not the second.** In raw counts the topic looks like a mid-century
subject; normalised, it is a **nineteenth-century subject that returns**. The rate peaks in the
1880s (Bering Sea + North Atlantic fisheries, 43 per 1,000 documents), collapses to its floor in
the 1940s (7.2, when the corpus is saturated with the war), and climbs steadily back to 23.5 in
the 1980s. The 1880s and the 1980s are the two peaks — the fur-seal seizures and the Law of the
Sea Treaty — and the shape of the curve is itself an argument worth pursuing.

Modern subseries totals on a slightly wider query (adding `"law of the sea"` and
`"continental shelf"`): 1969–76 **663**, 1964–68 221, 1977–80 200, 1958–60 199, 1981–88 193,
1952–54 135, 1955–57 124, 1961–63 122, everything earlier 2,581.

Continental shelf appears from 1937 and peaks in 1976 (30 documents), 1969 (22), 1977 (19),
1945 (19).

## 6. Evidence you need in order to check me

**Precision is roughly 70%, and I am estimating that from a sample of sixteen.** I took every
173rd hit of query family A with a snippet and read them. Clearly on topic: 1886 (Anglo-American
fisheries relations), 1891 (British seal fishery act), 1906 (fishing rights, American agent for
the fisheries), 1929 (fisheries fact-finding commission), 1938 (Fisheries Convention signature),
1945 (continental shelf and coastal fisheries — the Truman Proclamation), 1956 (continental shelf,
adjacent zones, fisheries), 1963 ("the President asked whether the fisheries question is the most
important issue"), 1969 (12-mile fisheries zone), 1983 (US fishery resource jurisdiction). Clearly
**off** topic: 1875 (a Norwegian Arctic expedition crewed by whale-fishermen), 1951 (an aid budget
line listing "agricultural and fisheries $495,000"), 1973 (Haitian migrants "disguised as
fishermen"). Marginal: 1864 (an incident involving two fishermen), 1919 (Versailles terms leaving
Germany her fishing fleet). That is 10 clear / 3 clear-negative / 3 marginal — **call it 65–75%,
with a wide error bar on a sample this size.** Enlarge the sample before quoting a precision
figure.

**The territorial-waters contamination is measured, not guessed.** 2,473 documents match a
territorial-waters phrase; only 533 (21.6%) also match a fishing phrase. A stratified snippet read
across decades gives the flavour: 1871 "United States to fish within the territorial waters of Her
Britannic Majesty" (fisheries) vs 1920 "Enemy to reveal position of all his mines outside
territorial waters" (armistice) vs 1960 "junks are also sailing outside the territorial waters of
South Viet-Nam" vs 1972 "measures within the internal and claimed territorial waters of North
Vietnam to interdict…". And in `frus1964-68v29p1` (Korea), where I found 70 hits, every snippet I
pulled is *Pueblo*: "the Pueblo could have been in North Korean territorial waters", "they claim…
that the Pueblo was within their territorial waters."

**Nineteenth-century documents cannot be found by header.** Sampled headers from the 1870–99 hits
read "Mr. Fish to Mr. Thornton.", "Mr. Thornton to Mr. Davis.", "[Untitled]". There is no subject
line. Modern headers are no better for a different reason — "Telegram From the Embassy in Korea to
the Department of State", "Notes of Meeting". **In both eras the subject lives in the volume's
table of contents, not in the document's own header.** This is why route 3 matters and why any
search design that leans on `header` will fail across the whole corpus.

**Reproduce anything above with:**

```sql
-- the broad query
SELECT count(*) FROM frus_documents WHERE frus_documents MATCH
 'fisheri OR fishermen OR "fishing rights" OR "fishing vessels" OR "fishing grounds"
  OR "fishing limits" OR "fishing industry" OR "territorial waters" OR "territorial sea"
  OR "three mile limit" OR "marginal sea" OR "maritime jurisdiction" OR "territorial limits"';

-- the editorial spine (Python over volume_structures.structure_json, recursing
-- through sections[].subsections[] and matching each node's "title")

-- the archival keys
SELECT decimal_class, count(*) FROM document_sources
 WHERE decimal_class LIKE '%.628%' OR decimal_class LIKE '%.627%'
    OR decimal_class LIKE '%.0145%' GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 2 DESC;
```

## 7. Gaps, and what they are gaps *of*

* **1964–68 and 1977–80 have no Law of the Sea compilation.** The Carter-era UNCLOS III
  negotiation — arguably the decisive phase — is not compiled. This is an editorial choice, and
  worth confirming against the published volume list before you build on it.
* **The corpus stops in the 1980s and the 1980s are thin.** Only twelve of an eventual ~40
  volumes of the 1981–88 subseries are published, and the 1989–92 subseries has one. Anything
  after the 1982 signature decision is essentially unavailable here.
* **Person markup does not exist before ~1950**, so you cannot trace an individual negotiator
  across the nineteenth-century fisheries correspondence using the person tables. Hamilton Fish
  runs through the 1870s material and has 291 tagged mentions corpus-wide; William H. Seward has
  **one**. Do it by full text and by volume section, not by person.
* **The subject taxonomy has no fisheries category**, so any dashboard built on subject tags will
  silently under-report the fishing half of this question by roughly two orders of magnitude.
* **Whaling is entangled and probably wants separating.** 497 documents; the editorial thread runs
  from the 1902 Russian claims volume through the 1937–46 conferences to the 1981–88 "International
  Regulation of Whaling Practices" (48 docs). It is a conservation-regime story that shares a
  vocabulary with the fisheries story but is not the same dispute.

## 8. Where I would start

1. **Read the 134 editorial section headings as a reading list** and pull the ~1,800 documents
   under them. That is your working corpus; the 4,493 full-text hits are the recall net around it.
2. **Take three case files whole**, one per era, each of which the editors already assembled:
   `frus1902app1` (Whaling and Sealing Claims Against Russia — 202 docs, a complete arbitration);
   `frus1937v04` + `frus1939v02` (the Alaska fisheries dispute with Japan — 77 docs); and
   `frus1958-60v02` "LAW OF THE SEA" (108 docs, the two Geneva conferences).
3. **Follow `894.628`, `822.628`, `811.0145` and `861.0145` into the central files** for the
   documents the editors did *not* print.
4. **Test one hypothesis the rate curve suggests**: that the vocabulary of the dispute is
   continuous — the 1912 Russian extension of the limit to twelve miles for customs purposes,
   the 1930 Hague codification attempt, the 1945 Truman Proclamation, and the 200-mile claims of
   the 1970s are arguably one argument conducted over sixty years. The corpus has the documents at
   every node, in blocks the editors themselves marked out, which is a rare thing.
