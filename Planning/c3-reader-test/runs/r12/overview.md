# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

*Research run r12, 2026-10-26. Every figure below is conditional on the library of 553 volumes I searched, and the figures that matter most are in the companion memo.*

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series answers the first half of the question well, in the Department's own words, from 1877 to 1973; it answers the second half case by case. The stated test is short and old: a new regime counted once it held the machinery of government (was *de facto*, meaning in actual control whatever its legal title), was accepted by the people or at least not resisted, and was able and willing to meet its country's international obligations. It is written out for Colombia in 1900 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530) and set down again as settled practice in 1948 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). What changed was what the Department asked in addition. Under Wilson and his successors it asked for constitutional title (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379), and in 1921 it recognized Bolivia only after elections and in concert with Argentina and Brazil (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238). From 1923 it backed a Central American treaty that barred recognition of coup leaders, enforced it against El Salvador's General Martínez from 1931 until January 1934, and abandoned it in 1936 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232; Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). The specific asks are on the record: a claims commission, a financial adviser and a loan convention from Nicaragua in 1911 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Document 933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933); a treaty protecting property acquired before 1917 from Mexico in 1921 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344); an armistice, an early free election and an undertaking not to stand, put to Huerta's Mexico in 1913 and refused (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028); cooperation against the Axis, floated for Argentina in 1944 (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 226, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d226); civil liberties, free elections by a date and international observers, sought from Peru's junta in 1962 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423); and private pledges against Communism and against restoring relations with Cuba from Bolivia's junta in 1964 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152). From 1948 the Department said openly that withholding recognition was a poor way to change a regime (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115), and by 1973 its public line was that it recognized governments, not heads of government (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348). The evidence is about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes whose editors headed chapters on revolutions and recognition (1895 to 1950), plus about 450 later documents from 20 Western Hemisphere volumes found by search; the collection that goes with this report holds the 56 documents the argument rests on.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The printed papers are a selection, and the editors name the shelves they came from. The two channels below answer different questions (where the printed papers were found, and what the footnotes cite without printing), and I have not added them together. Almost everything came from one place: the Department of State's central decimal file, in General Records of the Department of State, Record Group 59 at the National Archives. In that system each paper carried a number built from a subject class and a country number, so that 824.00 is Bolivia's political affairs and 812.00 is Mexico's. The offline data I have glosses those numbers but does not carry the series' Archives identifier, entry number, dates or access status, so I do not state them; the finding aid has to come from the Archives.

*Series the printed documents came from (channel one, the chapter documents only):*

- Central decimal file, Record Group 59, file numbers in the 800 range (internal affairs, by country) and some 700 numbers, 1910 to 1950 in this set: more than nine in ten of the chapter documents cite a file number as their source (a number is glossed only inside the years the Archives' classification schedule governs, and these fall inside them). Identifier, entry and access: not in the data available to me.
- Diplomatic despatches and instructions before 1906, Record Group 59, which carry no file number: about ninety chapter documents have no source citation in the index, most of them in the 1895 to 1904 volumes. The data names the Archives' microfilm rolls for three: despatches from ministers to Venezuela, roll July 1899 to March 1900, Archives identifier 188967467; to Colombia, roll September 1900 to January 1902, 189300896; to Ecuador, roll May 1894 to December 1896, 189312130. Instructions to ministers are named for Colombia (volume 19, November 1898 to August 1906, 149302787) and Ecuador (volumes 1 and 2, 1848 to 1906, 149303924); none was found for Venezuela. Access status for these rolls is not in the data.
- Policy Planning Staff files (the Secretary of State's planning office), lot 64 D 563 (a lot is an accession of Department files known by year and number), Record Group 59: one chapter document cites it. The Archives divides this lot among twelve series; one candidate is the Chronological Files, identifier 1136548, entry A1 558-D, 1947 to 1962, unrestricted, five feet three inches.

*Series the footnotes cite without printing (channel two):*

- The editors' notes to about sixty chapter documents cite about seventy file numbers, all to the same central decimal file; there are no lot-file or presidential-library citations among them.
- The bundled citation index has citations for 33 of the 41 volumes and finds seven collections cited anywhere in their notes (twelve citations in all), none specific to recognition: Inter-American Affairs subject files, lot 53 D 26 (identifier 2108774, entry A1 1130, 1949 to 1953, unrestricted); records of meetings and visits of foreign dignitaries, lot 53 D 444 (2168520, A1 1197, 1949 to 1952, restricted possibly); National Security Council policy-paper records, lot 66 D 95 (2839192, A1 1586E, 1947 to 1979, restricted in part); a Far Eastern political file, lot 56 D 625 (2092759, A1 1207, 1948 to 1955, unrestricted); two lots the data names but cannot resolve to an Archives series (53 D 250, Under Secretary's Meetings, and 60 D 137, NAC Files); and the Roosevelt Library at Hyde Park. This channel is thin for the question.

The offline archival data barely reaches before 1940, so the thin pre-war roadmap above is a limit of the data, not a finding about the Archives.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *The Latin American side.* FRUS prints United States papers. The Mexican declaration of 1930 now called the Estrada Doctrine (that Mexico grants no recognition and only keeps or withdraws its diplomats) is mentioned in about thirty documents in eighteen volumes, 1931 to 1977; the mentions I looked at are United States descriptions of it, and the count says the doctrine is discussed, not that its text is printed. I searched the whole library for the phrase, with the phrase “Department of State” as a positive control (nearly 94,000 documents in 552 of the 553 volumes) and a meaningless string as a negative control (none). What foreign ministries said and decided is on shelves in the capitals concerned, which the offline data does not locate. (b) *Anything after 1980.* The library holds only fourteen volumes dated 1981 or later and one of them is on Latin America (South America, 1981 to 1988); none covers Central America, the Caribbean or Mexico after 1980, so the Haitian coup of 1991 and later cases cannot be answered here, and a search for the full names of Haiti's 1991 military leader and of its ousted president (Raoul Cédras; Jean-Bertrand Aristide) returned nothing, which tells us about the library and not about the world. (c) *Before 1895.* The library has 57 volumes for 1861 to 1894 and a loose search (recognition, together with revolution, provisional government or *de facto*, together with a Latin American country) finds about 450 documents in 55 of them; I read only two, on Mexico in 1877 and 1878. This is a thread not followed, not an absence. (d) *Whether the regimes kept their pledges, and what recognition changed.* The papers record the Department's view at the time; following a pledge forward needs a separate pass. (e) *The Department's own study of recognition.* The 1948 planning paper relies on a 1947 internal study of recognition practice that the editors say is not printed (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7); the Archives shelf for it is not identified in the data. The memo has the exact figures behind every number in this report.

**Scope.** This is the full library of 553 volumes; I took about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes from 1895 to 1950 under the editors' own headings on revolutions, coups and recognition, and about 450 documents in 20 later Western Hemisphere volumes by search, and I read the documents the collection holds. I left out volume front matter and editorial notes throughout, and I did not use the library's summaries or readers' notes.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as “by force”.** If you mean any change of government outside the constitution's own procedure, as the editors' chapter headings do, the set includes bloodless cases such as Brazil in 1937; if you mean only violent seizures, those drop out and the 1960s weigh more heavily. I used the wider rule.
- **What counts as “recognition”.** The Bogotá conference of 1948 deliberately avoided the word and spoke of continuing or resuming relations. If only the formal act counts, the story ends in 1948; I counted any act the papers treat as recognition, resumption of relations or accreditation of an ambassador.
- **Which countries count as Latin America.** I included Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean republics and South America, including Cuba, Haiti, Panama and the Dominican Republic, where treaties gave the United States special rights. If you leave out those protectorate cases, the Cuban material of 1933 and 1934 goes and the Secretary of State's 1930 remark about treaty exceptions loses its point.
- **The period.** The asks change by decade (property and debts before 1930, elections and constitutional title in between, the Axis in 1943 to 1945, Communism and the inter-American criteria after 1960). If you want one window, say which; I covered 1877 to 1973.

## Findings

### 1. The inherited test was control, consent and the treaties, and recognition came with a bill (1877 to 1913)

In 1877 the Department told its minister in Mexico that it would wait before recognizing Porfirio Díaz until it was sure his election was approved by the Mexican people and that his government would keep its treaties, and that it wanted a guarantee of frontier order as a condition before recognition, not a hope after it (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222). It recognized him ten months later, saying that Mexico's complaint that non-recognition itself blocked a settlement was reason enough to stop waiting (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1878, Document 344, printed as No. 339, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344). By 1900 the rule was in the form later planners would quote (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530). In Nicaragua in 1911 the list of what the new government had pledged, and what the United States expected next, was set out for the incoming minister. The words *de facto* appear in about one chapter document in fifteen, so the idea is everywhere in these files.

> “the rule of the United States is to defer recognition of another executive in its place until it shall appear that it is in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority, and that it is in a position to fulfill all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state under treaties and international law.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530

> “the Government of the United States before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico waited to be assured that his administration was approved by the Mexican people, and was possessed of due stability and animated by a disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1878, Document 344, printed as No. 339, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344

> “The Government of Nicaragua is to proceed at the earliest possible date to the signature of a convention with the United States which shall authorize the contemplated bankers’ loan contract, the terms of the latter to be approved thereafter by both Governments, and to make provision, among other things, for the service of the loan secured by a certain percentage of the customs receipts of the Republic collected in a manner satisfactory to both Governments.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Document 933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933

### 2. Wilson and after: the government should be constitutional, and sometimes elected first (1913 to 1928)

Wilson's statement of March 1913 said cooperation required government “based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force” (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). The August 1913 message to Congress carried the terms put to Huerta's Mexico: an armistice, an early free election in which Huerta would not run, and acceptance of the result by all parties, with a pledge to recognize the administration so chosen (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). Lansing refused to deal with Tinoco in Costa Rica unless a government was elected legally and installed under the constitution (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379). Colby held back from Bolivia's junta until elections and tied the timing to Argentina and Brazil (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume I, Document 387, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d387; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238). Hughes made Mexico's recognition follow a treaty on property (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). The Ecuador papers show how long recognition could be kept in suspense: the regime of 1925 was dealt with only as de facto authorities and received full recognition three years later (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1925, Volume II, Document 54, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1925v02/d54; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1928, Volume II, Document 741, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1928v02/d741).

> “the desire which this Government has of seeing the will of the people prevail in governmental matters in Costa Rica has forced it to the conclusion that no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379

> “has now determined to extend recognition to the existing Government of Bolivia, not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238

> “In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344

### 3. A treaty rule for Central America, enforced and then dropped (1923 to 1936)

The 1923 General Treaty of Peace and Amity, which the United States did not sign, barred its five Central American signatories from recognizing governments that took power by coup or revolution until freely elected representatives had reorganized the country, and barred recognition even of an elected president who had led the coup or held high office within the previous six months. Hughes announced in 1923 that the United States, though not a party, was “in the most hearty accord” with it (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). In September 1930 the Secretary of State cited that statement while resuming relations with the new governments of Argentina, Peru and Bolivia, saying he was following the regular rules of international law, with tests of control, absence of resistance, and a promise to meet obligations and hold elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). In December 1931 the Department barred General Martínez of El Salvador under the treaty (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232), and recognized him in January 1934, just over two years later, once El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced it and the neighbors had recognized him (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199; Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 255, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d255; Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 257, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d257). In April 1936 the Department decided to stop being guided by the treaty (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112).

> “will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375

> “The United States Government, therefore, will not recognize General Martínez as President of Salvador.”—Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232

> “the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America, nor should it endeavor to utilize that Treaty as justification for any other action it may take or fail to take”—Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112

### 4. Cuba, the Good Neighbor and the wartime consultation test (1933 to 1946)

In November 1933 the Department named its criteria for a Cuban government: popular support and the ability to maintain law and order (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 508, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d508). In January 1934 the President decided that recognition could be promised to no one in advance (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 85, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d85), and nine days later the Secretary ordered formal recognition of the Cuban government (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 103, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d103). In the war the Department added a hemispheric test: consult the other American republics and ask whether Axis influence helped the coup (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 334, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d334; Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486; Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 388, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d388). In April 1944 Hull relayed a British suggestion that Perón be told the road to recognition would open with collaboration against Axis activities, new cabinet ministers and a date for elections (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 226, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d226). Argentina declared war and adhered to the Mexico City act in the spring of 1945, the Department proposed that the republics recognize it on 9 April (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 251, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d251), and the Secretary told the President the step would be announced at noon that day (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 254, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d254). A Department officer explained El Salvador's recognition in 1945 by saying recognition could not be used as a weapon to impose democracy (Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 883, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d883), and in 1946 the criteria given for Bolivia were public order, stability, popular support and intention to meet obligations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The American Republics, Volume XI, Document 322, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d322).

> “This Government will recognize a Cuban provisional Government which is substantially supported by the Cuban people and is able to maintain law, order and the normal functions of Government.”—Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 85, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d85

> “the road would be open for reconsideration of our attitude on recognition: 1. Thorough and effective collaboration to foil Axis activities in the Argentine, particularly those relating to espionage, smuggling, and telecommunications; 2. Appointment of new Cabinet Ministers, particularly in Foreign Relations and Interior, that would inspire confidence of collaboration for hemisphere security; and 3. Fixing of a date for general elections.”—Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 226, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d226

> “There are certain well established requirements of international law for recognition: (a) Control of the machinery of Government; (b) General popular support without active opposition; and (c) willingness and ability to observe international obligations. Due to the war, the Committee for Political Defense of Montevideo established a fourth—whereby when a Government came into power through force, the countries of the hemisphere would consult to determine whether its assumption to power demonstrated direct or indirect Axis influence.”—Foreign Relations of the United States: Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 883, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d883

### 5. In 1948 recognition was separated from approval, and from leverage

At Bogotá in 1948 the American republics adopted a resolution on continuing diplomatic relations, and the Department's own paper of that spring made it policy that recognition implies neither approval nor disapproval and is not to be withheld as a weapon save in extreme cases (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). Within months it was applied to Peru, where the memorandum notes the resolution avoids the formal word recognition (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89), and to Venezuela, where Kennan's planning staff advised recognizing after a decent interval while stating that force as an instrument of political change is deplorable (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115). The Army's view that recognition was favorable for oil and the military mission went to the Acting Secretary (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 118, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d118), and a circular reminded the republics of their commitments to democracy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d119). A Panama note of 1949 shows the form: assurances first, recognition second (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 450, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d450). By 1951 the Secretary could describe the criteria “applied in other recent cases” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1951, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 688, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v02/d688).

> “The recognition policy of the Department should be based upon the three following factors, any or all of which would be applied in a given case according to U.S. national interests: (a) de facto control of the territory and the administrative machinery of State, including the maintenance of public order; (b) the ability and willingness of a government to discharge its international obligations; (c) general acquiescence of the people of a country in the government in power.”—Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7

> “non-recognition is not a suitable weapon for influencing the course of internal events in Venezuela at this time.”—Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115

> “From appearances to date the Junta has met the criteria which the United States has applied in other recent cases involving the recognition of Latin American governments which have come to power through irregular procedures. It has established its authority over Bolivian territory, with the substantial acquiescence of the people, it has declared its intent to honor Bolivia’s international obligations, and its assumption of power has not been due to any external influences.”—Foreign Relations of the United States, 1951, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 688, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v02/d688

### 6. From prompt recognition to suspended relations and back (1955 to 1973)

In the 1950s the Department recognized quickly: Argentina's junta in 1955 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Central and South America, Volume VII, Document 193, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v07/d193), Colombia's in 1957 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Central and South America, Volume VII, Document 465, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v07/d465) and Cuba's provisional government in January 1959, on the day the Secretary asked (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). Under Kennedy it tried the opposite. Before Peru's election it considered getting neighbors to say they would not recognize a takeover (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 416, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d416); after the coup it suspended relations and sought guarantees (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423). In the Dominican Republic in October 1963 it set conditions, including elections within a year (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359), then recognized in December because the prospects for further influence by non-recognition were judged “virtually nil” (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). Honduras went unrecognized from 3 October to 14 December 1963, ten weeks (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 72, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d72). Johnson's Department recognized Bolivia's junta on private pledges (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152), and in 1966 treated Argentina's coup under the inter-American criteria of the 1965 Rio conference (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 139, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d139). By 1973, after the Chilean coup, it chose not to be first and to say it recognized governments, not heads of government; a State Department participant, Kubisch, called too hasty recognition of military regimes, Brazil for example, among the biggest recent errors (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348).

> “1. Guarantee of continued civil liberties. 2. Guarantee of free and fair elections, preferably before June 9 but at no later date, with full rights to all parties under Constitution, and with guarantee that Armed Forces will respect results those elections. 3. Offer by Junta, as indication of good faith, to invite OAS observers during electoral processes. This concession would particularly strengthen Junta’s position with respect continental opinion and would also facilitate US recognition.”—Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423

> “The classical criteria for recognition (i.e., general control of the country and pledge to honor international obligations) have been met. We are still awaiting public affirmation of the OAS criteria: respect for human rights, peaceful settlement of disputes, and eventual return to constitutional government.”—Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 139, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d139

## How this was done

I started from the editors' own chapter headings, not from modern phrasing, because their titles repeat a formula (“Revolution in…”, “Recognition of…”, “Refusal of the United States to recognize…”). I listed every chapter title across the 553 volumes that names recognition, revolution, coups or de facto governments for a Latin American country, kept the chapters that concern the recognition decision or the revolution it answered, and took all the documents in them (about 2,700 documents in 41 volumes, 1895 to 1950). Later volumes are not organized that way, so for them I searched the text of 20 Western Hemisphere volumes for papers that mention recognition together with a coup, a junta or *de facto* power (450 documents) and read those that carry the decisions, and I ran a looser search of 1861 to 1894 without following it. The search tool matches word stems, so “recognize”, “recognized” and “recognition” are found together; I therefore checked each phrase I count against the actual text (the words “international obligations” turn up in about a hundred chapter documents, nearly all in exactly that form and every one with at most minor variations), and the word-family “recognition” appears in about three of every eight chapter documents against about one in ten of the other documents in the same volumes, so it measures the topic rather than the corpus. I read in full 55 of the 56 documents in the collection and part of the other; the exact figures, the queries and the quotations are in the memo. Before finishing I checked that every quotation in this report and every excerpt in the collection matches its document, and that every document this report cites is in the collection.
