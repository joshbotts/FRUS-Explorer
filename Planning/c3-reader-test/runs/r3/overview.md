# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The published diplomatic papers answer the question well, and the answer is that there was no single rule. The Department's baseline, written out in 1900, was to recognize whoever actually held power, with the assent of the people and without substantial resistance, and able to meet its international obligations: its instruction says the policy was "to base the recognition of a foreign government solely on its de facto ability to hold the reins of administrative power" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Acting Secretary Hill to Minister Hart, September 8, 1900, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530). Around that baseline the Department added conditions that changed with the administration and the country. Wilson added a constitutional test in Latin America: "Cooperation is possible only when supported at every turn by the orderly processes of just government based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, circular telegram of March 12, 1913, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). In Mexico, 1920 to 1923, the condition was a written guarantee of American property: "when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Secretary Hughes's statement of June 8, 1921, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). In Central America the Department followed a 1923 treaty by which the five republics refused to recognize governments that took power by coup. For South America in 1930 it returned to the plain test of control and no active resistance, and for Cuba in 1933 it named two criteria, popular support and the ability to keep order. During the Second World War it began to consult the other republics first, and after 1948 its staff tried to separate recognition from approval. In the 1960s the Department mostly recognized, but only after it had extracted private assurances about elections, communism or peaceful relations with neighbours. The set that carries this is 38 documents in 25 volumes, read from a library of 553 volumes, and it is a selection of the best evidence, not a census.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Two channels, which are kept apart and never added together. *Where the printed documents came from:* for 37 of the 38 documents the index records a source note (the 1900 instruction has none), and nearly all of those came out of the State Department's central files (Record Group 59), cited by decimal file number: the Central Decimal File for 1910 to 1963 (for example the Mexico political file 812.00 and the Cuba file 837.00), then the Department's Central Files for 1964 to 1966 and its Central Foreign Policy File for 1973. Only a few came from other shelves: the Eisenhower Library's Whitman File for the 1960 El Salvador decision; the Johnson Library's National Security File for Peru in 1968 and Bolivia in 1964 (the catalogue lists the Latin America Country Files, 1963 to 1969, National Archives identifier 567985, within the National Security Files, identifier 567979); and the Nixon materials' National Security Council files for Chile in 1973. The app's reference data names the Central Decimal File and the later Central Files but gives no National Archives identifier, entry number, dates or access status for them, and it carries no access status for the library collections either; those have to be got from the National Archives. *What the editors cited without printing:* in the 38 documents' own footnotes there are nine citations in seven documents: six to further decimal files (for example 723.00 for Peru in 1962 and 819.01 for Panama in 1949), two to the Nixon materials' National Security Council files, and one to a lot file: the Bureau of Inter-American Affairs' Office of Central American and Panamanian Affairs "Subject and Country Files" (lot 63 D 127; Record Group 59; entry A1 3157; National Archives identifier 2363840; 1955 to 1963; about three and a half linear feet; the National Archives lists access as "Restricted, possibly"). Across the sixteen whole volumes from 1911 to 1945 that carry these documents, the footnotes cite the Department's decimal files for Latin American political affairs about 3,400 times, out of about 15,700 footnotes that name a file class; the largest numbers are the Mexico, Cuba, Nicaragua and Costa Rica political files (see the memo for the counts, which are whole-volume, not narrowed to recognition).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Four things. (a) *A single list of conditions.* I read the Department's own statements across eight decades and they do not agree on one list; the nearest is a 1948 staff paper approved by the Secretary, which is a statement of intended policy and not a record of every case. The searches were for the phrases "policy of recognition", "recognition policy" and "question of recognition", run across the whole library, and the search was shown to work by two controls in the same sitting: the phrase "Department of State" turned up in 552 of the 553 volumes, and a nonsense word turned up in none. (b) *The other side.* The new governments' own arguments, and the foreign ministries' reports, are not here; the footnotes of the 25 volumes cite a foreign archive once in about 19,600 footnotes. The shelf for these is each country's own foreign ministry archive, which the app's reference data does not locate. (c) *The working papers of decisions.* FRUS prints the decision memoranda and instructions; the staff studies and the Department's daily files behind them are on the decimal-file shelf named above. (d) *Cases not read.* I found chapter headings for recognition of the governments of Bolivia (1921), Peru (1920), Guatemala (1922 and 1931), Ecuador (1928), Argentina (1944 and 1945), El Salvador (1945) and Haiti (1950) but did not read their documents, and the findings below rest on the cases that were read. A search of the 1900 to 1912 volumes (about 13,600 documents) shows that FRUS prints earlier cases too (Honduras 1907, Haiti 1908 and 1911, Nicaragua 1910), which I did not follow.

**Scope.** Twenty-five volumes of the 553-volume library, from 1900 to 1973, chosen because the editors' chapter headings and a search of the text pointed to them; 38 documents, all dated, with front matter and editorial notes excluded, and no second edition set beside its first edition. Every number here is conditional on this library, which holds all 553 volumes of the series as indexed on 25 October 2026 but which I searched by topic, not exhaustively.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "came to power by force".** If you mean only coups and revolutions, the Mexican cases of 1920 to 1923 and some irregular successions drop out; if you include forced resignations and irregular successions (as the El Salvador case of 1931 did, where the question was whether the Vice President had helped the coup), the set grows. I included both and marked the Mexican and Salvadoran cases as such.
- **Whether "recognition" includes the quieter forms.** After 1948 the Department increasingly avoided the word and spoke of continuing or resuming relations; if you want only the word, the 1949 to 1973 cases largely disappear. I included both, and the 1973 Chile and Afghanistan documents are marked as showing this shift.
- **Whether to count what was asked in private.** Public tests and private assurances differ (Bolivia 1964, El Salvador 1960). I counted both and labelled each.
- **Whether to reach before 1900 or to count the protectorates (Cuba, Haiti, Dominican Republic) separately,** where treaties changed the rules. I included Cuba and Haiti and did not go before 1900.

## Findings

### 1. The baseline of 1900, and how Wilson changed it, 1900 to 1919

The 1900 instruction treats recognition as a matter of fact: control, the assent of the people, no substantial resistance, and ability to meet obligations. The Department could also hold recognition back until it had a promise: in 1911 it told its minister in Haiti to take no step toward recognizing a new government until he could "communicate satisfactory assurances to this end to the Department" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Secretary of State to the American minister, August 10, 1911, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d353). Wilson's administration then made the test constitutional. Its instruction to the minister in Costa Rica in 1917 was that "no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Secretary of State to Minister Hale, February 9, 1917, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379). Before Wilson, in February 1913, Taft's Secretary of State had already separated working relations with those in control of Mexico from the formal act of recognition, which he said was to be avoided for the moment (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Secretary Knox to the Embassy in Mexico, February 28, 1913, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d894).

### 2. Recognition as a bargain: Mexico, 1920 to 1923

The Department would not infer recognition from dealings: "Recognition cannot be accomplished by inference merely but by the full and formal entrance into international relations through the public action of the respective executives of the two countries" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume III, Secretary Colby to the Chargé in Mexico, May 25, 1920, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v03/d199). In 1921 it proposed a treaty of amity and commerce protecting titles gained before the Mexican constitution of 1917, and President Harding told General Obregón that "In entering into this treaty with you recognition would be effected, ipso facto" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Under Secretary Fletcher to the Chargé in Mexico, enclosing President Harding's letter, July 21, 1921, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d349). Obregón replied that he could not sign a treaty before recognition. Hughes's 1923 briefing to the American commissioners explained the Department's reasoning: Carranza had been recognized on assurances that were later broken, so a clear understanding was sought first (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Secretary of State to Commissioner Warren, May 8, 1923, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d481).

### 3. The Central American treaty rule and the Hoover-Stimson practice, 1926 to 1934

In 1926 Secretary Kellogg wrote that the United States "has adopted the principles of that Treaty as its policy in the future recognition of Central American Governments", the treaty being the five republics' general treaty of 1923, and refused to recognize General Chamorro in Nicaragua (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1926, Volume II, Secretary of State to the Minister in Nicaragua, January 22, 1926, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1926v02/d476). In September 1930 Secretary Stimson recognized the new governments of Argentina, Peru and Bolivia on a plainer test: "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, press release of September 17, 1930, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). The two rules ran side by side. In El Salvador, in December 1931, the Department asked its minister whether the Vice President had helped the coup, because "if the Vice President was a leader in the coup d’état or revolution, under the terms of the treaty of 1923 he could not be recognized" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Secretary of State to the Minister in El Salvador, December 7, 1931, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d208).

### 4. Cuba, 1933 to 1934: popular support and Latin American opinion

For Cuba the Department stated its criteria in a form that is close to a checklist: "We have maintained that the criteria of Cuban recognition are: (1) popular support, and (2) ability to maintain law and order and carry on the functions incumbent upon any stable government" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Acting Secretary of State to the Secretary of State, November 28, 1933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d508). Ambassador Welles used the same criteria to argue for delay, writing in October 1933 that "if we recognize a government now which does not possess at least a considerable measure of popular support we postpone a return by Cuba to normal and stable conditions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Ambassador Welles to the Secretary of State, October 5, 1933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d430), and in January 1934 the Department told the British that recognition of the Grau regime would be "a great mistake" and that the sentiment of the Latin American governments was against it (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Acting Secretary of State to Mr. Caffery, January 8, 1934, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d75).

### 5. Consultation, and recognition without approval, 1943 to 1949

In December 1943 the American republics' Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense resolved that they should not recognize a government set up by force before consulting one another, and Hull accepted that on December 27 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d34). It was applied at once to the Bolivian junta, which the United States would not recognize pending consultation, and a month later Hull reported that "All of the nineteen republics participating in the consultations and exchange of information regarding the Bolivian revolution have refused recognition to the Junta in public statements" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, circular telegram of January 28, 1944, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d403). In March 1948 the Policy Planning Staff proposed a worldwide policy in which "Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7).

### 6. Assurances and elections, and the retreat from the word, 1957 to 1973

By the 1960s the question was what to ask for privately. In July 1962 the Department told its embassy in Peru to seek a restatement of civil liberties, free elections and observers from the Organization of American States, and added that "Some civilianizing of Cabinet, provided civilian members were known for dedication to constitutional processes, would make our recognition easier, but our view is not to insist on this as sine qua non of recognition" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423). In December 1964, for Bolivia, the Acting Secretary reported that "Barrientos and his principal advisers have privately pledged that communist influence will be reduced and eventually eliminated, and that they will not reestablish relations with Cuba or Czechoslovakia" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152). An instruction to the Embassy in Afghanistan in 1973, which states the Department's general practice and is not itself about Latin America, shows where this ended: "the Department’s policy is to minimize to the greatest extent possible recognition questions" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–8, Documents on South Asia, 1973–1976, Document 6, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve08/d6).

## How this was done

The question was scoped from the editors' own formula, the chapter headings in the older volumes (seventeen of them, concerning nine Latin American countries, carry the word recognition in their titles, as in "Recognition of the Leguia government by the United States"), and from a text search of the library's index for recognition in the same document as a revolution, coup or junta, country by country and period by period, followed by reading the documents themselves. The word "recognition" alone is not a good guide here: it occurs in about one document in eighteen across the whole library and in about one in eighteen among the 41 volumes titled for the American republics (the same rate), so it measures the corpus, and it was used only with a country or a regime beside it. The search index treats words with the same root as the same word, so each phrase was checked in the documents' actual text: on a sample of forty documents each, the word root and the control phrase both matched forty of forty. All 38 cited documents were retrieved whole; three were first read in part and then read to the end, while for the fourth, the 1923 Hughes briefing, only the first 9,500 of its 33,346 characters were read. Archival leads come from the app's reference data, not from the National Archives itself. Quotations are given in the documents' own spelling and were checked against the document text. I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo verified against its document, and that every document cited in this overview is in the collection.
