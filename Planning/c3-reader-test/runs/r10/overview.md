# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** For more than a century the Department of State applied one working test to governments that seized power in Latin America, restated in nearly the same words from 1900 to 1976: recognize the new government once it holds the machinery of the state without substantial resistance, has at least the acquiescence of its people, and is able and willing to meet the country's international obligations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530; 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375; 1948, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7; 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 2, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p2/d34). Around that test Washington repeatedly added demands. Seward in the 1860s and Wilson after 1913 required that a government installed by force be ratified by its own people through constitutional means, and from 1923 to 1934 the United States applied a Central American treaty that barred recognizing a coup government until freely elected representatives had constitutionally reorganized the country (1868, Part II, Document 499, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1868p2/d499; 1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378; 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345; 1936, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). It made recognition wait on concessions: border policing from Díaz's Mexico, a customs convention from Haiti, a property-rights treaty and claims conventions from Obregón's Mexico (1877, Document 226, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d226; 1914, Document 544, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d544; 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). In 1943–1945 it recognized no government installed by force until the American republics had consulted on whether it honored the hemisphere's wartime commitments, and it held the Bolivian and Argentine regimes to concrete action against the Axis (1943, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33; 1944, Volume VII, Document 227, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d227; 1945, Volume IX, Document 250, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d250). From 1948, the year of the Bogotá conference, it held that recognition implied no approval and that withholding it was a feeble sanction, while still asking new regimes for assurances on their obligations, private capital, communism, elections and civil liberties (1949, Volume II, Document 457, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d457; 1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 327, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d327; 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 135, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d135); by the 1970s it stopped speaking of recognition and decided only whether to continue relations (1969–1976, Volume E–16, Document 148, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve16/d148; 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, Document 137, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d137). The answer rests on a set of about 2,100 documents in 115 of the series' 553 volumes, all of which are indexed: about 1,300 that the editors filed under 56 chapters titled for a recognition question (1895–1950) and about 1,100 in Latin American chapters whose own wording speaks of recognizing a government (1862–1980), the two overlapping by some 300. Fifty-two documents carry the argument and form the accompanying collection: 44 from this set and 8 more reached by reading its chapters and cross-references (exact counts and their queries are in the memo).

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series are indexed, and the set is the documents that the editors placed in one of 56 chapters on recognizing a Latin American government (1895–1950) or that, in any Latin American chapter, speak of recognizing a government (1862–1980): about 2,100 documents in 115 volumes. Front matter, editorial notes, the index's chapter-heading records and the second editions of two volumes were excluded.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "came to power by force."** I included every change the documents or the editors treat as raising a recognition question: coups, revolutions, juntas and forced resignations. If you also include self-coups by sitting presidents (Vargas in Brazil, 1937), the Department's own ruling that these needed no recognition becomes part of the subject; if you exclude them, the set stays with new regimes only.
- **Recognition, or the continuation of relations.** From 1948, and openly from the early 1970s, Washington often continued relations without any act of recognition. Counting those decisions keeps the 1950–1980 record (about 200 documents); excluding them leaves the story ending around 1950, where the editors stopped titling chapters for recognition.
- **Conditions asked, or criteria weighed.** Some documents record explicit preconditions put to a regime (Haiti 1914, Mexico 1921, Argentina 1945); others record tests Washington applied to itself (popular support in Cuba, 1933). I report both and say which is which; if you want only preconditions, the collection narrows to the third, fourth and sixth findings.
- **Where Latin America ends.** I used the twenty republics (Haiti and the Dominican Republic among them) and Central America as a region, but also kept what the editors placed in volumes titled for Latin America, which brings in nine documents on Guyana, the Bahamas and the eastern Caribbean (1964–1980). None is in the collection; excluding them changes no finding.
- **Whose recognition.** I kept the decisions of other governments only where U.S. policy was tied to them (the Central American states under the 1923 treaty, the consulting republics of 1943–1945). Widening to Latin American recognition practice in general would bring in the Estrada doctrine and the Betancourt position, which the documents mention but do not follow.

## Findings

### 1. One working test ran through the whole record: control, consent, and the country's obligations

The same three questions recur in instructions written seventy-six years apart. Acting Secretary Hill set them out in 1900 when Vice President Marroquín seized the executive in Colombia: does the new authority hold the machinery of the state, does it govern with the assent of the people and without substantial resistance, and can it meet the country's obligations? Stimson applied them in September 1930 to the provisional governments of Argentina, Peru and Bolivia, adding the promise of elections; the Policy Planning Staff codified them in March 1948 as the Department's worldwide policy; and Ambassador Hill restated them to the Argentine military a month before the 1976 coup. The variations are telling at the edges: Roosevelt's Cuban policy of 1933 turned the people's assent into a demand for demonstrated popular support, and the Legal Adviser ruled in 1938 that a recognized government that overthrew its own constitution needed no new recognition at all (1933, Volume V, Document 502, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d502; 1937, Volume V, Document 340, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1937v05/d340).

> "the rule of the United States is to defer recognition of another executive in its place until it shall appear that it is in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority, and that it is in a position to fulfill all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state under treaties and international law." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530

> "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375

> "at present it is the US Government’s policy to recognize a government that effectively exercises power and responsibly discharges its international obligations." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–11, Part 2, Document 34, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p2/d34

### 2. Twice Washington made constitutional origin the test, and both times it gave the test up

In 1866 Seward told the minister in Peru that revolutions in republican states would not be accepted until the people had adopted them by organic law, and in 1868 he spelled out the one thing required of a government installed by force (1866, Part II, Document 502, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1866p2/d502); by January 1877 Fish was ready to authorize recognizing Porfirio Díaz once he had no important adversary in arms and could be regarded as the actual ruler of Mexico (1877, Document 216, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d216). Wilson's statement of March 1913 revived the idea for the hemisphere; the terms offered to Huerta that August (an armistice, a free election in which Huerta would not stand) and Lansing's 1917 refusal to recognize any government in Costa Rica not shown to be elected by legal and constitutional means, followed by word that Tinoco would not be recognized even if elected, applied it (1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7; 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028; 1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378). For Central America the rule became treaty law: the 1907 conventions and Article II of the 1923 General Treaty of Peace and Amity, which Hughes adopted as U.S. policy although the United States was not a party to it. Stimson invoked it against Martínez in El Salvador in 1931; the United States recognized him in January 1934, a day after Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua did, and in 1936 the Department formally stopped applying the article (1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232; 1934, Volume V, Document 257, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d257). In the recognition documents of 1930–1942 about one in four invoke the treaty rule, against about one in twenty before 1930 and almost none after 1942.

> "What we do require, and all that we do require, is when a change of administration has been made, not by peaceful constitutional processes, but by force, that then the new administration shall be sanctioned by the formal acquiescence and acceptance of the people." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1868, Part II, Document 499, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1868p2/d499

> "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345

> "we are no longer warranted in invoking the Treaty as a reason for denying recognition to any régime in Central America" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1936, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112

### 3. Recognition was also a price: specific concessions were demanded before it was given (1877–1923)

Where the United States had direct stakes, recognition waited on terms. Díaz's Mexico was nonetheless told in 1877 that before recognition the United States would expect action against raids into Texas and repeal of the free-trade zone on the border, a condition first conveyed under Fish and kept under Evarts. In 1903 the minister in Santo Domingo told the Morales government he would recognize it once it pledged to honor every engagement made with the legation and with earlier administrations (1904, Document 227, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d227). Bryan made the recognition of Théodore in Haiti in 1914 wait on a commission empowered to settle a customs convention, the American railroad and bank disputes, protection of foreign interests and a promise never to lease territory to a European power. Hughes made Obregón's recognition concurrent with a treaty protecting American property titles; in 1923 relations were renewed on the commissioners' agreements and followed by two claims conventions (1923, Volume II, Document 488, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d488). Mentions of American claims or expropriation run at about one recognition document in ten in 1913–1929 and almost nowhere else.

> "the United States, prior to deciding in favor of official recognition of the Diaz government, would expect that efficient measures would be taken toward checking inroads into their States and territories, and toward the repeal of the “Zona Libre.”" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 226, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d226

> "recognition by the United States will be granted him as provisional president when a commission to be named by the Haitian Government with full powers to act upon certain questions of interest to this Government and to the Republic of Haiti is in position to give necessary assurances." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 544, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d544

> "In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344

### 4. In wartime the hemisphere decided together, and the test was the war (1943–1945)

After the Bolivian coup of December 1943 Hull put hemispheric security first (1943, Volume V, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486), and the Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense, at Montevideo, recommended that no government instituted by force be recognized until the American governments had consulted. All nineteen consulting republics refused the Bolivian junta (1944, Volume VII, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d403), and by June 1944 Hull was circulating a report that the regime had removed the pro-Nazi party leaders, expropriated Axis firms and expelled Axis aliens, and that recognition would help (1944, Volume VII, Document 432, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d432). The ambassador in Buenos Aires argued that recognition was a more powerful instrument against Farrell's Argentina than was generally appreciated, and in March 1945 the ambassadors of twelve American republics agreed at Blair House on a written program after which Argentina would be recognized. In the recognition documents of 1943–1948, about one in four mention consultation with the other republics and about as many mention the Axis, against a few in a hundred in other periods.

> "they do not proceed to the recognition of a new government instituted by force, before consulting among themselves for the purpose of determining whether this government complies with the Inter-American undertakings for the defense of the continent" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1943, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33

> "we do not intend to recognize until there is concrete evidence in the form of significant anti-Nazi measures that the Government is no longer linked to the Extreme Nationalist and pro-German elements." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1944, Volume VII, Document 227, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d227

> "(a) Declared by decree the existence of a state of war with Germany and Japan; (b) Expressed conformity with the principles and declarations of the Final Act and complied with such principles and declarations; (c) Signed the Final Act of Mexico City at the Pan American Union." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1945, Volume IX, Document 250, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d250

### 5. After Bogotá, recognition was said to imply no approval, and withholding it was judged a feeble weapon (1948–1960)

The Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948 reduced the test to three factors and declared that recognition implied no approval; the Bogotá conference adopted the same principle for the hemisphere (1948, Volume IX, Document 74, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d74). Kennan applied it to the Venezuelan junta that December (1948, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115), and Daniels's review after the 1949 Paraguayan coup judged suspension of relations a weak and interventionist sanction. The asks did not disappear: Panama's new government in 1949 was checked for outside intervention, popular support, civil liberties and its international commitments (1949, Volume II, Document 448, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d448); Batista in 1952 was recognized on statements about obligations, private capital and communism; and the 1960 note recognizing El Salvador's junta rested in its own text on assurances of constitutional rule, human rights and free elections (1958–1960, Volume V, Microfiche Supplement, Document ES–43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05mSupp/es_d43). In the recognition documents of 1949–1960 about one in four mention international obligations, against about one in ten before 1949, and about three in ten mention elections, against fewer than one in ten before 1949.

> "10. Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs. 11. Recognition should not be withheld as a political weapon or to express moral censure except in extreme cases when U.S. national interests would be served thereby." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7

> "Suspension is a feeble sanction, is not clearly a deterrent to other military coups, and has not been shown in the past to have made any lasting contribution to establishment of representative government" — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, Volume II, Document 457, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d457

> "The Batista regime has formally requested our recognition and has made satisfactory public and private statements with regard to Cuban intention to fulfill its international obligations; its attitude towards private capital; and its intention to take steps to curtail international communist activities in Cuba." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 327, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d327

### 6. In the 1960s the asks became elections and civil liberties; in the 1970s the Department stopped speaking of recognition

Before Peru's 1962 election the Kennedy administration drew up contingency plans to withhold recognition and aid from any government the military imposed (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 417, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d417), did so after the July coup, and reported that the pressure had moved the junta. It withheld recognition from the Dominican and Honduran regimes in the autumn of 1963 to press for a return to elected government, then recognized both in December, judging that further pressure would gain nothing (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). Under Johnson the Barrientos junta in Bolivia was recognized on its restoration of liberties and its promise of elections (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152), and after the 1966 Argentine coup Johnson approved seeking the customary assurances without insisting on an election date. After Peru's 1968 coup a senior official proposed that a coup should change the level of relations rather than end them (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512); Nixon told the National Security Council in 1969 that recognition should be automatic, so that Washington would not have to judge whether regimes were good or bad (1969–1976, Volume E–10, Document 14, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d14); and by 1978 the Department simply continued relations with Bolivia's new rulers, while in 1980 it chose instead to isolate the new military regime there over human rights, narcotics and the return to democracy. In the recognition documents after 1961 about three in ten mention civil liberties or human rights, against one in twenty or fewer before 1949. Communism is mentioned in about half of the recognition documents of 1949–1960 and two in five after 1961, but in a reading sample of fifteen such mentions only five bore on the recognition decision itself.

> "It seems clear that the sharp reaction of the United States and other Latin American countries has led the junta to take a position with respect to new elections, with respect to current civil rights, with respect to leaders of the previous government and of APRA, and with respect to the private business community and the Alliance for Progress more liberal and more in accordance with United States views than would otherwise have been the case." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421

> "we should seek the usual assurances regarding acceptance of international obligations, as well as respect for civil liberties and an early return to constitutional government, before recognizing. But we should not take too rigid a position on the scheduling of elections which might preclude recognition if we did not get the commitment." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 135, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d135

> "In recent years, the United States has moved away from the concept of recognition, focusing instead on whether diplomatic relations are to continue. This avoids the value judgments often attributed to “recognition” of a Government." — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume E–16, Document 148, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve16/d148

## How this was done

I worked from a copy of the FRUS Explorer index covering all 553 volumes, the TEI XML the index was built from, and the finding aids bundled with the app. I first read the editors' chapter headings across the series and kept the 56 chapters, 1895–1950, whose titles name the recognition, non-recognition or continued relations of a Latin American government; after 1950 the editors stopped using that formula, so I added every document in a Latin American chapter whose text uses one of 52 phrasings for recognizing a government. I tested that phrase family on an evenly spaced sample of 40: all 40 contain the wording literally once inflections are allowed (30 of the 40 in the exact base wording), and 35 of the 40 are about recognizing a government installed by force or irregularly; a looser family that only required the two words to stand near each other was right in 20 of 40 and was dropped. I counted condition words (obligations, elections, consultation, the Axis, communism, civil liberties, the treaty rule) in every document of the set and read samples of the hits to see whether they bore on the decision. On the XML, with the editors' footnotes separated from the document text, I counted spelling variants and found the footnotes carry little of the recognition language; I also found that the question's own phrase "by force" is barely commoner in the recognition chapters than in Latin American chapters generally (about three documents in two hundred against two in two hundred), while the documents' own terms coup d'état and de facto are six to nine times commoner there, so I searched on the documents' terms rather than the question's. Every quotation here was checked character for character against its document and confirmed to lie in the document's own text rather than in a footnote. The source notes and footnote citations were resolved against the bundled finding aids, which stop at the record group for the central files. The memo holds every query, count, sample and check.
