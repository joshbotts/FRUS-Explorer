# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series prints, in the Department of State's own words, the test it applied each time a Latin American government took power by arms, from Evarts's year-long wait before recognizing Porfirio Díaz in 1878 to the recognition of Nicaragua's provisional government in 1979, and it prints what the Department asked of the new government first. The constant core was three questions of fact: does the government hold the country, do the people acquiesce, and will it honour the republic's international obligations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1878, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344; 1895, Part I, Document 246, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d246; 1945, Volume IX, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170; 1950, Volume II, Document 478, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d478). Two further demands came and went: Wilson's requirement of a constitutional origin or a free election, stated as a rule in 1917 (1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378) and revived by Kennedy in 1962–63 (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363); and the Harding administration's demand that Mexico bind itself by treaty to protect American property before recognition (1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). From 1964 the Department increasingly arranged not to face the question at all (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 206, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d206; 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348). The set this rests on is nearly two thousand documents in fifty-seven volumes: the editors' own recognition chapters for seventy-four episodes between 1876 and 1950, and about one hundred and thirty single documents after 1950 found by search and read for relevance one by one.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** Came-from channel (where the printed documents were taken from): nearly all of them came out of one series, the Department of State's Central Decimal File, 1910–1963, in Record Group 59 at the National Archives at College Park, from the country classes for internal 'political affairs' and, about one time in five, from the class the Department's filing scheme labels 'Government', which is where it filed recognition. The bundled reference data carry no identifier, extent or access line for that series itself, so those are not stated here. For the cases before 1910 the bundle names the predecessor series: Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Mexico, 1823–1906 (NARA identifier 177380741), Diplomatic Instructions, Mexico, volume 19, 1873–1879 (149324017), Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Venezuela, 1835–1906 (177380736) and to Ecuador, 1848–1906 (177380753), all in Record Group 59 and open. Pointed-at channel (what the editors' footnotes cite without printing): the same decimal classes for Nicaragua, Bolivia, El Salvador, Haiti and Peru; the Bureau of Inter-American Affairs' Assistant Secretary's files, 1978–1981 (Record Group 59, entry UD-14D 61, identifier 27499857, 9 linear feet, fully restricted under the national-security exemption); and, across the whole of these fifty-seven volumes, the Assistant Secretary's subject files for 1949–1953 (entry A1 1130, identifier 2108774, 6 linear feet, unrestricted) and 1957–1959 (entry A1 1135, identifier 2108779, 7 linear feet, unrestricted), the Legal Adviser's records relating to Chile policy, 1964–1970 (entry UD-UP 313, identifier 26309351, 2 linear feet, fully restricted), and Deputy Secretary Christopher's records, 1975–1981 (entry P 14, identifier 1487627, 14 linear feet, partly restricted). After 1963 the notes of recognition themselves are, in the editors' words, "Attached but not printed"; their shelf is the Central Foreign Policy File, 1963–1973, under the subject-numeric category POL 16, which the Department's own scheme labels 'Independence. Recognition.' (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 141, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d141).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Three things. First, the inter-American texts the Department invoked are never printed: Article II of the 1923 General Treaty of Peace and Amity is quoted only inside a 1936 instruction, and the 1943 Montevideo Resolution XXII, the 1948 Bogotá Resolution XXXV and the 1965 Rio Resolution XXVI appear only in editors' footnotes (searches for each resolution number beside the recognition words across all 553 volumes found twenty, eight and three documents respectively; a nonsense control found none); the shelf is the published Final Acts of the Pan American Union and the Organization of American States. Second, the Latin American doctrines the Department argued with are named but not printed: the Tobar doctrine in eighty-eight documents, sixty-five of them in the Latin American volumes, the Estrada doctrine in twenty-eight; their texts are in Ecuadorean and Mexican foreign-ministry publications outside the bundle. Third, the practice before 1876: the editors headed no earlier Latin American chapter by recognition, though one hundred and nineteen documents of 1861–1875 pair the recognition words with 'de facto'; they were not read for this study and the 1860s cases (Mexico, Peru, the Dominican Republic) remain open.

**Scope.** Fifty-seven volumes of the full series of 553, holding nearly two thousand documents: every document in the seventy-four chapters the editors headed as the recognition of a Latin American government between 1876 and 1950 (the six largest 'political affairs' chapters on Mexico, Haiti and the Dominican Republic limited to documents that use the recognition words), plus about one hundred and thirty documents from 1950 onward in which the recognition words fall within twelve words of 'junta', 'coup', 'de facto', 'new government' or a like term, each read and kept only if it concerns a government in power. Front matter, editorial notes and the second editions were excluded; so were the 1961 planning for a Cuban exile 'provisional government', the Grenada and Suriname cases, and documents about recognizing governments outside the hemisphere.

## Decisions this needs from you

- Whether a disputed election counts as coming to power by force: the editors' chapters include Ecuador 1913, Peru 1919–20 and Honduras 1919, where the question was recognition after a contested vote; keeping them is the rule used here, and dropping them removes about sixty documents.
- Whether a government the Department chose to treat as a continuation (Brazil 1937, Bolivia 1970 and 1978) belongs in the set; it is kept here, because declining to raise the question was itself the decision.
- Whether recognition of an insurgent government not yet in power (the Cuban exiles of 1961, the Sandinista junta before Somoza left) is part of the question; it is excluded here, and including the Cuban planning would add about thirty-six documents.
- Whether to extend the study to 1861–1875, where the editors' headings do not help and the documents must be found by search.

## Findings

### Before Wilson, the test was possession, consent and obligations

The oldest instructions ask nothing about how the government came to power. In 1878 Evarts summed up what the United States had waited for before recognizing Díaz: it "waited to be assured that his administration was approved by the Mexican people, and was possessed of due stability and animated by a disposition to comply with the rules of international" law, and he then waived the wait because non-recognition was blocking a frontier settlement (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1878, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344). Olney's one sentence of 1895 on Ecuador is the practice in full: "the practice of this Government has been to enter into effective relations with the de facto government when it shall have been fully established with the general consent of the people" (1895, Part I, Document 246, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d246). Bryan's telegram recognizing Peru's junta in 1914 rests on the same two findings, "uncontested exercise of executive power" and exercise "freely acquiesced in by the people" (1914, Document 1799, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d1799).

### Wilson added a constitutional test, and stated it as a rule for the region

Wilson's conditions to Huerta in August 1913 were an armistice, "an early and free election in which all will agree to take part", Huerta's pledge "not to be a candidate", and all parties' agreement "to abide by the results of the election", with recognition pledged to the administration so chosen (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028); when Huerta held his own election the President "would not feel justified in accepting the result of such an election or in recognizing a president so chosen" (1913, Document 1047, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1047). Carranza was recognized in 1915 only as "the de facto Government of Mexico" (1915, Document 1003, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915/d1003). In February 1917 Lansing made the test a standing rule for Central America: the United States "will not give recognition or support to any Government which may be established unless it is clearly proven that it is elected by legal and constitutional means" (1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378). The 1910s hold the largest share of the whole set, about a third of it, and Mexico the largest share of that.

### In the 1920s the price was a binding promise; in Central America it was a treaty rule, and both were given up

Hughes told the chargé in Mexico in 1921 that "The question of recognition is a subordinate one": "when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). In Central America the 1923 General Treaty's Article II made the refusal automatic: in 1932 Stimson ruled that "Martínez can under no circumstances be recognized. This is not due to any animus against Martínez, but because of the clear provisions of the Treaty" (1932, Volume V, Document 618, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d618). By 1936 Welles judged that its violation by Guatemala and Honduras "make the Treaty a complete dead letter" (1936, Volume V, Document 111, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d111), and Hull instructed the missions that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (1936, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). The Legal Adviser's 1938 opinion on Vargas shows the narrower rule left standing: recognition arises only "where the recognized government has been ousted by a coup d’état or revolution and a new régime set up contrary to constitutional methods" (1937, Volume V, Document 338, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1937v05/d338).

### In the war years the question became collective, and the fear was of being last

Hull's statement on Bolivia's new regime in December 1943 put hemisphere security first: "Considerations of the security of the Hemisphere and of the war effort of the United Nations must have first importance in any matter of this or similar character" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1943, Volume V, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486), and the republics were asked, "before proceeding to recognize the new Government of Bolivia", to carry out "the consultations and the exchange of information recommended in Resolution XXII" (1944, Volume VII, Document 386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d386). Byrnes's circular on Venezuela in October 1945 is the three-part finding in one sentence: the junta "is in control of that country", "enjoys general support without active opposition" and "intends to fulfill its international obligations" (1945, Volume IX, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170); five days later he hurried the date because other states were recognizing and the United States risked being "last to recognize, thus creating possible prejudice to our interests in Venezuela" (1945, Volume IX, Document 1182, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1182). Non-recognition still had teeth where the hemisphere held together: for a year in 1947 Marshall required that Somoza be dealt with "as a private citizen only and not in his official capacity as Minister of War" (1947, Volume VIII, Document 733, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v08/d733).

### After 1950 a settled formula, with elections hoped for but not required

The post-war notes repeat one formula. For Haiti in 1950 the note recorded that the junta "will honor all Haiti’s internatl obligations", that its authority was "recognized throughout Haiti", and that it "intends maintain democratic practices and call for new legislative elections soon as practicable" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, Volume II, Document 478, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d478). For Batista in 1952 the statements concerned "international obligations; its attitude towards private capital; and its intention to take steps to curtail international communist activities in Cuba" (1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 327, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d327); for Castro in 1959 Dulles found the new government's statements "made in good faith" and recommended recognition "without delay" (1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). The 1957 telegram on Haiti states the doctrine behind the formula: "it is not ordinarily desirable in inter-American relations to utilize recognition as an instrument of policy", and "Whether or when elections are to be held is an internal decision not properly a condition precedent to recognition" (1955–1957, Volume VI, Document 356, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v06/d356).

### Kennedy made recognition a lever against coups; after 1963 the Department arranged not to face the question

Before Peru's 1962 coup the ambassador had told the generals, on the President's authority, "that we would find it very difficult to recognize a government installed by military coup and next to impossible to continue assistance programs" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 427, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d427); for Ecuador in 1963 recognition waited on a dated return to civilian rule and on other republics acting first (1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, Document 109, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d109). In December 1963 the Department explained why it was giving up: "our principal objective in withholding recognition has been return at earliest possible date constitutionally elected government", and the prospects of gaining more were nil (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). From the Brazilian coup of April 1964 the preferred course was continuity: "there would be no problem of U.S. recognition of the new government because we would merely continue our relations with the President" (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 206, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d206); in 1968 the Assistant Secretary proposed "moving U.S. recognition doctrine and practice toward a variant of Estrada Doctrine", the Mexican rule that a government is dealt with because it exists and not because it is legitimate (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512); for Bolivia in 1970 Kissinger noted "the legal fiction that this is a change of administrations not a new government" (1969–1976, Volume E–10, Document 95, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve10/d95); and for Chile in September 1973 the public line was that "we recognize governments, not heads of government, and that question of recognition has not arisen" (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348). The one pledge that survived was the Dominican one of 1963, "US recognition of triumvirate was based on promise of election schedule" (1964–1968, Volume XXXII, Document 14, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v32/d14); by 1979, for Nicaragua, recognition was "considered to be natural", the statement only to "make reference to the provisional government’s pledges" (1977–1980, Volume XV, Document 282, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v15/d282).

## How this was done

The study used a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer full-text index of all 553 volumes, the TEI text of the volumes, and the reference data bundled with the application. Because the recognition words appear in about one document in seven of the Latin American volumes, the same rate as in the series as a whole, a word search alone would have measured the series and not the question; the scope was therefore built from the editors' own chapter headings, read in full for every volume (two hundred and fifty-three headings name recognition anywhere in the series; a nonsense term found none and a common institutional name found thousands, so the scan works), and after 1950, when such headings stop, from a search requiring the recognition words within twelve words of a term for a government installed by force, whose every hit was checked for the literal words and read for relevance. Periods were taken from each document's own date, not from the volume's series year. Fifty-nine documents were read whole and thirty-four are quoted; every quotation was checked as an exact passage of its document, and every document cited here is in the companion collection. The archival shelves were resolved through the bundled indexes of lot files, series facts and filing schemes, and are reported by channel, never summed. Exact counts, denominators and queries are in the memo.
