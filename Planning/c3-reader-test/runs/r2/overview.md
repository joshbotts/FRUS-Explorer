# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** For most of the record the deciding test was not how a government had taken power but whether it held the country and would honor what the state owed. In 1900 the Department told its minister in Bogotá that "the rule of the United States is to defer recognition of another executive in its place until it shall appear that it is in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority, and that it is in a position to fulfill all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state under treaties and international law." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530). In 1948 the Policy Planning Staff restated the same test as three factors: "(a) de facto control of the territory and the administrative machinery of State, including the maintenance of public order; (b) the ability and willingness of a government to discharge its international obligations; (c) general acquiescence of the people of a country in the government in power." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). Between those two statements the series records three departures from the test. From 1913 the Wilson administration refused, in Mexico and Central America, to recognize governments set up by force unless they were "elected by legal and constitutional means", though it went on recognizing de facto governments in Peru (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378). From 1923 to 1936 a treaty rule for Central America barred recognition of governments born of "a coup d’état or a revolution" and of their leaders (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). From December 1943, for the duration of the war, a government "instituted by force" was to be recognized only after the American republics had consulted (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33). What Washington asked in return was concrete and changed with the period: frontier guarantees from Porfirio Díaz in 1877, Huerta's pledge not to stand for election in 1913, a customs convention and four other undertakings from Haiti in 1914, a treaty protecting American property from Mexico in 1921, wartime evidence that a new regime had no Axis inspiration, and, from 1962, election dates and respect for civil liberties. The set behind this answer is about 2,300 documents in 137 volumes; about 1,200 of them sit in the 51 chapters that the editors themselves titled as recognition questions, all dated 1895 to 1950.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.**
- *A full run of cases after 1950.* I searched every chapter and section heading in all 553 volumes for the words *recognition*, *revolution*, *coup*, *junta*, *de facto*, *provisional government*, *overthrow* and *insurrection*. From 1950 on, only one Latin American heading names recognition (Haiti's military junta, 1950) and one names an overthrow (Cuba, 1958–1960). A search of the document text finds about 290 candidate documents for 1951–1981, and in a reading sample only about half of those concern recognition. The Brazilian coup of 1964 is handled as a matter of continuing relations with a constitutional successor, which the main search does not catch; a separate search found eight documents (a control search for the phrase Department of State found 27 in the same pages, and an impossible string found none). The shelf: the Department's Subject-Numeric File for 1963–1973 (country files POL 16 and POL 23-9) and the Central Foreign Policy File after 1973, both in Record Group 59 at the National Archives; the bundled data give no series identifier for either.
- *The nineteenth-century files behind the printed despatches.* The edition prints no source notes in these years: all but two of the 384 documents dated through 1905 carry none. The shelf is inferred from the bundled roll index, not cited by the editors: the National Archives' filmed despatches from U.S. ministers and diplomatic instructions, by country (for example, *Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Mexico, 1823–1906*, file unit NAID 177380741).
- *The Latin American side of the bargain.* FRUS prints what the new governments told American diplomats, and occasionally their notes (Mexico's of 1923 is in the collection), but not their own deliberations. Nothing in the bundled data points to those archives.
- *The question's own vocabulary.* The phrases *came to power*, *by force* and *Latin America* are nearly absent from the documents: they appear in two, 25 and 32 of the roughly 1,200 documents in the editors' recognition chapters, within a factor of two of their rates across the whole series. The period's own words (*revolution*, *de facto*, *coup*, *junta*) were searched instead.
- *The 1980s.* The two volumes of the series past 1980 that concern Latin America (South America, 1981–1988, and the South Atlantic conflict of 1981–1984) hold one candidate document between them, from Paraguay in 1984; the other matches in the South Atlantic volume concern sovereignty over the islands, not the recognition of a government (the control search found 67 and 337 documents in the two volumes, and the impossible string none).

**Scope.** Every count here is conditional on the 553 volumes in this copy of the index, which is the whole series as the app lists it. The scope is 2,333 documents from 137 of those volumes, dated 1861–1981 apart from one stray earlier paper in a claims appendix: the 1,206 documents of the 51 chapters the editors titled as recognition questions about Latin American governments (1895–1950), plus 1,127 more from Latin American sections whose text puts a word for recognition within a few words of *government*, *regime* or *junta* and also uses the vocabulary of revolution, coup, junta, de facto rule, overthrow, insurrection, usurpation, unconstitutional acts or provisional government. Front matter, editorial notes, the reader's own notes and summaries, the British and Dutch Caribbean and Canada were excluded (no duplicate second edition falls in the set), and seven documents outside the set were added to the collection because they state the policy without that vocabulary (Wilson's 1913 declaration, for example, never uses the word *recognition*).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "came to power by force".** I counted revolutions, coups, juntas, civil-war victors, a self-coup (Vargas in 1937) and the French-imposed empire in Mexico. Coups presented as constitutional succession, like Brazil's in 1964, fall outside the text rule because Washington treated them as continuing relations; widening the rule to catch them adds cases. The text rule does catch the provisional government the United States planned to install in Cuba by invasion in 1961, which accounts for about a fifth of the 1960s candidates; whether a government Washington itself meant to put in power by force belongs in this question changes that decade's count by that much.
- **What counts as Latin America.** I used the twenty republics and left out the British and Dutch Caribbean. That drops Grenada (1979) and Suriname (1980): about two dozen documents in the 1977–1980 Caribbean volume mention either one together with recognition or coup words.
- **Whether continuing relations counts as recognition.** After 1948 Washington often avoided the word. Adding *continue*, *resume* or *diplomatic relations* to the text rule adds about a hundred candidates, roughly a tenth more; I kept the narrower rule.
- **Whether recognition of insurgents as belligerents belongs.** It is a different act (it treats a rebellion as a war, not a rebel group as a government). Three of the forty documents in the reading sample were of this kind or concerned recognition between the factions themselves, and I left them out of the argument.
- **Whether you want the whole span or one era.** The editors' own recognition chapters cover only 1895–1950, so any comparison across 1950 rests on a text search with lower precision after that date.

## Findings

### 1. Before 1913, recognition followed control, but Washington could name a price first

The nineteenth-century instructions treat recognition as a finding of fact: who holds the capital, whether resistance has ended, whether the people acquiesce, and whether the new authorities will keep the treaties. In 1899 John Hay put the test in one line of telegram: "Test of recognition is complete regency of affairs by de facto government capable of fulfilling international obligations." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1899, Document 792, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1899/d792). The test could still be made conditional. After Porfirio Díaz's revolution of 1876 the Department said that "it waits before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico until it shall be assured that his election is approved by the Mexican people, and that his administration is possessed of stability to endure and of disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties." It then asked for frontier cooperation against raids, arguing that "some guarantee of such an arrangement should be made the condition precedent to any recognition, rather than to trust to the possibility that it may ultimately follow." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222). The same pattern runs through Venezuela in 1892, where Joaquín Crespo was recognized once he gave assurances that he could and would meet the country's international obligations (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1892, Document 517, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1892/d517). It runs through the 1900 instruction quoted above, and through Nicaragua, where Juan Estrada's revolutionary government was recognized on the strength of written pledges of constitutional guarantees and financial rehabilitation (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Document 933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933).

### 2. From 1913 to 1920, Wilson added a test of origin

At the end of February 1913, days after Huerta seized power, the outgoing Taft Department was still weighing what it called the usual tests: popular acquiescence, and the ability to protect foreigners and meet obligations (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 894, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d894). Wilson's declaration of March 1913 changed the premise: "Cooperation is possible only when supported at every turn by the orderly processes of just government based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). In August he set out what Mexico would have to do, including "The consent of General Huerta to bind himself not to be a candidate for election as President of the Republic at this election", and promised that the United States "pledges itself to recognize and in every way possible and proper to assist the administration chosen and set up in Mexico in the way and on the conditions suggested." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). In 1915 Lansing described the search for a Mexican government that would restore peace, secure individual rights and perform its international obligations (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, The Lansing Papers, 1914–1920, Volume II, Document 384, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914-20v02/d384). When Federico Tinoco overthrew Costa Rica's president in 1917, the Department declared that "it will not give recognition or support to any Government which may be established unless it is clearly proven that it is elected by legal and constitutional means." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378). Lansing told the deposed president the same thing in private (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, The Lansing Papers, 1914–1920, Volume II, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914-20v02/d348). The test of origin was applied selectively, however. In May 1914 Washington recognized the provisional government that Colonel Benavides had set up in Peru, in step with Argentina, Brazil and Chile (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 1812, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d1812), and in August 1919 it recognized Leguía, who had overthrown President Pardo, as head of the de facto government of Peru (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1919, Volume II, Document 720, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919v02/d720). In the editors' recognition chapters, the language of constitutionality appears in the documents' own text about one time in four for 1913–1929, against about one time in ten before 1913.

### 3. What Washington asked: treaties, customs, property and elections

The most explicit price lists in the series come from Haiti and Mexico. In November 1914 the Department offered Théodore recognition as provisional president only on terms: "recognition by the United States will be granted him as provisional president when a commission to be named by the Haitian Government with full powers to act upon certain questions of interest to this Government and to the Republic of Haiti is in position to give necessary assurances." The commission was to settle five matters: a customs convention, the railroad and bank disputes, protection of foreign interests, and "Fifth. Agreement by Haiti never to lease any Haitian territory at Mole St. Nicholas or anywhere else in the country to any European Government for use as a naval or coaling station." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 544, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d544). A week earlier the Department had laid out a parallel plan: a provisional president who would not himself be a candidate would hold elections watched by American observers, after which Washington would recognize the elected president and congress as the constitutional government (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 540, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d540). The Haitian government answered that it could not negotiate a control of its customs in any form (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 568, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d568). For Obregón's Mexico the price was a treaty safeguarding property acquired before the 1917 Constitution: "In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). Mexico's foreign minister welcomed the Department's move away from "the narrow method of signing a treaty of amity and commerce—which as an unavoidable condition precedent to granting recognition to the present Government of Mexico prejudiced the national sovereignty and dignity". His note also lists the five points the Department had named as obstacles to relations: the foreign debt, the oil companies, titles acquired before 1917, the court rulings on non-retroactivity, and land taken without compensation (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 472, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d472).

### 4. From 1923 to 1936, two rules at once: a treaty rule for Central America, the old test elsewhere

In 1923 Secretary Hughes announced that, for the five Central American republics, Washington would follow Article II of their new General Treaty of Peace and Amity, under which the signatories "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country." The article also barred the leaders of a coup, their close relatives and its senior officials even after an election (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). In South America the old test held. In September 1930 Stimson recognized the revolutionary governments of Argentina, Peru and Bolivia because "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status." In the same statement he explained that the Central American treaty was a separate rule (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). Under the treaty rule Washington opposed General Orellana in Guatemala and was ready to recognize a constitutional successor once new elections had been called (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 412, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d412). It told El Salvador that Martínez could under no circumstances be recognized, citing the treaty's clear provisions (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 618, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d618). In Cuba in 1933, where no treaty applied, Sumner Welles argued against recognizing a government without popular support (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1933, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 430, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933v05/d430). The treaty rule ended in two steps. Once El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced the treaty, Welles proposed a route to recognizing Martínez, who had been minister of war until days before the revolt (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199). Then, in 1936, the Department told its missions that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America, nor should it endeavor to utilize that Treaty as justification for any other action it may take or fail to take" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). The treaty's language is in about one in five of the editors' recognition-chapter documents for 1930–1942 and almost nowhere else.

### 5. From 1943 to 1945, recognition by consultation, with a wartime security test

In June 1943 the ambassador in Buenos Aires assured his colleagues that Washington would not make a break with the Axis a condition of recognizing Argentina's new military government, and wanted only clearer knowledge of its intentions (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 335, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d335). In December 1943, as a new regime took power in Bolivia, the inter-American Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense, meeting in Montevideo, recommended (in terms its chairman said referred to no particular case) that the American governments "do not proceed to the recognition of a new government instituted by force, before consulting among themselves for the purpose of determining whether this government complies with the Inter-American undertakings for the defense of the continent" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33). Washington meanwhile withheld recognition from the Bolivian regime and held back exports to Bolivia (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 142, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d142). Recognition was then arranged jointly, for a date agreed among the republics (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 437, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d437). The old tests survived, with a security test added. For Ecuador in 1944, after the familiar findings on control and obligations: "As reports reaching us indicate no threat to the Hemisphere or to the United Nations war effort nor any pro-Axis inspiration in the Velasco Ibarra revolutionary movement, his Government also appears to deserve recognition from the security angle." (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 1018, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1018). For El Salvador in 1945 the result of a consultation is printed: "Fourteen of the sixteen replies are favorable to early recognition of El Salvador on the grounds that the government is (a) in de facto control of the machinery of government and of the country, (b) enjoys general support without active opposition and (c) has declared its intention and is able to fulfill its international obligations." (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 880, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d880). In the editors' recognition chapters the formal consultation machinery appears in no document before 1943 and in about one in twelve of the 1943–1950 documents, and the Axis in about one in six of them.

### 6. After 1948, relations without approval, and conditions pressed through aid and delay

The 1948 Policy Planning Staff paper kept the old test and drained recognition of moral meaning: "Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). The Bogotá conference's resolution on the continuity of diplomatic relations gave the practice an inter-American form, and in November 1948 the Department resumed relations with Peru's military junta on that basis, noting that the resolution avoids the formal term *recognition* (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89). In Haiti in 1957 an official argued that making elections a condition would itself be intervention (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Multilateral; Mexico; Caribbean, Volume VI, Document 353, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v06/d353). In January 1959 the provisional government installed after Castro's victory was recognized within a week, on its assurance that it controlled the republic and would honor its commitments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). The Kennedy administration used delay as leverage. After the Peruvian coup of 1962 it sought an earlier election date, a public pledge of free elections, assurances on Communist influence and, if possible, a civilian cabinet before resuming relations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421). In December 1963 it recognized the Honduran and Dominican regimes, explaining that "In cases both countries our principal objective in withholding recognition has been return at earliest possible date constitutionally elected government." and that further non-recognition would achieve nothing (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). After the Brazilian coup of 1964 the question was avoided, because Washington would simply continue its relations with the constitutional successor (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 206, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d206). In Argentina in 1966 the asks were reduced to statements: "We also indicated informally to the Argentine authorities that public statements on the points contained in Resolution XXVI, plus a commitment on peaceful settlement of disputes, would facilitate recognition by the United States." and "The significant omission is any statement on elections." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 141, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d141). In Chile in 1973 the junta itself volunteered what it expected Washington to want (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 355, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d355). In Bolivia in 1978 the ambassador said Washington would first need to know the new government's plans for democratization and human rights (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, South America; Latin America Region, Document 133, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d133). Among the post-1950 candidates, communism appears in the text of about three documents in five and human rights in about one in twelve, but only about half of those candidates are about recognition at all.

## How this was done

I worked from a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer index covering all 553 volumes of the series, the TEI XML files of the same volumes, and the app's bundled reference files. I first searched the editors' chapter and section headings in every volume, read each one that named recognition or a seizure of power, and took as the core of the scope the 51 Latin American chapters whose titles name the recognition, non-recognition or continued relations of a government that came to power by revolution or coup. I added the documents of the Latin American sections whose text joins a recognition word to *government*, *regime* or *junta* and also uses a word for seizing power. The index treats *recognition*, *recognized* and *recognizing* as one word, so I read the literal text of an evenly spaced sample of 40 matches: every match held the words, but only 23 of the 40 were about recognizing a government, so I treat that part of the set as candidates, not as a count. The vocabulary rates were measured on the TEI files with the editors' footnotes and headings removed, so that they describe the documents and not the editors. Dates are each document's own date. The archival sources come from the printed source notes, and the unprinted citations from the separate citation index, resolved through the app's bundled lot, series and catalogue files. Before finishing I checked two things: every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection was matched word for word against its document in the memo and found in the document's own text, not in an editorial footnote; and every document cited here is in the collection.

## Read next

The collection *Recognizing governments that took power by force in Latin America (FRUS)* holds the 41 documents this overview rests on, under the six findings above in date order, with the quoted passages marked as excerpts. Three additions from you would change what can be said. A decision on whether continuing relations and belligerency count would let the scope be re-run on a firm definition. A pass marking the post-1950 candidates as on or off topic would turn them from candidates into a count. A visit to the Decimal File's country *Government* files (8xx.01) and to the Subject-Numeric POL 16 files, for the cases FRUS prints only in part, would supply the internal memoranda that the editors cite but do not print.
