# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series prints, under chapter headings the editors themselves wrote for the purpose (Revolution in Peru; Refusal of the United States to recognize the Martínez regime; Recognition of the Venezuelan revolutionary junta), the reasoning behind nearly every decision from 1895 to 1980, and the demands put to each new government are usually quoted in the instruction that carried them. Read together, the documents show six distinct rules in succession rather than one policy. Wilson asked for legitimacy of origin: a government "elected legally and established according to the Constitution", and in Mexico an armistice, an early free election in which Huerta did not stand, and acceptance of the result (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Documents 7 and 1028; 1917, Document 379; 1921, Volume I, Document 238). In Central America from 1923 to 1936 the United States applied Article II of the Central American treaty, a rule it had not signed, which barred recognition after a coup until freely elected representatives had reorganized the country and barred the coup's leaders from the presidency (1923, Volume II, Document 345; 1932, Volume V, Document 639; 1936, Volume V, Document 112). Elsewhere Stimson applied three tests in 1930: control of the country without active resistance, a declared intention to fulfil international obligations, and elections in due course (1930, Volume I, Document 375). During the war a fourth test, friendliness to the Allied cause, was added and the decision was taken, at least in form, by consultation among the American republics (1943, Volume V, Documents 334 and 486; 1945, Volume IX, Document 1170). From 1948 the Department decided that recognition should not be used as a political weapon and, under the Bogotá resolution, continued relations after a decent interval on three assurances: control, international obligations, elections (1948, Volume IX, Documents 89 and 115; 1950, Volume II, Document 478). Kennedy reversed this for a time, suspending relations after the coups in Peru, the Dominican Republic and Honduras until elections were scheduled (1961–1963, Volume XII, Documents 421 and 359); after 1965 the Department consulted under the Rio resolution and from 1968 moved openly toward the Mexican doctrine of recognizing states rather than governments, so that by 1973 and 1978 it said the question of recognition had not arisen (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Documents 132 and 512; 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 349; 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, Document 137). The set these findings rest on is about three and a half thousand documents in 52 volumes; a wider net over every Latin American volume, catching any document that speaks of recognizing a government, regime or junta, finds about five and a half thousand in 228 volumes (see the memo).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The documents came, through 1949, almost entirely from one place: the Department of State's Central Decimal File, Record Group 59, in the "internal affairs" class of each country (Mexico, Cuba, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Bolivia, Guatemala, Peru, the Dominican Republic, Argentina, Venezuela), with the decimal class glossed from the Department's own 1910–1949 manual; the bundled reference data holds no catalogue entry for that file itself, only for its predecessor, the Numerical File of 1906–1910 (National Archives identifier 654171, microfilm M862), so the shelf for 1910–1949 is the class, not a series record. For 1964–1980 the printed documents came from the Central Files 1964–66 and 1967–69 (the subject-numeric files, Record Group 59), from the Kennedy Library's National Security File (identifier 1164; Country Files 193236, 1961–1963), the Johnson Library's National Security File (567979; Latin America Country Files 567985, 1963–1969), the Nixon NSC Files, and the Carter Library's Office of the National Security Advisor (560131; Brzezinski's Country Files 560151, 1977–1981). That is the came-from channel. The footnotes cite without printing, the pointed-at channel, 160 citations across 118 documents, most of them to further Department of State papers with no collection named; the lot files among them resolve to Record Group 59 series at College Park: Subject and Country Files of the Office of Central American and Panamanian Affairs, lot 63D127, entry A1 3157, identifier 2363840, 1955–1963, possibly restricted; Records Relating to El Salvador, lot 64D16, entry A1 3159, identifier 1750062, partly restricted (national security); the Secretary's and Under Secretary's Memorandums of Conversation, lot 65D330, entry A1 1566, identifier 602231, 1953–1964, unrestricted; Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers, lot 66D95, entry A1 1586E, identifier 2839192, from 1947, partly restricted; Records Relating to Petroleum Issues in Lima, lot 71F154, entry UD-WX 660, identifier 27023004, 1962–1966, fully restricted; and the Official Files and Briefing Books of Edmund Muskie, lot 82D100, entries UD-05D 71–72, identifier 26309182, 1977–1981, fully restricted. Two more lots appear in the came-from channel only: the Presidential Memorandums of Conversation, lot 66D149, entry A1 5158, identifier 610077, 1956–1964, unrestricted, and the Briefing Books of Cyrus Vance, lot 84D241, entry UD-14D 69, identifier 26309216, 1978, partly restricted. The two channels are reported separately and never added.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Three parts of the question are not answered here. First, the 1950s: in the set of chapters the editors titled for recognition, only four documents fall between 1950 and 1959, and the Cuban coup of 1952, the Bolivian revolution of 1952, the fall of Arbenz in 1954 and the fall of Perón in 1955 appear only inside general country chapters, where the search for recognition near government, regime or junta does find documents (in the 1952–1954 and 1955–1957 American Republics volumes) that this session did not read; so this is a limit of the reading, not evidence of silence. The same holds for Brazil in 1964 and Argentina in 1976. Second, the Latin American side: the Mexican Estrada doctrine of 1930, Resolution XXII of the Emergency Advisory Committee for Political Defense (1943) and Resolution XXVI of the Rio conference of 1965 are cited by the editors in footnotes, with their criteria summarized, but their texts are not printed, and the other republics' reasoning survives only as United States diplomats reported it; the shelf is the Pan American Union's and the Committee's own publications, which the footnotes name. Third, the Department's own doctrinal file: the Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948 that set the rule is printed earlier in the 1948 volume but was not read in this session, and an editorial note on Acheson's recognition speech of September 1949 could not be located by search (the search for the words recognition, Acheson and September among the editorial notes of the 1949 volume returned nothing, while the same engine returned 590 documents for the control phrase Department of State inside the set and none for a nonsense string).

**Scope.** Fifty-two volumes between 1895 and 1980, chosen by the editors' chapter titles for recognition, revolution, coup or overthrow of a Latin American government, plus eight hand-marked runs of documents in the thematic volumes after 1950, about three and a half thousand documents in all. Front matter and the editors' own notes were excluded, as were the two second editions in the library; the reader's own summaries and notes were never consulted.

## Decisions this needs from you

- Whether "came to power by force" includes a sitting president who seized extra-constitutional powers (Vargas in Brazil in 1937, Estrada Cabrera's fall in 1920) or only governments that displaced another: I counted only displacements, which keeps Brazil 1937 out and Guatemala 1920 in.
- Whether the question covers the Mexican Revolution as a whole (932 documents of 1913–1916, two thirds of the 1910s set) or only the recognition decisions within it: I kept the whole chapters, so Mexico weighs heavily in every count; dropping it would halve the 1910s.
- Whether the 1950s and 1964–1976 coups that the editors did not title for recognition belong in the answer: they are not in this set and would need a second reading of the country chapters named above.
- Whether the Latin American counterpart's reasoning (Estrada, Guani, Rodríguez Larreta) is wanted in its own words: FRUS gives it only at second hand.
- The rule used meanwhile: a document is in if its chapter's title, written by the editors, names the recognition of, or a revolution or coup producing, a government in a Latin American republic, or if it sits in one of the eight post-1950 runs listed in the memo.

## Findings

### 1. Wilson made legitimacy of origin the test, and asked for an election the usurper could not win (1913–1921)

Wilson's declaration of March 1913, circulated to every mission in Latin America, set the premise: "just government rests always upon the consent of the governed", and the United States "can have no sympathy with those who seek to seize the power of government to advance their own personal interests or ambition" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). Applied to Huerta, this became a list, put to him through John Lind: "(a) An immediate cessation of fighting throughout Mexico, a definite armistice solemnly entered into and scrupulously observed; “(b) Security given for an early and free election in which all will agree to take part; “(c) The consent of General Huerta to bind himself not to be a candidate for election as President of the Republic at this election; and “(d) The agreement of all parties to abide by the results of the election and cooperate in the most loyal way in organizing and supporting the new administration", with a pledge to recognize the administration so chosen; Wilson told Congress that "Mexico has a great and enviable future before her, if only she choose and attain the paths of honest constitutional government" (1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). Huerta's foreign secretary answered that Wilson was "laboring under a serious delusion" in calling Mexico's situation incompatible with its international obligations (1913, Document 1029, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1029). The rule was stated most baldly for Costa Rica after Tinoco's coup: the wish "of seeing the will of the people prevail in governmental matters in Costa Rica has forced it to the conclusion that no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition" (1917, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379). For Bolivia in 1920–1921 the same test was applied jointly with Argentina and Brazil: the United States would wait because "the elections to be held in Bolivia in November will afford the first indication as to whether the new Government has the formal support of the Bolivian people" (1920, Volume I, Document 391, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d391), and then recognized Saavedra "not as the de facto Government, but as the Constitutional Government of that Republic" (1921, Volume I, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238).

### 2. In Central America the United States borrowed the republics' own rule, applied it to the letter, and gave it up (1923–1936)

Hughes announced in June 1923 that United States recognition in the five Central American republics "will be consonant with" Article II of their new treaty, which he quoted: the parties "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country" (1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). The hardest case was El Salvador, where General Martínez, war minister days before the coup of December 1931, fell under the treaty's bar on recent ministers; when he proposed to deposit power temporarily in a designate, Stimson replied that "if Salvador desires to put its Government on a basis whereby recognition can be extended consistently with the obligations of the 1923 Treaty, there is no other way open than for Martínez to resign the Presidency outright" (1932, Volume V, Document 639, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d639). Welles later explained to Roosevelt that "in accordance with a policy, already announced and well established, of supporting the Treaty, the United States also declined to recognize General Martínez" (1934, Volume V, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199), and recognition followed only after El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced the treaty. In April 1936 Hull told the five missions that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America, nor should it endeavor to utilize that Treaty as justification for any other action it may take or fail to take" (1936, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112).

### 3. Outside Central America the test was effective control plus two promises (1930)

When Argentina, Peru and Bolivia all fell to revolutions in September 1930, Stimson recognized the three provisional governments within weeks and told the press why: "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status" (1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). He insisted this was the regular policy since Jefferson and that Central America was the exception. Those three questions, control, obligations, elections, recur in nearly every later instruction: the phrase international obligations appears in about one document in twenty-seven of the set, free elections in 54 of its documents, and de facto in about one in sixteen (see the memo for the exact counts and the checks on them).

### 4. War added a security test and turned the decision into a consultation (1943–1947)

After the Argentine coup of June 1943 Hull agreed that "recognition of the new Government, if and when accorded, should be as nearly simultaneous as possible", and that "In order that all the American governments may arrive at a common decision and take simultaneous action it is obviously necessary that there be the fullest exchange of information and consultation" (1943, Volume V, Document 334, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d334). After the Bolivian coup that December he said publicly that "Considerations of the security of the Hemisphere and of the war effort of the United Nations must have first importance", and that "the question whether outside influence unfriendly to the Allied cause played any part" was relevant (1943, Volume V, Document 486, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d486). The Montevideo committee's Resolution XXII then made consultation before recognition the hemisphere's recommended procedure, and the United States used it: for Argentina it found it "almost incredible that any American regime should expect recognition and the advantages of membership in the American family of nations without sincerely contributing to this common effort" (1944, Volume VII, Document 231, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d231); for the Venezuelan junta of October 1945 it reported that "the seven man Junta in Venezuela is in control of that country; (2) that it enjoys general support without active opposition and (3) that it intends to fulfill its international obligations", and asked each republic's view before acting (1945, Volume IX, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170); for Bolivia in 1946 it was "favorably impressed by public assurances of Junta that elections will be called in near future and that Bolivia’s international obligations will be fulfilled" (1946, Volume XI, Document 312, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d312). By 1947 the method was failing in Nicaragua, where the Department would follow the majority of the republics while admitting that the Somoza-made regime "has all attributes and qualities of stable de facto Govt" (1947, Volume VIII, Document 742, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v08/d742).

### 5. In 1948 recognition ceased to be a weapon: "continuing relations" on three assurances (1948–1950)

The Nicaraguan impasse led directly to Resolution XXXV at Bogotá, which, as the Department's Latin American director explained when Peru's junta seized power that autumn, "was also intended to simplify the whole process of establishing relations with new governments, and to this end avoids use of the formal term “recognition”" (1948, Volume IX, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89). Asked about the Venezuelan junta, Kennan's Policy Planning Staff restated the rule set in March: "Recognition of a government by the United States should imply neither approval nor disapproval of the policies of that government with respect to strictly domestic affairs. … Recognition should not be withheld as a political weapon or to express moral censure except in extreme cases when U.S. national interests would be served thereby" (1948, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115). What was still asked of a junta is visible in the Panama consultation of 1949, where the republics agreed "(1) … that there was no outside intervention in the recent political upheavals in Panama; (2) the government has a broad popular basis of public support … and (3) the present government apparently intends to live up to its international commitments" (1949, Volume II, Document 448, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d448), and in the Haitian note of 1950, which recorded that "(1) Junta will honor all Haiti’s internatl obligations, (2) authority Junta recognized throughout Haiti, and (3) Junta intends maintain democratic practices and call for new legislative elections soon as practicable" (1950, Volume II, Document 478, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d478).

### 6. Kennedy asked for elections again; after 1965 the Department stopped asking the question (1960–1980)

The Eisenhower administration's note recognizing El Salvador's junta in December 1960 already listed what a junta was expected to promise: "In view of the assurances set forth in your note and of the official affirmations made public concerning the holding of free elections, my Government has instructed me to state that it welcomes these assurances, and that it is pleased to extend recognition to the Junta of Government as the provisional Government of El Salvador" (1958–1960, Volume V, Microfiche Supplement, Document ES–43, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05mSupp/es_d43). Kennedy went further, suspending relations after the Peruvian coup of July 1962; Ball reported that "the sharp reaction of the United States and other Latin American countries has led the junta to take a position with respect to new elections, with respect to current civil rights, with respect to leaders of the previous government and of APRA" (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421). After the Dominican coup of September 1963 the embassy was told to "state clearly and emphatically that USG will not recognize or deal with this regime as it stands. This regime and the politicians who named the cabinet represent a minority of the Dom people. Regime must find some way to return toward representative constitutional democracy", with seven conditions attached, among them OAS-supervised elections within a year (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359). After the Argentine coup of 1966 the formula was already procedural: "In keeping with international practice in such cases, diplomatic relations are suspended. We will be consulting with other OAS members in accordance Resolution XXVI of 1965 Rio Conference" (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132), the editors noting that the resolution asked whether a foreign power was involved, and whether the new regime promised free elections, international obligations and human rights. After the Peruvian coup of 1968 the Assistant Secretary proposed "moving U.S. recognition doctrine and practice toward a variant of Estrada Doctrine", the Mexican rule that recognition implies no judgement of legitimacy (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512). The day after the Chilean coup Kissinger reported the public line: "as to the question of recognition the issue has not arisen—in any event, we do not recognize individual governments, but rather countries" (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 349, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d349). By 1978, after the Bolivian coup, the press guidance read: "When we continue relations with a country, as in this instance, it is not necessary to address the question of recognition and it has not been our practice to do so" (1977–1980, Volume XXIV, Document 137, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d137), although the embassy was at the same time told to press for a shorter election timetable and to condition aid on it.

## How this was done

The question was scoped against a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer full-text index (553 volumes, the whole published series as downloaded on 25 October 2026) in three passes. First, 233 Latin America-related volumes were selected from the bundled manifest and searched for any document in which a form of "recognize" stands within six words of "government", "regime" or "junta"; the index treats "recognize", "recognized" and "recognition" as the same word, so the counts are of word families, and a positive control phrase and a nonsense string were run in the same pass to show the search worked. Second, the editors' chapter titles in every one of those volumes were read, and 90 chapters whose titles name the recognition of, or a revolution, coup or overthrow producing, a government in a Latin American republic were taken as the core, with eight hand-marked runs of documents in the post-1950 thematic volumes; front matter and editorial notes were excluded. Third, within that core the frequency of the key phrases was measured and each measurement checked against the literal text of a sample, in two forms (exact spacing, and allowing inflections and punctuation), with the shares and their denominators in the memo. Fifty-two documents were then read in full, chosen by ranking each episode's chapter for the recognition family, and thirty passages were cut from the stored text and verified character for character against it. The documents' own archival citations were tallied by channel (where a document came from; what its footnotes cite without printing) and resolved through the application's bundled catalogue data for decimal classes, lot files, series facts and presidential libraries; the two channels are never added. Dates are the documents' own, not the volumes' series years. Before finishing I checked that every quotation above is one of the thirty verified passages and that every document cited above is in the accompanying collection; both checks passed.

## Read next

The collection "Recognizing governments that came to power by force in Latin America" holds the 52 documents read, under six headings that follow the findings above, with the 30 verified passages marked as excerpts and a note on each document. Three additions by the historian would unlock what this run could not: reading the 1952–1957 country chapters (Cuba, Bolivia, Guatemala, Argentina) and the Brazil 1964 and Argentina 1976 documents the index locates but this session did not open; the Policy Planning Staff paper of March 1948 printed earlier in the 1948 volume, which is the doctrinal source for everything from 1948 to 1980; and the Pan American Union and Emergency Advisory Committee publications the footnotes cite, which hold the Latin American side of each consultation in its own words.
