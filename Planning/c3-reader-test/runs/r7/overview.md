# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series prints the Department of State's own reasoning on this question for more than a century, and it prints it in the instructions, circular telegrams and memoranda where the decision was made rather than in retrospective policy papers. Across the whole published series as indexed, about eleven hundred documents in 123 volumes pair the word recognition with a coup, a junta, a de facto or provisional government inside Latin American chapters, and the editors gave sixty-three chapter headings to a recognition question outright, covering some two thousand two hundred further documents (see the memo for every count). Read together they show one durable test and four overlays. The durable test is John Hay's of 1899 — "Test of recognition is complete regency of affairs by de facto government capable of fulfilling international obligations." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1899, Document 792, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1899/d792) — restated by Knox in 1913, by Stimson in 1930, by Byrnes in 1945, by Acheson in 1952 and by Herbert Hoover junior in 1955. The overlays are what Washington added to it: Woodrow Wilson's demand for constitutional legitimacy and elections the United States could judge (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028); the Central American treaty rule against governments born of coups, adopted in 1923 and dropped in 1936 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112); wartime consultation among the republics with an anti-Axis test (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170); and the Cold War's anti-communist assurances, to which Kennedy added dated elections, civil liberties and observers (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423). After 1964 the documents show relations simply continuing, or recognition following consultation, with a deliberate drift toward recognizing states rather than judging governments (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** The printed documents came, nine times in ten among those that carry a source line, from the Department's Central Decimal File, Record Group 59 — the file classes for a country's political affairs and its government, such as Mexico's political affairs, Haiti's, Bolivia's and El Salvador's government class — a series for which the app's bundled indexes give no catalogue identifier, extent or access status (the file is held at the National Archives at College Park). Documents before 1910 carry no source line at all; the bundle names the series they would have come from: Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Mexico, 1823–1906 (NARA 189098908), to Peru, 1826–1906 (189346553), to Chile, 1823–1906 (188290667), to Venezuela, 1835–1906 (188942579), to Haiti, 1862–1906 (188973924) and to Bolivia, 1848–1906 (189314921), with the Notes from each legation. From 1961 the documents came from the Kennedy Library's National Security Files, Country Files, 1961–1963 (NARA 193236); the Johnson Library's National Security File, Latin America Country Files, 1963–1969 (567985); the Nixon National Security Files, 1969–1974 (572235); and the Carter Library's National Security Advisor records, Brzezinski's Country Files, 1977–1981 (560151); the bundle gives no access status for these. That is the came-from channel. The pointed-at channel — what the editors' footnotes cite without printing — is thin for these documents themselves (one document in twenty carries such a footnote, none before December 1910) and names the same presidential files and the Department's central files; at the scale of the volumes that hold the question it points to Record Group 59 lot files, each at College Park, Textual Reference: the Executive Secretariat's S/S Files, Lot 65 D 438, entry A1 3053 (NARA 27022950), 1962–1963, restricted in part; the Bureau of Inter-American Affairs' files, Lot 53 D 26, entry A1 1130 (2108774), 1949–1953, unrestricted, and Lot 59 D 573, entry A1 1135 (2108779), 1957–1959, unrestricted; the Holland Files, Lot 57 D 295, entry A1 1134 (2108778), 1953–1956, unrestricted; the OAS Files, Lot 60 D 665, entry A1 5474A (2660902), 1951–1960, unrestricted; the S/S–NSC (Miscellaneous) Files, Lot 66 D 95, entry A1 1586E (2839192), 1947–1979, restricted in part; the Deputy Secretary's D Files, Lot 81D113, entry P 14 (1487627), 1975–1981, restricted in part; and the Bureau's Nicaragua/El Salvador Working Files, Lot 81D64, entry UD-14D 61 (27499857), 1978–1981, fully restricted. The two channels are different counts over different things and are not added.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** Four parts of the question are not in the corpus. First, the text of the 1923 Central American treaty whose Article II Washington adopted: the search for recognition near ‘treaty of peace and amity’, ‘1923 treaty’ or ‘article II’ (168 documents in the whole index, 65 in the set) finds only discussion and one quotation of the article, cited to the separately published conference proceedings; the 1907 convention it copied is printed, inside the American delegate's report (one document of the 178 in that volume that name the Department). Second, any general policy paper on coups from the 1960s: the search for a coup-policy phrase near recognition in the three Kennedy and Johnson volumes for the region returned eleven documents, every one a country case; the shelf is the Kennedy and Johnson country files named above and the Department's central files for 1964–1966, which the bundle cannot identify. Third, the Latin American side of the argument: the Tobar, Estrada and Betancourt doctrines appear in 29 documents in the whole index, 16 in the set, always as reported by American officers; no bundled index reaches a Latin American archive (four footnotes in the scope volumes cite one). Fourth, the coups of 1981–1988: the one South America volume indexed for the decade holds nineteen documents that use the word recognition and nine that mention a coup, but none that pairs them, while the control phrase ‘Department of State’ returns sixty-nine, so the absence is the volume's and not the search's; the shelf is the Reagan Library's National Security Council Latin American Affairs Directorate records (NARA 12024796, general office files 1983–1989) and the Department's Central Foreign Policy File, for which the bundle has no identifier.

**Scope.** The whole published series as indexed — 553 volumes, with the two duplicate second editions set aside — read through its Latin American chapters and volumes, which hold about sixty-three thousand documents; the set the findings rest on is the eleven hundred documents whose text pairs recognition with the vocabulary of force, together with the two thousand two hundred under the editors' own recognition headings, three thousand one hundred and thirty-six in all. Front matter and the editors' notes were excluded, as were the editors' footnotes wherever a sentence was checked against the original mark-up, and nothing in the reader's own notes or summaries was read. The discussion is densest in the 1910s, about six and a half documents in every thousand dated documents of that decade, falls to about two in a thousand in the 1940s and 1950s, and rises again to about five in a thousand in the 1960s (the exact counts, denominators and each decade's largest volume are in the memo).

## Decisions this needs from you

- Whether ‘came to power by force’ includes a sitting government's own coup against its constitution (Brazil in 1937) and the victors of civil wars (Peru in 1881, Chile in 1891): included here, which is what brings the Legal Adviser's rule that a self-coup needs no new act of recognition into the findings; excluding them removes the three nineteenth-century documents and that rule.
- Whether ‘recognition’ means a formal act or also the quiet continuance of relations that the Department used from 1937 and relied on in Brazil in 1964: included here, which is why the 1960s and 1970s appear at all; counting only formal acts leaves Kennedy's withholdings as the decade's only material.
- Whether the Caribbean occupations count as Latin America: Haiti and the Dominican Republic are included, and they supply the plainest statements of conditions (a customs convention, a constabulary) before recognition; excluding them removes those.
- Whether the series' first forty years belong in the answer: the 1860s to 1900s hold about one document in six of the vocabulary-defined set (187 of 1,131) and show the rule before Wilson changed it; starting in 1899 or 1913 changes the first finding, not the others.
- Whether the editors' footnotes may be quoted as evidence: they were not, and three sentences a reader would otherwise take as the documents' own were set aside as the editors' (the terms of the Honduras recognition of December 1963, the summary of the 1965 Rio resolution, and the gloss on the Estrada doctrine).
- The rule used meanwhile: a document is in the set if it stands in a Latin American chapter or volume and its text puts recognition within fifteen words of a coup, junta, revolution, overthrow, or de facto, provisional, military or constitutional government, or if the editors filed it under a heading naming recognition of a government; the index treats ‘recognize’, ‘recognized’ and ‘recognition’ as the same word and the test was repeated on the literal text (nine hundred and eighty-eight of the eleven hundred and thirty-one match strictly, 1,117 when inflections and punctuation are allowed).

## Findings

### 1. Before Wilson the rule was to wait, to test control and capacity, and to refuse to judge a title

From the Civil War to the Taft administration the Department answered a government born of force with the same two moves: it declined to prejudge, and it asked whether the new authority held the country and could meet its obligations. In 1865 Seward refused France's suggestion that the United States promise Maximilian de facto recognition if the French withdrew, recording "a decision that the recognition which the Emperor had thus suggested cannot be made" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1865, Part III, Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1865p3/d423); in Chile's civil war of 1891 Wharton told the legation that "the Executive is not disposed to prejudge the question of recognition of a revolutionary movement" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1891, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1891/d152). When Castro marched into Caracas in 1899 Hay's whole instruction was to wait and apply the test quoted above, transacting necessary business meanwhile with whoever was locally responsible. In Honduras in 1907 the test already carried its usual rider, a promise of elections: recognition followed on "Being advised of the competency of the provisional government to perform international obligations, and being satisfied with the steps taken to revive the constitutional régime in Honduras and to provide for the election of the executive thereof" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1907, Part II, Document 27, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1907p2/d27). Knox's practice in Nicaragua was to answer no insurgent's request and to warn every faction that Washington "would hold strictly accountable for the protection of American life and property the factions de facto in control" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Document 933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933); a week after Huerta's coup he named "the usual tests applied to such cases", among them "the degree to which the population of Mexico acquiesce in and assent to the new régime and the question of disposition and ability to protect foreigners and their interests", and distinguished dealing with a de facto government from formally recognizing it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 894, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d894; see also Document 849, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d849, which asked assurances on the outstanding questions between the two countries first).

### 2. Wilson made recognition a lever and attached conditions to it

From 1913 the test changed from capacity to legitimacy, and the conditions became specific. Wilson's terms for Mexico, delivered through John Lind, were an armistice, "Security given for an early and free election in which all will agree to take part", "The consent of General Huerta to bind himself not to be a candidate for election as President of the Republic at this election", and all parties' agreement to abide by the result, with recognition pledged to the government so chosen (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). In Haiti Bryan would recognize Zamor because "the advent of this Government is based upon the consent of the governed", but only after the Department's financial and territorial conditions had been answered (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 506, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d506), and by November Wilson's plan promised that "if the United States Government is satisfied that these elections have been free and fair and carried out under conditions which enable the people of the republic to express their real choice, it will recognize the President and Congress thus chosen" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 540, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d540). The Dominican provisional government of 1916 "will not be recognized until it shows itself to be favorable to our interpretation of convention as to control, constabulary and other reforms" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1916, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1916/d244); Tinoco in Costa Rica was told that "even if he is elected he will not be given recognition by the United States" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 382, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d382). Carranza, by contrast, was recognized in 1915 after the Pan-American conferees found "the Carrancista party is the only party possessing the essentials for recognition as the de facto government of Mexico" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, Document 993, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915/d993), and in Bolivia in 1920 Washington and Buenos Aires agreed to "wait until a government has been formed as a result of the elections in accordance with the Constitution before recognizing it" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume I, Document 391, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v01/d391). Across the vocabulary-defined set the word election stands near recognition in about one document in nine (106 of 1,131), and most densely in these years (25 of the 233 dated 1909–1920) and again under Kennedy (19 of 114).

### 3. In Central America the United States adopted a treaty rule it had not signed, and gave it up when the signatories did

The rule had a text. The 1907 Washington conference's additional convention, printed inside the American delegate's report, bound the five republics: they "shall not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics as a consequence of a coup d’etat, or of a revolution against the recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1907, Part II, Document 148, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1907p2/d148). The 1923 treaty repeated it, and in June 1923 Hughes announced that United States practice toward Honduras would follow its Article II — the parties "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). Kellogg applied it to Chamorro's coup in Nicaragua: Washington "would not extend recognition to any government which had come into power by violent or unconstitutional means" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1925, Volume II, Document 498, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1925v02/d498). Stimson applied it twice in 1931: a Guatemalan provisional president who had come in constitutionally could be recognized once elections were called within six months (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 412, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d412), while in El Salvador "the policy of nonrecognition of Central American Governments which come into power by revolution" barred even a vice president who had led the coup (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 208, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d208). Welles explained in 1934 why Martínez had been refused — "Since General Martínez had been Minister of War until within two or three days prior to the revolution, the other Central American Republics declined to recognize him as President" — and how he could be recognized once El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced the treaty (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199). In April 1936 Hull instructed the five missions that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). Mexico had been handled differently: in 1921 Hughes made a treaty on property rights the instrument, so that "the making of the treaty in proper form will accomplish the recognition of the Government that makes it" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). The treaty's shadow is measurable: recognition stands near the treaty, ‘article II’ or ‘1923 treaty’ in 19 of the 173 documents of 1921–1932 and 15 of the 90 of 1933–1942, and in none after.

### 4. Outside Central America the 1930s restored the de facto practice and settled when a formal act was needed

For the South American revolutions of 1930 Stimson announced publicly that "these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule", that each intended to meet its obligations and "to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status", and that this was no new policy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). In private he added one thing Washington would watch: the capacity-to-protect test obliged it to "pay attention to the way in which they showed themselves capable of protecting the deposed members of the last Government from being made the victims of private revenge and persecution or mob violence" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume III, Document 658, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v03/d658). When Bolivia's army replaced one colonel with another in 1937 and claimed no new recognition was needed, Welles asked "a little time, in accordance with its traditional policy, to ascertain whether the present government enjoyed a substantial measure of popular support and likewise was enabled to carry out the ordinary functions of government" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1937, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 271, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1937v05/d271). The Legal Adviser then fixed the two rules the Department used for the next forty years: "Where a new régime is established through a coup d’état or revolution, some form of recognition is necessary", though it may be ordinary dealing; and "A formal act of recognition is not required where the existing government has executed a coup d’état displacing the Constitution by a new one or setting it aside" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1939, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 312, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1939v05/d312).

### 5. The war added consultation and an Axis test; Bogotá took non-recognition off the table

From Bolivia in December 1943 to Haiti in 1950 no recognition was extended without consulting the other republics, and the word consultation, absent from the set's documents of 1909–1920 (none of 233), stands near recognition in 37 of the 186 documents of 1943–1951 and in 17 of the 114 of 1961–1968. The Bolivian junta's path to recognition was a decree calling elections that also "grants amnesty to political offenders and recognizes the liberty of the press" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 414, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d414); for Argentina the embassy urged that "we do not intend to recognize until there is concrete evidence in the form of significant anti-Nazi measures" (Document 227, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d227), and the Department's list of January 1945 required an interim constitutional government, a break with the Axis, and "(iii) Termination of the state of siege and prompt action to restore the Argentine Constitution and the calling of elections thereunder" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 242, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d242). The Axis test is confined to these years (11 of 186 documents, none before or after). Guatemala's junta was recognized in 1944 because it was "accepted as the de facto government of Guatemala without active resistance on the part of the Guatemalan people and to be in full control of the administrative machinery of the state" and had announced elections (Document 1138, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1138), and Byrnes's circular on Venezuela in October 1945 is the test in its wartime form — "(1) the seven man Junta in Venezuela is in control of that country; (2) that it enjoys general support without active opposition and (3) that it intends to fulfill its international obligations", no Axis inspiration, and consultation under the republics' Resolution XXII before recognizing (Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170); Bolivia in 1946 was "favorably impressed by public assurances of Junta that elections will be called in near future and that Bolivia’s international obligations will be fulfilled" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The American Republics, Volume XI, Document 312, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d312). After the 1948 Bogotá conference the premise changed: Lovett wrote that "non-recognition is not a suitable approach to much broader problem of dictatorships and use of force as a political procedure" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d119). What remained was the old test plus a new question. In 1950 "the President wishes to be consulted concerning the recognition of Latin American regimes which assume power by force" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1950, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 477, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d477); for Bolivia in 1952 "The first prerequisite to recognition of a de facto government is that it have control of the national territory" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 136, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d136); and Batista was recognized that spring on statements about his obligations, private capital and "its intention to take steps to curtail international communist activities in Cuba" (Document 327, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d327). Communism near recognition is absent from the set before 1921 and present in 19 of the 81 documents of 1952–1960 and 15 of the 114 of 1961–1968.

### 6. The Cold War pattern: quick recognition of anti-communist juntas, Kennedy's conditional withholding, and the drift toward recognizing states

Eisenhower's Department recognized within days on the de facto tests: for Argentina in 1955 "we were now satisfied that the new government had de facto control of the country", the President asking only about freedom of the press (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Central and South America, Volume VII, Document 195, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v07/d195); for Venezuela in 1958 "early recognition on our part would contribute materially to the stability of the new regime" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Volume V, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics, Document VE–1, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05mSupp/ve_d1); for Cuba in January 1959 Dulles found that "The Provisional Government appears free from Communist taint" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). Kennedy's Department recognized El Salvador's junta in 1961 because "The takeover by the Junta arrested a dangerous drift in El Salvador toward Castroism and Communism and it has promised to hold elections" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics; Cuba 1961–1962; Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 114, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d114), but met the coups of 1962–63 with suspension and terms. Peru's generals had been warned in March 1962 that "we would find it very difficult to recognize a government installed by military coup and next to impossible to continue assistance programs" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 427, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d427); what Washington then asked of the junta was "1. Guarantee of continued civil liberties. 2. Guarantee of free and fair elections, preferably before June 9 but at no later date, with full rights to all parties under Constitution, and with guarantee that Armed Forces will respect results those elections. 3. Offer by Junta, as indication of good faith, to invite OAS observers during electoral processes." (Document 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423). In Ecuador in 1963 the remaining demand was a public promise of civilian rule within about a year, and "USG will not extend recognition until at least several other Latin American countries have done so" (Document 109, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d109); in Honduras the embassy was to "scrupulously avoid any official contact" that could imply approval (Document 159, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d159); and in December 1963 the Department summed up that "our principal objective in withholding recognition has been return at earliest possible date constitutionally elected government", with the aims to deter plotters elsewhere and to "(c) secure fullest possible respect for civil liberties and non-communist party activity pending new elections" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). Civil liberties stand near recognition only in these years (four documents, all 1961–1968). Under Johnson and Nixon the lever was put down. For Brazil in April 1964 "there would be no problem of U.S. recognition of the new government because we would merely continue our relations with the President" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 206, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d206); for Argentina in 1966 "In keeping with international practice in such cases, diplomatic relations are suspended" and consultation opened under the 1965 Rio resolution (Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132); for Peru in 1968 the Assistant Secretary proposed "moving U.S. recognition doctrine and practice toward a variant of Estrada Doctrine" (Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512) while the Department "initiated bilateral consultations through its Embassies with other Latin American Governments on the situation in Peru and the question of recognition, in accordance with procedures established at the Second Inter-American Conference of 1965" (Document 515, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d515). On the day after Allende's fall the public line was that "we recognize governments, not heads of government", while Kubisch warned that "The biggest errors we have made in Latin America in recent years have involved too hasty recognition of military regimes—in Brazil, for example." (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348), and eight days later it was agreed that "recognition of the new government would be announced on Monday, 24 September 1973" (Document 361, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d361). Carter's staff returned to withholding: for Nicaragua in July 1979 "We should first immediately consult with our Latin American friends (Panama, Venezuela, Costa Rica, Andean countries) on an agreed strategy to the new government" and hold recognition until others had acted (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XV, Central America, Document 294, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v15/d294); after García Meza's coup in Bolivia "it would be very difficult for the U.S. to recognize or support the GOB under present circumstances" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1977–1980, Volume XXIV, South America; Latin America Region, Document 328, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d328). Human rights stands near recognition in eight documents of the set, three of them from 1977–1988.

## How this was done

The whole index was first read through the editors' chapter headings, which name recognition in 255 of its 25,115 sections; 63 of those, after fifteen non-governmental ones were set aside by hand, are Latin American recognition chapters and give 2,263 documents. A second, vocabulary-defined set was built by searching every Latin American chapter and volume for the word recognition — the index folds ‘recognize’, ‘recognized’ and ‘recognition’ into one stem and was searched by that stem — within fifteen words of a coup, junta, revolution, overthrow, or a de facto, provisional, military, revolutionary or constitutional government or president; that gave 1,131 documents in 123 volumes, and the whole set was then re-tested on the literal text in two forms, a strict one with the phrases spelled exactly (988 of 1,131) and a tolerant one allowing inflections and punctuation (1,117 of 1,131), so the stemmed search is reported as a count of real occurrences. Counts of what Washington asked (elections, obligations, consultation, the 1923 treaty, the Axis, communism, civil liberties, human rights) were made the same way, each with its literal share, and two families that fell below the eight-in-ten line were discarded. Documents were dated by their own dates, never by a volume's series year, and every decade's count is given per thousand dated documents of that decade with the share held by its largest volume. Front matter, editors' notes and the duplicate second editions were excluded; the index's text flattens the editors' footnotes into the documents, so each sentence quoted from a post-1960 volume was checked against the original mark-up and three that proved to be footnotes were dropped. Eighty-four documents were retrieved whole and fifty-three are quoted. The archival series were resolved through the application's bundled indexes, the came-from and pointed-at channels kept apart and the document-level counts kept apart from the volume-level ones. Before finishing, every quotation above and every excerpt in the collection was checked to be an exact passage of its document as retrieved, and every document cited above was checked to be in the collection.
