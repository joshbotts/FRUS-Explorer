# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** FRUS answers both halves of the question, and the answer changes several times. From the 1870s the Department applied a working test — is the new government in control, does the population acquiesce, will it honour treaties and claims — and it used the waiting period to collect specific promises: in 1877 it held off General Díaz until his government's "disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties" was shown (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222), and in 1903 the legation in Santo Domingo named four claims and concessions the provisional government had to accept first (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1904, Document 228, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d228). Wilson added a test of origin — constitutional succession and a free election, not force — announced in March 1913 (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7) and applied to Huerta and to Tinoco in Costa Rica. In Mexico in 1920–23 recognition was traded for guarantees of American property. From 1923 to 1934 the Department bound itself, for Central America only, to the 1923 treaty's rule that no government born of a coup or revolution would be recognized, then abandoned that rule in 1936 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). In the war years recognition became a matter of consultation with the other American republics, focused on Axis influence; the Bogotá resolution of 1948 then tried to make continued relations the norm and to strip recognition of any judgment. After 1950 the documents show the Department asking, case by case, for elections, civil liberties, and — in the 1950s — assurances on communism and private capital. This rests on 43 documents, read whole, out of a scope of about 2,450 documents in 54 volumes (see the memo).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *A complete census of decisions.* I searched the editors' tables of contents in all 553 volumes for chapters naming recognition: the Latin American ones run from 1895 to 1950 and stop there, and before 1895 no chapter title names recognition of a Latin American government. A search for "recognize/recognition" near "government" with "de facto", "revolution" or "provisional" finds about six hundred unchaptered candidates in 58 volumes before 1895 (I read two, on Díaz), and a search pairing "recognize/recognition" with "junta", "coup", "new government", "de facto", "provisional government", "military government" or "military regime" across the 17 Latin American volumes of 1951–1988 finds about 110 documents in 15 of them; the phrase "Department of State" (present in 552 of the 553 volumes) and an impossible string (found nowhere) were run as controls. The full run of decisions after 1950 is on the shelf in the Subject-Numeric files under POL 16 (1963–1973) and in the Central Foreign Policy File, not in FRUS. (b) *The Department's legal reasoning.* A search for "Legal Adviser" within thirty words of "recognize/recognition" in the 54 scope volumes finds four documents; the shelf that exists for the later period is the files of the Assistant Legal Adviser for Inter-American Affairs (RG 59, Lot 75 D 163, A1 5773, NARA 12014843, 1961–1971, and Lot 81 D 324, P 871, NARA 17408805, 1968–1979, both partly restricted), whose contents I have not verified. (c) *The Latin American side.* Mexico's Estrada Doctrine appears only as reported to or discussed in Washington (28 documents in 18 volumes); the foreign ministries' own deliberations are not in FRUS and the bundled data offers no shelf for them. (d) *The question's own words.* "By force" turns up in the recognition chapters at about the rate it turns up anywhere in FRUS, so it cannot find this subject; the documents say "de facto", "revolution", "coup d'état" and "junta" ("junta" about eleven times as often as elsewhere).

**Scope.** Every document in the 62 chapters in which the FRUS editors name U.S. recognition or nonrecognition of a Latin American government (1895–1950) and in five general-policy chapters, plus about 110 later documents found by the search described above — about 2,450 documents in 54 volumes, with front matter and the editors' own notes (four of them) excluded, and 11 further documents reached by searching for the policy's own formulas. All 553 FRUS volumes are in the index used, so these figures describe the whole published series as indexed, not a partial library.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Where does "came to power by force" end?** Coups and revolutions only, or also unconstitutional extensions of term (Ubico, Carías, Somoza in 1936) and rigged successions? Including them doubles the Central American material of the 1930s; excluding them loses the 1936 decision, which turned on them. Meanwhile I included any change the editors themselves filed under recognition.
- **Is "recognition" only the formal act, or also the quiet continuation of relations?** From 1948 the Department deliberately avoided the word; counting only formal recognition makes the 1950s–1970s look empty, counting continuance brings in every quiet resumption. Meanwhile I included both where a document weighs whether to deal with a new regime.
- **Mexico 1911–1923.** The Mexican chapters are almost a third of the scope; treat them as one case, or as the model? Weighting them by volume makes property guarantees the central condition; treating them as one case makes elections and constitutional succession central. Meanwhile I gave Mexico one finding.
- **Before 1895 and after 1950.** The editors' chapters set the 1895 and 1950 boundaries, not history. Extending backward (Díaz, 1877–78) and forward (the coups of 1962–1976) requires reading search results rather than the editors' selection. Meanwhile I cited two pre-1895 and eleven post-1950 documents as markers and did not count the rest.

## Findings

### 1. Before Wilson: control, acquiescence, and a price paid in promises (1877–1911)
The early test was practical and the waiting period was leverage. In 1877 Washington declined to recognize Díaz until his government showed it could last and would keep its treaties, and held, of a frontier arrangement, that "some guarantee of such an arrangement should be made the condition precedent to any recognition"; it recognized in 1878 after Mexico paid two instalments of a claims award (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222; 1878, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344). In Santo Domingo in 1903 the price was a list of four American claims and concessions (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1904, Document 228, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1904/d228). In 1907 Washington coordinated recognition of a Honduran provisional government with Mexico and asked first about a constitution and elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1907, Part II, Document 22, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1907p2/d22); in 1910–11 Nicaragua's Estrada was recognized on written pledges of reform and financial rehabilitation (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1911, Document 933, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1911/d933).
- "it waits before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico until it shall be assured that his election is approved by the Mexican people, and that his administration is possessed of stability to endure and of disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties." (1877, Document 222)
- "requests that I would name the agreements to which I have requested your excellency’s Government’s assent prior to the recognition of the present provisional government" (1904, Document 228)
- "I then asked what steps had been taken toward the establishment of constitutional government in Honduras and toward the election of a president and congress." (1907, Part II, Document 22)

### 2. Wilson's test of origin: constitutional succession and a free election (1913–1919)
In the last week of the Taft administration the Department had already ordered that "no formal recognition is to be accorded those de facto in control" in Mexico (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 873, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d873). Wilson's statement of 11 March 1913 made the manner of taking power the test (1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). In August he set Huerta's terms: an armistice, an early and free election, and Huerta's promise not to stand (1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). Against Tinoco in Costa Rica in 1917 the rule was stated flat, and Tinoco's answer, as the chargé reported it, was "recognition desirable but not necessary" (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Documents 379 and 409, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d409).
- "Cooperation is possible only when supported at every turn by the orderly processes of just government based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force." (1913, Document 7)
- "Security given for an early and free election in which all will agree to take part" (1913, Document 1028)
- "no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition." (1917, Document 379)

### 3. Mexico, 1920–1923: recognition traded for property guarantees
With Obregón the Department denied that it was setting conditions while naming them: protection of life and property, repair of damages, and above all assurance that the 1917 Constitution would not be applied retroactively to American titles, to be written into a Treaty of Amity and Commerce whose signature would itself be the act of recognition (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1920, Volume III, Document 230, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v03/d230; 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). In 1922 the sequence was negotiated as claims conventions first, then the treaty (1922, Volume II, Document 569, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1922v02/d569).
- "the United States Government does not contemplate dictating conditions on which recognition will be extended" (1920, Volume III, Document 230)
- "when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place." (1921, Volume II, Document 344)

### 4. Central America's treaty rule, 1923–1936, and its abandonment
In 1923 Hughes announced that the United States, though not a party, would follow Article II of the Central American treaty (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). Stimson in 1930 drew the line explicitly: South American revolutionary governments were recognized on the ordinary test, Central American ones were not (1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). The rule barred General Martínez of El Salvador in 1932 — "Martínez can under no circumstances be recognized." (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 618, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d618); in 1934 Welles devised a procedure by which the remaining treaty states would recognize him first, with the United States following (1934, Volume V, Document 199, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d199); in 1935 Welles read the treaty narrowly (1935, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 823, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1935v04/d823); and in 1936, after Ubico and Carías extended their own terms, the Department dropped the rule (1936, Volume V, Document 112). Outside the treaty, the same lever was used against Grau San Martín in Cuba, where the British were told that "recognition of the present regime in Cuba would be a great mistake" (1934, Volume V, Document 75, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d75).
- "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country." (1923, Volume II, Document 345, quoting Article II)
- "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule." (1930, Volume I, Document 375)
- "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (1936, Volume V, Document 112)

### 5. Consultation, then the attempt to drain recognition of meaning (1943–1950)
From December 1943 a resolution of the inter-American Committee for Political Defense at Montevideo required consultation before recognizing any government installed by force, limited to whether Axis influence was behind it; each government kept the decision (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Documents 1, 386, 1018 and 1020, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d386, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1018, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1020). The criteria named in the circulars were control, no active resistance, a pledge to meet international obligations, and no pro-Axis inspiration; El Salvador (1945) waited three months and an election, Venezuela's junta in October 1945 on the same criteria (1945, Volume IX, Documents 875 and 1172, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d875, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1172). With Haiti's military junta in 1946 the Department worried about promises of democracy broken after recognition (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The American Republics, Volume XI, Document 773, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d773). Nicaragua's Somoza-made government went unrecognized for most of a year (1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 75, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d75), until the Bogotá resolution of 1948 declared continuity of relations desirable and implying no judgment — applied to Costa Rica in May and to Peru in November (1948, Volume IX, Documents 384 and 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d384, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89). Officials then policed the separation of recognition from approval (1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 446, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d446) and, for Haiti in 1950, timed recognition so the United States would not appear to lead (1950, Volume II, Document 476, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1950v02/d476).
- "the resolution does not require a joint decision with regard to whether recognition is to be accorded; that decision remains one for the exclusive determination of each government." (1944, Volume VII, Document 1)
- "past experience with regimes in hemisphere which before recognition have promised to observe democratic practices but after recognition had been extended sometimes ignored them." (1946, Volume XI, Document 773)
- "Adoption of that resolution at Bogotá was in large measure motivated by a desire of the American Governments to separate the question of maintaining normal diplomatic relations with other American governments from the question of approval or disapproval of any newly established government." (1948, Volume IX, Document 89)

### 6. After 1950: continuity as the default, conditions bargained case by case
In 1952 Batista was recognized once he had given assurances on international obligations, private capital and communism (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 327, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d327); Bolivia's revolutionary government the same year was held to the rule that "The first prerequisite to recognition of a de facto government is that it have control of the national territory.", while tin nationalization was argued over (Document 136, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d136). For Haiti in 1957 an official argued that Bogotá put the burden of proof on withholding, and against making elections a condition (1955–1957, Volume VI, Document 353, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v06/d353). Kennedy's Department took the opposite line: for Peru in 1962 it planned to withhold recognition and aid and then asked the junta for a package — civil liberties, free elections by a date, OAS observers (1961–1963, Volume XII, Documents 417 and 423, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d417, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d423) — and in December 1963 it recognized the Dominican and Honduran regimes because withholding had stopped working (Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). In Brazil in 1964 the Under Secretary saw "no problem of U.S. recognition" because relations would simply continue (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 206, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d206); in Argentina in 1966 relations were suspended pending consultation (Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132); for Peru in 1968 the Assistant Secretary, in a telegram to the Embassy, floated moving toward the Estrada Doctrine (Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512). In Chile in 1973 the only question was timing (1969–1976, Volume XXI, Documents 360 and 361, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d360, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d361).
- "has made satisfactory public and private statements with regard to Cuban intention to fulfill its international obligations; its attitude towards private capital; and its intention to take steps to curtail international communist activities in Cuba." (1952–1954, Volume IV, Document 327)
- "Guarantee of free and fair elections, preferably before June 9 but at no later date, with full rights to all parties under Constitution, and with guarantee that Armed Forces will respect results those elections." (1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 423)
- "our developing practices as to responses to coups no longer fits exactly within the textbook doctrinal pattern" (1964–1968, Volume XXXI, Document 512)

## How this was done
I worked from a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer index (all 553 volumes) and the published TEI text. I began from the editors' tables of contents rather than from my own vocabulary: 62 chapters in which the editors name U.S. recognition or nonrecognition of a Latin American government (1895–1950) and five general-policy chapters define the core set; a proximity search (recognition near junta, coup, de facto, new, provisional or military government) supplies the post-1950 candidates, where the editors stop naming the subject. I tested the question's own phrase against a corpus baseline and dropped it ("by force" is barely more frequent in these chapters than anywhere in FRUS), and counted the spelling variants of the key terms in the TEI text with footnotes and front matter removed. Every phrase count was checked against the literal text, and one formula family ("continuity of diplomatic relations") was discarded because the index's word-folding returns other phrases. A first post-1950 search missed documents that use only the verb "recognize"; it was rerun and every archival figure recomputed (the memo shows both). Archival sources were resolved through the app's bundled NARA indexes, keeping where documents came from separate from what the editors cited. I retrieved 51 documents whole, read 45 of them, and cite 43; every quotation here (24 passages) was checked word for word against the stored text of its document, every passage is also an excerpt in the accompanying collection, and every document cited here is in that collection — I checked all three before finishing. See the memo for every query and count.
