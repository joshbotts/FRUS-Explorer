# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series, *Foreign Relations of the United States* (FRUS), answers both halves of the question, and mostly in the Department of State’s own instructions. From 1877 to 1949 the Department described its test for a government that had taken power by force in nearly the same words: does it hold the machinery of government, does the population accept it, and can and will it meet the state’s international obligations (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). In two periods Washington demanded more than that: under Wilson it asked for constitutional legitimacy (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7), and from 1923 to 1936 it followed a Central American treaty that barred recognition of any government that took power by coup or revolution (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232; Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 109, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d109). What it asked of the new rulers before recognizing them was never one fixed price. It asked for assurances about treaties and the treatment of foreigners (Mexico, 1877; Chile, 1932), for a treaty (Haiti, 1915, giving the United States financial control and a right to intervene; Mexico, 1921, protecting American property), for proof of popular support (Cuba, 1934), for a date for elections or for civilian rule (the Dominican Republic and Ecuador, 1963), and for a clean record against the Axis or against communism (Bolivia, 1943–44 and 1964); from 1943 it also asked the other American republics. The raw material is large: about 2,900 of the 57,000 documents in the Latin American parts of the series, roughly one in twenty, mention recognition close to the word government, regime, junta or administration, and about three in five of those, judging from a sample of forty, are about recognizing or refusing to recognize a Latin American government. Forty-five documents carry the argument, and the collection that goes with this overview holds them.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** There are two channels, reported separately and never added together. *Came from* is the archive each printed document was taken from. Most of them — 30 of the 45 documents I rely on, and about two in three of the 2,900 candidates — carry file numbers from the Department of State’s central decimal file for 1910–1963, which is part of Record Group 59 (the Department’s records at the National Archives, NARA); the reference data shipped with the app gives this file no NARA identifier, entry number or access status, and I have not supplied them from memory. For 1963–73 the source is the Department’s subject-numeric central files (also Record Group 59), whose headings for independence and recognition (POL 16) and for rebellion and coups (POL 26, later POL 23-9) hold the recognition files; again no identifier is given. Two lots of the Department’s working files supply documents I rely on (a lot is a batch of an office’s files, cited by number): the Policy Planning Staff papers (lot 64 D 563, which NARA divides among twelve series, so the paper itself may sit in any of them; the staff studies of 1947–49, NARA 1151288, entry A1 558-A, dates 1947–1949, unrestricted, are the likeliest) and the Department’s National Security Council files (lot 62 D 1, which NARA divides among three series, so a paper may sit in any of them: Alphabetical Files, NARA 2198167, entries A1 1583A–B, 1948–1961, partly restricted; Numerical Files, NARA 2838991, entries A1 1583C–D, 1947–1961, access status not given, the likeliest home of a numbered policy paper; and Records Relating to Activities with the National Security Council, NARA 2838992, entries A1 1583E–F, 1948–1961, partly restricted). A smaller group of later documents comes from presidential libraries, whose collections the reference data resolves to the libraries’ own finding aids and, in the catalog it ships, to NARA identifiers and dates but not access status: the Kennedy Library’s National Security Files (NARA 1164; its Country Files, NARA 193236, 1961–1963), the Johnson Library’s National Security Files (NARA 567979; its Latin America Country Files, NARA 567985, 1963–1969) and the Nixon Library’s National Security Council Files (the catalog’s Institutional Files, NARA 572247, 1969–1974, are the likeliest match for the Institutional (H) Files cited); the Eisenhower Library’s Project Clean Up records are in neither the catalog nor the finding aids. The nineteenth-century documents print no source note (nearly a quarter of the candidates, almost all before 1910); the reference data places such instructions on NARA’s microfilmed volumes of Diplomatic Instructions (series 593313; Mexico, 1873–79, is roll 149324017, and Colombia, 1898–1906, roll 149302787) and Despatches (series 603720), which is an inference from country and date, not a citation. *Pointed at* is what the editors’ footnotes cite without printing, and it is thin: only about one candidate in fourteen carries such a footnote, naming central-file classes (about 150 documents, 1912–62), lot files of Department offices, most of them the Bureau of Inter-American Affairs’ (about 35, 1950–80) or presidential libraries (about 35, 1955–80). Among those lots are the Assistant Secretary’s subject files for 1957–59 (lots 59 D 573 and 61 D 279; NARA 2108779; entry A1 1135; dates 1957–1959; unrestricted), the Central American and Panamanian office’s subject and country files (lot 63 D 127; NARA 2363840; A1 3157; 1955–1963; restricted possibly), the El Salvador records (lot 64 D 16; NARA 1750062; A1 3159; 1958–1975; partly restricted) and the Ecuador subject files (lot 70 D 247; NARA 26309336; A1 5741; 1964–1978; partly restricted). Recorded footnote citations begin in December 1910, in the Latin American parts and in the whole series alike, so for the nineteenth century this channel is empty, not negative.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) *The Department’s own reasoning before 1920.* I searched the Latin American parts of all 553 volumes for recognition within six words of a government, regime, junta or administration. That finds about 1,150 documents dated 1861–1919, of which only six are internal memoranda, papers or minutes, against more than half (263 of 476) in 1950–79; the same test on document headings finds tens of thousands of memoranda across the series and nothing for a phrase that cannot occur. Before 1920 the printed record is the instruction going out and the despatch coming in; the reasoning is on the shelf, in the microfilmed Diplomatic Instructions and Despatches, in the Department’s Numerical File on microfilm (series NARA 654171, which the data indexes by case number, not by date) and, from 1910, in the central decimal file. (b) *The labels historians use.* “Wilson Doctrine” and “Mann Doctrine” appear in no document of the series, “Tobar Doctrine” in one, “Betancourt Doctrine” in three and “Estrada Doctrine” in 25 of the Latin American documents (the phrase reads literally the same way in 24 of the 25; the other puts Estrada in quotation marks). Officials described their own rule as the traditional policy, so a search for doctrine names finds the Latin American rules (Estrada’s, Betancourt’s, Tobar’s), not the American one. (c) *The new governments’ own reasoning.* Spanish recognition vocabulary appears in only eleven of the 57,000 documents, so what the new governments said reaches the series almost entirely in English, in American reports and translations; their foreign ministries’ files are the shelf, and none of the archival reference files shipped with the app lists them (a search of that data for the ministries’ names in English and Spanish found them only in its word-frequency tables and its registry of persons). (d) *The 1980s.* For 1981–88 the library holds one volume on South America and one on the South Atlantic conflict, and none on Central America or the Caribbean; the Latin American scope has only about 400 documents dated in the 1980s, against about 3,200 for the 1970s, so Reagan-era recognition decisions cannot be answered from it. The shelf is at the Reagan Library, and the finding aids shipped with the app name it: the National Security Council’s Latin American Affairs Directorate records (NARA 12024796; series dated 1983–1989) and the White House Central Files’ subject files on South and Central America and on Argentina, Bolivia, Chile, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua and Panama (1981–1989); that data gives their identifiers and dates but no access status. The controls for these searches were a phrase that must occur in nearly every volume (it did in 552 of 553) and an impossible phrase that must occur in none (it occurred in none).

**Scope.** I searched the Latin American material in all 553 volumes of the series as indexed on 25 October 2026: the 46 volumes devoted to the hemisphere in full and the Latin American sections of 111 other volumes, about 57,000 documents dated from 1861 to 1988. I left out front matter, the editors’ notes, chapter headings and the duplicate second editions of two unrelated volumes, and I used the editors’ own chapter titles as evidence of what they thought the topic was.

## Decisions this needs from you

1. **What counts as recognition:** counting only formal acts (a note of recognition or an explicit refusal) keeps mainly the chapters the editors titled for recognition, which stop in 1950, while counting continued or resumed relations without a formal act (Brazil in 1937; the Bogotá resolution of 1948) brings in the Cold War cases; meanwhile I counted both and say which is which.
2. **What counts as “by force”:** coups and revolutions only keeps Bolivia in 1920–21 and Chile in 1932 but drops irregular successions without violence, while any change outside the constitution adds cases such as Nicaragua in 1925–26; meanwhile I took any change of government that the editors or the Department treated as raising a recognition question.
3. **What counts as Latin America:** all twenty republics brings in Cuba and Haiti, which the United States had bound by treaty, and the Dominican Republic, while leaving those three out removes Haiti in 1915 (recognition tied to a treaty) and the Cuban decisions of 1933–34; meanwhile I included them.
4. **How far back to go:** the series runs from 1861 to 1988, and the tests as written in 1877 and 1900 matter if you want continuity, but if you want only the inter-American system after 1945 findings 5 and 6 stand alone and the others become background; meanwhile I used the whole span.
5. **What “asked” means:** an explicit condition put to the government gives Haiti in 1915, Mexico in 1921 and the Dominican Republic in 1963 as the clearest cases, while assurances sought and obtained bring in Chile in 1932, Ecuador and Guatemala in 1963 and Bolivia in 1964, although officials sometimes said they were not bargaining; meanwhile I counted both and mark where the Department denied bargaining.
6. **Asked, or obtained:** the series prints what the Department wanted and what the new government said, and seldom whether the promise was kept (in Guatemala in 1963 no public timetable for elections was obtained), so enforcement is a question for other sources; meanwhile I report only what was asked and what was answered.

## Findings

### 1. The stated test: control, consent and the ability to meet obligations

In September 1900 the Department told its minister in Bogotá, after the vice-president had taken over the executive of Colombia, that its rule was to defer recognition of a new executive until it held the machinery of the state with the assent of the people and could meet the state’s obligations. When Secretary of State Stimson announced on 17 September 1930 that the United States would recognize the provisional governments of Argentina, Bolivia and Peru the next day, he gave the same grounds — control, no active resistance, and an intention to meet obligations and to hold elections in due course — and called them the regular policy since Jefferson (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). In 1937 a cable to the chargé in Bolivia asked whether a new government met the criteria of the Department’s recognition policy and put four questions: its stability and hold on order, the public’s support or opposition, the attitude of other countries, and what the regime meant to do to secure recognition (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1937, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 265, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1937v05/d265). The Policy Planning Staff paper of 15 March 1948 turned the practice into three factors and advised against withholding recognition as a political weapon; the following year Truman told the ousted president of Venezuela why the United States had nonetheless resumed relations with the junta that overthrew him, tracing the practice back to Jefferson.

The rule of 1900, as the Acting Secretary of State put it to the minister in Bogotá:

> “the rule of the United States is to defer recognition of another executive in its place until it shall appear that it is in possession of the machinery of the state, administering government with the assent of the people thereof and without substantial resistance to its authority, and that it is in a position to fulfill all the international obligations and responsibilities incumbent upon a sovereign state under treaties and international law.”
>
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1900, Document 530, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1900/d530

The three factors of 1948:

> “(a) de facto control of the territory and the administrative machinery of State, including the maintenance of public order; (b) the ability and willingness of a government to discharge its international obligations; (c) general acquiescence of the people of a country in the government in power.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7

And the advice in the same paper:

> “Recognition should not be withheld as a political weapon or to express moral censure except in extreme cases when U.S. national interests would be served thereby.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7

Truman’s letter of 3 February 1949:

> “This Government, furthermore, is of the opinion that nonrecognition is seldom, if ever, effective in bringing about the broader aim of strengthening democratic governments.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 489, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d489

### 2. When the test was tightened: constitutional legitimacy under Wilson, a treaty rule in Central America

Wilson’s statement of 11 March 1913 was wired to the American diplomatic officers in Latin America and put the consent of the governed and rule by law at the head of policy. His envoy Lind carried to Mexico a proposal for a settlement conditioned on an armistice, an early free election, Huerta’s agreement not to stand and everyone’s acceptance of the result, with a pledge to recognize the administration so chosen; Mexico rejected it, and Wilson laid the terms and the rejection before Congress on 27 August (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 1028, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d1028). The test was applied to Costa Rica, where the Secretary of State told the President in 1919 that the United States had recognized neither a de jure nor a legitimately de facto government (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1919, Volume I, Document 867, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1919v01/d867), and to Bolivia, where the United States, Argentina and Brazil postponed recognition until the new government had shown that it represented the will of the great majority of the Bolivian people and had been established under the constitution as amended by a constitutional convention, and then recognized it as the constitutional government, not the de facto one (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume I, Document 238, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v01/d238). The Department’s own staff paper of 1948 recalled that a requirement of constitutionality had been a factor in the Civil War and under Wilson (FRUS 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7). For Central America the rule was written into a treaty. The Department adopted Article II of the 1923 treaty as its own guide and set out in December 1925 how it would apply it to Nicaragua (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1925, Volume II, Document 498, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1925v02/d498); it refused to recognize General Martínez in El Salvador in December 1931; after El Salvador and Costa Rica had denounced the treaty and Guatemala, Nicaragua and Honduras had recognized Martínez, the Assistant Secretary of State asked President Roosevelt on 25 January 1934 to approve recognition the next day, and the approval is dated 26 January (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 255, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d255); and in 1936 it decided to stop being guided by Article II.

Wilson’s statement, as telegraphed to the missions on 12 March 1913:

> “just government rests always upon the consent of the governed, and that there can be no freedom without order based upon law and upon the public conscience and approval.”
>
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7

The instruction of 20 December 1931 on General Martínez:

> “The Department has stated on various occasions that the policy of the Government of the United States in its dealings with the Central American republics is to be guided by the principles established in the Treaty of 1923. The United States Government, therefore, will not recognize General Martínez as President of Salvador.”
>
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1931, Volume II, Document 232, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931v02/d232

The Department’s own account of the change, 21 April 1936:

> “Under our new policy, if General Somoza headed a régime which effectively governed the country and fulfilled its international obligations we would extend recognition to him regardless of his eligibility to recognition under the 1923 Treaty.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 109, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d109

### 3. What was asked in return, first part: assurances, and in two cases a treaty

In May 1877 the Department made recognition of Porfirio Díaz wait until it was assured that the Mexican people approved and that his government was stable and would keep its treaties, and said that some guarantee of an arrangement on frontier raids and its other complaints should be the condition precedent to any recognition; in March 1878 the President recognized him on the strength of the peace Díaz had kept, firmer measures against raids and two payments on the claims award, waiving the prior guarantee (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1878, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d344). In Haiti in December 1914 Bryan said recognition would come when there was a government able to keep order and meet its obligations and resting on the consent of the governed, and asked for the government’s fiscal position and its attitude to foreigners (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914, Document 567, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1914/d567); in August 1915 Lansing told the chargé to recognize the president-elect when Congress authorized a treaty and at the same moment to sign it — a treaty that set up a financial adviser and receivership, a constabulary organized by Americans and a United States right to intervene (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, Document 448, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1915/d448). In Mexico in June 1921 Hughes said publicly that recognition would come with the government’s readiness to bind itself by treaty to protect property. In Chile in 1932 Stimson held back from the socialist regime of June, citing doubts about its stability and what it threatened for foreigners’ property; in October, of the government formed under Vice-President Oyanedel, which claimed to be the constitutional successor of the Montero government, he insisted that the Department was not bargaining while still asking for some assurance of respect for international obligations (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 560, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d560), the foreign minister gave one in a letter (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 561, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d561), and recognition followed on 21 October (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 567, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d567).

The instruction of 16 May 1877 on Díaz:

> “In the present case it waits before recognizing General Diaz as the President of Mexico until it shall be assured that his election is approved by the Mexican people, and that his administration is possessed of stability to endure and of disposition to comply with the rules of international comity and the obligations of treaties.”
>
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1877, Document 222, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1877/d222

Hughes’s public statement of 8 June 1921, telegraphed to the chargé in Mexico:

> “In short, when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place.”
>
> — Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344

Stimson’s instruction on Chile, 16 July 1932:

> “You are correct in your understanding that the policy of this Government is not to associate recognition with the particular type of government or political institution which the people may decide to adopt. In this connection there is a two-fold criterion: First, that there should be no discrimination in the protection which must be accorded to the nationals of other countries and their property, to both of which must be accorded a degree of protection at least as high as that given to the natives of the country, and secondly, that this standard of protection must not fall below the minimum standard set by international law and practice.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 518, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d518

### 4. What was asked in return, second part: popular support, elections, a date for civilian rule

In January 1934 the President’s decision on Cuba was to recognize a provisional government supported by the people and able to keep order, but to promise recognition to no one in advance. By 1963 the demands had become lists: for the Dominican Republic, seven points to be agreed before recognition or resumed aid; for Ecuador, a public statement of a return to civilian government within about a year, with recognition left until several Latin American states had acted (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics; Cuba 1961–1962; Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 109, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d109); in Guatemala only a verbal assurance of elections within about two years, and the United States recognized anyway (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volumes X/XI/XII, Microfiche Supplement, American Republics; Cuba 1961–1962; Cuban Missile Crisis and Aftermath, Document 137, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v10-12mSupp/d137); and in Bolivia in 1964, private pledges to reduce communist influence and not to restore relations with Cuba or Czechoslovakia (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 152, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d152).

Quoting the President on Cuba, 14 January 1934:

> “This Government will recognize a Cuban provisional Government which is substantially supported by the Cuban people and is able to maintain law, order and the normal functions of Government.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 85, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d85

The instruction on the Dominican Republic, 4 October 1963:

> “to secure their agreement prior to our recognition or resumption of aid on following main points: 1. Free political activity of all non-Communist parties in preparation for OAS supervised elections in not more than one year, with provision for drafting new constitution thereafter. 2. Restoration of some semblance of constitutionality to present regime and recognition of normal role of PRD in DomRep by substituting for one member of the triumvirate Juan Casasnovas as President.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359

### 5. From 1943 the other American republics were asked first

In June 1943 the American envoys in Buenos Aires agreed to act together on the new Argentine government, and the United States said it did not wish to bargain with it or to make a break with the Axis a condition of recognition, only to know its intentions (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 335, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d335). In January 1944 the Department circulated its evidence that Bolivia’s junta was pro-Nazi and said recognition was unlikely; by the end of the month all nineteen republics in the consultations had publicly refused recognition (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 403, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d403). Once the party leaders accused of pro-Nazi ties had left the government and Axis aliens had been expelled (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 432, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d432), the United States said on 17 June that it was willing to recognize on 23 June, the date Mexico had suggested, if that was the consensus of the American governments (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 437, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d437). In October 1945 the test for Venezuela’s junta was control, general support without active opposition, an intention to meet obligations and no pro-Axis inspiration, and the Department proposed to consult the other republics first (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1945, The American Republics, Volume IX, Document 1170, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945v09/d1170). At Bogotá in 1948 the American states resolved that continuity of diplomatic relations was desirable and implied no judgment on a government’s domestic policy (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 74, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d74).

Hull’s circular of 10 January 1944 on Bolivia:

> “You should indicate to the Minister of Foreign Affairs that the available evidence indicating foreign involvement in the Bolivian revolt is considered here to be very strong and that it is unlikely that this government will recognize the new regime.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 388, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d388

The Acting Secretary of State to El Salvador, 27 October 1944:

> “Recognition of the new Administration in El Salvador will depend in part on its willingness and ability to fulfill its international commitments, including that to the other American Republics not to recognize unilaterally the present Government of Argentina.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 1090, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1090

Kennan on Venezuela, 10 December 1948:

> “(d) In the light of the above, it seems evident to us that non-recognition is not a suitable weapon for influencing the course of internal events in Venezuela at this time.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 115, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d115

### 6. After 1948: recognition by default, withholding as a short lever

With the three factors and the Bogotá rule, recognition became the default. In Guatemala on 28 June 1954 the Ambassador told the three colonels who had just announced a military junta that Washington would want a reasonable period to see what they did, and that one of the criteria for recognition was control of the country’s territory, which they lacked while Castillo Armas controlled a portion of it (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 490, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d490). On 9 July the Secretary of State told President Eisenhower that the new government, formally established on 2 July, appeared to control the entire territory and had sent a formal note saying it was prepared to fulfill Guatemala’s international obligations; he proposed to tell the other American republics, and London, Paris and Ottawa, that the United States was considering recognition on 13 July and to ask the republics’ views, a delay that would let others recognize first or coordinate so that the United States would not be conspicuously in the lead or behind; the editors record that recognition was extended on 13 July (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1952–1954, The American Republics, Volume IV, Document 502, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d502). A National Security Council policy of February 1959 told the government to recognize all Latin American governments that qualified under the accepted criteria of international law unless a substantial question arose about communist control (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, American Republics, Volume V, Document 11, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v05/d11), and Cuba’s Provisional Government was recognized on 7 January 1959 on its own statement of control and commitments and its freedom from communist taint (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1958–1960, Cuba, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). Withholding had become a short lever: the United States held back from the Dominican Republic until 14 December 1963, and the Department then explained that withholding had won some gains but that further influence by non-recognition was virtually nil and continuing it would undo them (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 363, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d363). The inter-American conference at Rio in 1965 recommended consulting before recognizing a de facto government and weighing foreign involvement, a promise of elections, respect for obligations and respect for human rights (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132).

Haiti, July 1957, after seven months in which the United States had stood aside from successive Haitian governments:

> “While fully appreciating significance of fair and free elections Department would prefer recognition independent of election pledge from Junta.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Multilateral; Mexico; Caribbean, Volume VI, Document 354, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v06/d354

In the same telegram:

> “does not wish to find itself recognizing a new de facto regime every few weeks and would like reasonable assurance that Junta is actually in effective control of country as well as ready to abide by nation’s international obligations.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, American Republics: Multilateral; Mexico; Caribbean, Volume VI, Document 354, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v06/d354

Peru, 19 October 1968, as the President’s adviser put it to him:

> “The Military Government is in full control, and no significant opposition has materialized. It has met all the traditional tests for recognition.”
>
> — Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 518, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d518

Chile, 20 September 1973, at the Washington Special Actions Group chaired by Kissinger, a State Department official answering his question about the normal procedure:

> “Right now would be a good time. They have satisfied the requirements.”
>

## How this was done

I searched the full-text index of all 553 volumes, which treats different forms of a word as the same word (it would treat “clearing agreement” and “clear agreement” alike), so every phrase count was checked against the literal text. I defined the Latin American material by volume and by the editors’ section headings (157 volumes, about 57,000 documents, with front matter, editors’ notes, chapter headings and duplicate second editions removed), read the editors’ own chapter titles, which name recognition in 62 chapters holding about 2,250 documents between 1895 and 1950, and searched for recognition within six words of government, regime, junta or administration. To test that search I read forty of its hits: 23 concerned recognizing a Latin American government, 2 concerned recognition of exile governments and 15 used the words in other senses; the bare word “recognition” is no more common in these sections than elsewhere (about 14 documents in 100 against 16), so I did not use it alone. I counted spelling variants in the text of the volumes themselves, which the index cannot do: the hyphenated “non-recognition” is nearly twice as common as “nonrecognition” (123 documents against 65), and about one document in forty that has recognition near government, regime, junta or administration has it only in a footnote or the source note. I retrieved 106 documents whole, read 96 of them end to end on screen and the rest in part, and quote 17. I checked that every quotation in this overview and every excerpt in the collection is one the memo verified against the text of its document, and that every document cited here is in the collection. The exact counts, the queries behind them and the files that hold them are in the memo.
