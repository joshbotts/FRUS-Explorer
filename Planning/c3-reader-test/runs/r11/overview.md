# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** The series documents the decision rule and the price of recognition across a century, and the rule changed four times. Until about 1913 Washington recognized whoever held the machinery of government with the people's acquiescence and would honor treaties (Papers Relating to Foreign Affairs, 1868, Part II, Document 632, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1868p2/d632; Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1895, Part I, Document 246, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d246). From Wilson to 1934 it added a test of constitutional origin, written for Central America into the region's 1923 treaty, which barred coup governments until freely elected representatives had reorganized the country (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). In Mexico it asked instead for guarantees of American property (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). In 1930 Stimson stated the working test for South America: effective control, no active resistance, a promise to meet international obligations and to hold elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). The Department dropped the treaty rule in 1936 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). During the war it made recognition a collective decision tied to hemisphere defense (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33). After the 1948 Bogotá conference it treated recognition as carrying no approval, while in the 1960s it still bargained for elections and political terms before resuming relations (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359). The evidence is a set of about 940 documents in 87 volumes. It is thickest between 1910 and 1939. The argument below rests on 26 of them, all read whole.

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* nearly nine in ten of those with a stated source come from the Department of State's Central Decimal File (Record Group 59), 1910 to 1963, and above all from the per-country "Government" subfiles. These subfiles carry the recognition correspondence: El Salvador's (816.01) is the largest, then Bolivia's (824.01), Argentina's (835.01), Ecuador's (822.01) and Guatemala's (814.01). The country "Political affairs" files of Mexico (812.00), Bolivia (824.00) and Costa Rica (818.00) come next. The app's bundled archival data gives no entry number, catalog identifier or access status for the Central Decimal File itself. Before 1906 the documents are ministers' despatches and the Department's instructions. These sit in the National Archives' microfilmed Despatches from U.S. Ministers (series identifier 603720; for example Ecuador, file unit 177380753, 1848–1906; Colombia 177380752, 1820–1906; Venezuela 177380736, 1835–1906) and Diplomatic Instructions (series identifier 593313); the bundled data gives no access status for these either. Each of a handful of post-1950 documents comes from a Department lot file, a set of office records retired as one block. Two examples are Record Group 59, entry A1 3159, Records Relating to El Salvador (identifier 1750062, 1958–1975, partly restricted), and entry A1 1586E, Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (identifier 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted). *What the editors cited without printing:* only about one document in fourteen carries such a citation. Most point back into the same decimal files (Bolivia, Haiti, El Salvador, Cuba, Peru). The lot files they name are: Record Group 59, entry A1 3157, Subject and Country Files of the Office of Central American and Panamanian Affairs (identifier 2363840, 1955–1963, possibly restricted); entry A1 1135, Subject Files of the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs (identifier 2108779, 1957–1959, unrestricted); entry A1 3147 (identifier 2068466, 1959–1962, partly restricted); and entry A1 5376, Speeches and Statements (identifier 631695, 1961–1969, unrestricted). The editors also cite the Johnson Library's Papers of George W. Ball (Brazil file, 1964–1966) and the Eisenhower Library's Dulles telephone conversations. The two lists are separate channels and are not added together. The editors' citations that the index records begin only in late 1910, so for earlier decades this second channel is empty.

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** (a) The Latin American side of the argument in its own words. Mexico's 1930 Estrada declaration, which rejected the practice of recognition, is not printed. A search for "Estrada" with any form of "recognize" in documents dated January 1929 to June 1931 found two documents, and neither is the declaration. Across all 553 volumes the doctrine is named in 28 documents, the earliest from 1931, always second-hand. The shelf is the Mexican foreign ministry's archive, which the app's archival data does not cover. (b) The Department's own 1947 internal study of recognition policy. The 1948 Policy Planning Staff paper relies on it, and the editors note that it was not printed and give no file number, so no shelf can be named. (c) Decisions after 1980. Only one document in the scope is dated in the 1980s, against about 6,000 dated documents from that decade. The corpus is not silent on the decade; it prints almost nothing on recognizing coup governments in it. A lead for the late 1970s (eight documents that place "recognize" near "states" and "governments") was found but not read. All searches ran with a control phrase found in every volume searched and an impossible string found in none.

**Scope.** About 940 documents from 87 of the 553 volumes, dated 1863 to 1980. They sit in the editors' chapters on recognizing a Latin American government, or in Latin American chapters where "recognize" stands within six words of junta, regime, de facto, provisional, military or new government. Front matter and editorial notes were excluded, and so were second editions; the wider net is about four-fifths on topic by a hand check (see the memo).

## Decisions this needs from you

- **What counts as "by force".** I counted any irregular change the editors or diplomats called a revolution, coup, junta or de facto government, and also the disputed elections that Washington treated the same way. Restricting to military coups would drop the nineteenth-century cases and most of the Mexican revolution; widening to every unconstitutional succession would bring in self-coups like Vargas's in 1937, which Washington never treated as a recognition question. The question's own phrase is no help: "by force" is no more common in the recognition chapters than in the corpus at large (see the memo).
- **Recognition versus resuming relations.** After 1948 Washington often suspended and then resumed relations instead of withholding and then granting recognition. I counted both as the same decision. Treating only formal recognition as the subject would shrink the post-1948 evidence to a few documents.
- **Whether Mexico, 1913–1923, belongs.** Huerta, Carranza and Obregón dominate the 1910s and 1920s. Keeping them makes property guarantees a central demand; dropping them leaves the Central American treaty rule as the main story of those decades. I kept them.
- **Whether Cuba, Haiti, Panama and the Dominican Republic under treaty or occupation belong.** I kept them but cite none in the findings, because Stimson himself called those treaty relationships exceptions to the general rule.

## Findings

### 1. Before 1913: control of the government and the people's acquiescence

Nineteenth-century instructions asked two things of a new government: that it hold the state's machinery, and that its people accept it. A third requirement, respect for treaty obligations, was often added. Ministers could do business with de facto authorities, but formal recognition waited on Washington. Seward told the minister to Colombia in 1867: "It has been the practice of this government to refrain from recognizing the various revolutionary changes which have occurred in the neighboring States of this continent until it had received satisfactory evidence of the acquiescence of the people concerned therein." (Papers Relating to Foreign Affairs, 1868, Part II, Document 632, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1868p2/d632). Olney put the whole test in 1895 for Alfaro's Ecuador: "the practice of this Government has been to enter into effective relations with the de facto government when it shall have been fully established with the general consent of the people." Once he was "satisfied that the new Government is in possession of the executive forces of the nation, and administering the same with due regard for the obligations of international law and treaties, you will enter into full relations with it." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1895, Part I, Document 246, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d246; see also Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d244, and for Venezuela in 1868, Papers Relating to Foreign Affairs, 1868, Part II, Document 598, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1868p2/d598). In 1899 Hay simply ignored Castro's seizure of Venezuela until formally asked to recognize it (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1899, Document 805, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1899/d805).

### 2. 1913–1934: a test of constitutional origin, and the Central American treaty rule

Wilson made the manner of taking power itself a test: "Cooperation is possible only when supported at every turn by the orderly processes of just government based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). Lansing applied it to Tinoco's coup in Costa Rica: "no Government except such as may be elected legally and established according to the Constitution shall be considered entitled to recognition." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1917, Document 379, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d379). For Central America, Hughes in 1923 adopted Article II of the region's own treaty: its signatories "will not recognize any other Government which may come into power in any of the five Republics through a coup d’état or a revolution against a recognized Government, so long as the freely elected representatives of the people thereof have not constitutionally reorganized the country." The same article went on to bar coup leaders and their relatives from the presidency even after elections (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1923, Volume II, Document 345, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1923v02/d345). The rule was applied without exception to Martínez in El Salvador in 1932 (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1932, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 618, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d618).

### 3. What Washington asked for: obligations, property and a promise of elections

Outside the treaty zone, the price of recognition was a commitment to meet obligations to foreigners. In 1921 Hughes made recognition of Obregón follow from a treaty protecting American property titles that predated Mexico's 1917 constitution: "when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). Stimson's 1930 statement on Argentina, Peru and Bolivia set out the working test for South America: "the evidence has satisfied me that these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status." (Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375).

### 4. Giving up the treaty rule, 1934–1936

The treaty rule ended when the Central American states themselves gave it up. In January 1934 Welles backed a Nicaraguan plan for "recognition of the Government of Salvador by the Governments of Guatemala, Honduras and Nicaragua, and subsequently by the Government of the United States". This was Martínez's government, the one refused two years earlier (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1934, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 220, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1934v05/d220). In 1936 a confidential instruction stated that "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112).

### 5. Wartime: consultation, with hemisphere defense as the test

In December 1943 the inter-American political defense committee in Montevideo recommended that the American governments "do not proceed to the recognition of a new government instituted by force, before consulting among themselves for the purpose of determining whether this government complies with the Inter-American undertakings for the defense of the continent" (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1943, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 33, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d33). The test became concrete in Bolivia: before it was recognized, the new government let the United States help it deport Axis nationals. Hull reported that it "wished to expel from Bolivia the most dangerous Axis aliens", while denying any link to recognition, which he said was for the consulting republics to decide (Foreign Relations of the United States, Diplomatic Papers, 1944, The American Republics, Volume VII, Document 428, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d428; see also Documents 438 and 1088, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d438, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1088). By 1946 the checklist for Haiti's military junta was the older one again. It "controls machinery of Govt and is maintaining order, appears to enjoy popular support and has promised to fulfill its international commitments", with elections promised. Washington still waited for a consensus of the other republics (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1946, The American Republics, Volume XI, Document 773, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1946v11/d773).

### 6. After Bogotá: relations without approval, and terms negotiated case by case

In 1948 a Policy Planning Staff paper weighed cutting the test to two criteria, effective control and the ability to meet international obligations, (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1948, The Western Hemisphere, Volume IX, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d7). The Bogotá conference declared that diplomatic relations imply no judgment on a government's domestic policy. Even so, after the Venezuelan coup that year Lovett wrote that "An important consideration in this Govt’s approval Bogotá Res 35 was belief that non-recognition is not a suitable approach to much broader problem of dictatorships and use of force as a political procedure" (same volume, Document 119, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d119; compare Paraguay, Foreign Relations of the United States, 1949, The United Nations; The Western Hemisphere, Volume II, Document 454, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d454). Under Kennedy the bargaining returned: Washington suspended relations with Peru's junta in 1962 (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1961–1963, Volume XII, American Republics, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421). In 1963 it told the Dominican triumvirate it would negotiate "to secure their agreement prior to our recognition or resumption of aid on following main points: 1. Free political activity of all non-Communist parties in preparation for OAS supervised elections in not more than one year" (same volume, Document 359, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d359). After Argentina's 1966 coup the Department wrote: "In keeping with international practice in such cases, diplomatic relations are suspended." The volume's editors explain the consultation it promised. The 1965 Rio resolution asked governments to weigh "whether the new regime promised to hold free elections, to honor its international obligations, and to respect human rights" (that wording is the editors' summary, not the Department's; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1964–1968, Volume XXXI, South and Central America; Mexico, Document 132, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d132). By 1968 officials were questioning the doctrine itself (same volume, Document 512, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v31/d512). On 20 September 1973 the White House crisis group agreed to announce recognition of Chile's junta on 24 September; the State Department's representative called the new government "an established fact" (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Chile, 1969–1973, Document 361, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d361).

## How this was done

I worked from the full FRUS Explorer index of all 553 volumes. I began with the editors' own chapter headings, read from the volumes' original XML, because their repeating formula (Recognition of the named Government; Withholding of recognition from the named regime) marks the subject more reliably than modern phrasing. The scope combined documents in those chapters that mention recognition; documents in Latin American chapters where "recognize" in any form stands within six words of junta, regime, de facto, provisional, military or new government; and two policy statements found by phrase search. The index treats words that share a root ("recognize", "recognition") as one word, so every phrase count was checked against the literal text of every hit. A control phrase present in every volume and an impossible string were run alongside the searches. Periods are dated by each document's own date, not the volume's year. The wider net was hand-checked on an evenly spaced sample. Archival sources were traced through two separate channels: the source note stating where each printed document came from, and the editors' footnotes citing unprinted files. Both were resolved with the app's bundled finding aids. File numbers were glossed only where the document's date falls within the classification scheme then in use. I read 39 documents in full. Every quotation here was matched character for character against its document. Every document cited in this overview is in the accompanying collection, and every quotation in the overview and every excerpt in the collection was verified against its document; I checked both before finishing.
