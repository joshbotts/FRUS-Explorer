What FRUS answers directly: across the whole published series, about twelve hundred documents in 73 volumes deal with whether Washington would recognize a Latin American government that had taken power by force. Three tests recur from 1895 to 1949: the new authority controls the country, it means to meet its international obligations, and its people accept it. Stimson's recognition of the Argentine, Peruvian and Bolivian juntas in September 1930 states all three and adds a promise of elections. From 1913 to 1936 Washington added a fourth test, how the government came to power. That test came from Wilson's March 1913 statement and from the Central American treaties of 1907 and 1923, which barred recognition of governments born of a coup until they were constitutionally reorganized. In practice recognition was also traded for specific undertakings: on Mexican oil and land law (Article 27) and claims in 1920–1923, on Axis influence in 1943–1944 (worked out in consultation with the other republics), and later on communism, elections and civil rights. After the Bogota conference's Resolution XXXV (1948), relations were to continue with any government without implying approval.
Where FRUS points for the rest: the printed documents came overwhelmingly from the State Department's Central Decimal File (Record Group 59), from the Mexico, El Salvador, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Haiti, Peru, Guatemala, Ecuador and Argentina political-affairs and government files. The bundled data names that file but gives no NARA identifier, entry or access status for it. After 1950 a few documents came from Bureau of Inter-American Affairs and Executive Secretariat lot files, for example Subject and Country Files (NAID 1074073, entry A1 5763, 1962–1975, partly restricted) and Records Relating to Honduras (NAID 2602882, entry A1 5732, 1957–1974, partly restricted). The editors' footnotes point, without printing them, to further decimal files (Cuba 737.00 and Peru 723.00 among them), to the Johnson Library's National Security File, to Carter's national-security-affairs files, and to lot files such as Subject Files of the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs (NAID 2108779, entry A1 1135, 1957–1959, unrestricted).
What this request cannot get from FRUS: the Latin American side of the question (the Estrada doctrine named in 27 documents, the Tobar doctrine named in one, the Betancourt doctrine in three) lives in foreign archives that no bundled index reaches. The Department's 8 June 1948 circular instruction on the three conditions is cited but not printed. The published series this index holds has no Central America volume after 1980. Before 1910 the archival roadmap reaches only the despatch and instruction series on microfilm.
