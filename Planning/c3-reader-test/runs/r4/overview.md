# How did the United States decide whether to recognize governments that came to power by force in Latin America, and what did it ask of them before recognizing?

## The bottom line

**1. What FRUS answers directly.** Across the whole published series (all 553 volumes are in this library), about twelve hundred documents in 73 volumes deal with whether Washington would recognize, or keep relations with, a Latin American government that had taken power by force. From 1895 to 1949 the Department applied three tests again and again: whether the new authority actually controlled the country, whether it meant to honour its international obligations, and whether its people accepted it. An instruction of 1895 on Ecuador (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1895, Part I, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d244) states them, and so do the February 1913 "usual tests" applied to Huerta (Foreign Relations, 1913, Document 894, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d894). Stimson's recognition of the Argentine, Peruvian and Bolivian juntas in September 1930 (Foreign Relations, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375) states all three and adds a promise of elections. In 1913 Wilson added a fourth test, how a government had come to power (Foreign Relations, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). The Central American treaties of 1907 and 1923 made that test binding among the five isthmian republics, and Washington followed it until 1936 (Foreign Relations, 1936, The American Republics, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). Within those tests, recognition was traded for specific undertakings. Mexico, 1920–1923, was asked about its oil and land law and about claims. In 1943–1944 Washington consulted the other republics and asked about Axis influence. After 1948 the questions were communism, elections and civil rights. The Bogota conference of 1948 then recast the whole matter as continuity of relations that implied no approval (Foreign Relations, 1948, Volume IX, Document 74, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d74).

**2. Where FRUS points for the rest.** *Where the printed documents came from:* more than four in five of those that carry a source note came from the State Department's Central Decimal File in Record Group 59 (National Archives at College Park), and mostly from a handful of files on one country's internal politics or government. The largest are Mexico, political affairs, 1910–1922; El Salvador, government, 1931–1948; Bolivia, political affairs and government, 1920–1946; Costa Rica, political affairs, 1911–1920; Haiti, Peru, Guatemala, Ecuador and Argentina follow. The app's bundled reference data names this file and glosses its numbers, but it gives no NARA identifier, entry number or access status for the file itself. After 1950 a few printed documents came from lot files, never more than three from any one: Bureau of Inter-American Affairs Subject and Country Files (RG 59, entry A1 5763, NAID 1074073, 1962–1975, partly restricted); Records Relating to El Salvador (RG 59, entry A1 3159, NAID 1750062, 1958–1975, partly restricted); Records Relating to Honduras (RG 59, entry A1 5732, NAID 2602882, 1957–1974, partly restricted); Records Relating to National Security Council Policy Papers (RG 59, entry A1 1586E, NAID 2839192, 1947–1979, partly restricted); and The Secretary's and the Under Secretary's Memorandums of Conversation (RG 59, entry A1 1566, NAID 602231, 1953–1964, unrestricted). Most of the remainder came from presidential library and intelligence files. *What the editors cited without printing:* fewer than one document in twelve has a citation the index can resolve. Those citations point to further decimal files (Cuba's internal political file of 1959–1962, Haiti's of the 1950s, Peru's of 1962), to the Johnson Library's National Security File (Latin America Country Files, NAID 567985, 1963–1969), to files cited as the Carter Library's "National Security Affairs", and to lot files. Among the lots are the Subject Files of the Assistant Secretary for Inter-American Affairs (RG 59, entry A1 1135, NAID 2108779, 1957–1959, unrestricted) and the Subject and Country Files of the Office of Central American and Panamanian Affairs (RG 59, entry A1 3157, NAID 2363840, 1955–1963, possibly restricted). Records Relating to Honduras is cited in both ways. Before 1910 the only shelves the bundled data reaches are the microfilmed despatches and instructions. For Ecuador in 1895 these are Despatches from U.S. Ministers to Ecuador, May 1894–December 1896 (NAID 189312130, in series 603720), and Diplomatic Instructions, Ecuador, 1848–1906 (NAID 149303924, in series 593313).

**3. What this request cannot get from FRUS.** *The Latin American side of the decision.* I searched the whole series for the region's own doctrines of recognition. The Mexican Estrada doctrine is named in 27 documents and the Venezuelan Betancourt doctrine in three. The Ecuadorian Tobar doctrine, the source of the Central American treaty rule, is named in one; the other 84 documents the index returns for the name Tobar do not name the doctrine. FRUS shows how Washington read these doctrines, not how the governments that framed them argued among themselves. The bundled indexes reach no foreign archive. *The Department's own general instruction.* A circular instruction of 8 June 1948 setting out the three conditions is cited (Foreign Relations, 1949, Volume II, Document 439, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d439) but not printed; its shelf would be the decimal file, which the bundled data does not identify. *The years after 1980 for Central America.* The published series this library holds has no Central America volume after 1980. *The years before 1910.* The citation channel the editors' footnotes feed has no entries before December 1910, and the bundled archival indexes barely reach before 1940. An empty archival trail before then is a limit of the tools, not evidence that no records exist. The controls that make these absences meaningful: "Department of State" appears in every one of the 74 volumes searched, and a nonsense string appears in none.

**Scope.** All 553 volumes of the series are in this library. The question was traced through 74 of them, and 1,227 documents in 73 volumes made up the working set: documents from chapters whose headings name recognition of a Latin American government (1895–1950) that use the word, documents from Latin American revolution chapters that speak of recognition and a coup, junta or new government in the same passage, and the same test applied to every Latin American volume from 1949 on. Front matter and the editors' own editorial notes were excluded, and no second edition of a volume was counted.

## Decisions this needs from you

- **Recognition, or continuity of relations?** After 1948 the Department deliberately stopped using the word "recognition" for Latin American governments. Counting only recognition language understates the period after 1948; counting every resumption or suspension of relations would sweep in breaks over other causes. Meanwhile I counted documents that use recognition language within a short distance of coup, junta or new-government language.
- **Which governments count as "by force"?** Including constitutional successions that Washington questioned (the 1934 Cuban provisional government, the Ecuadorian presidents of 1925–1928) widens the set; restricting to military seizures narrows the 1930s. Meanwhile I followed the editors' own chapter headings, which include both.
- **Does the Caribbean under occupation belong?** The Haitian and Dominican recognitions of 1911–1916 were made under United States intervention, and keeping them inflates the 1910s. Meanwhile they are in the counts, but no finding rests on them.

## Findings

### 1. Three standing tests, stated the same way for half a century

The Department's baseline barely changed between 1895 and 1949. In 1895 the minister in Ecuador was told to deal with "the de facto authority administering the public affairs of the State with the general acquiescence of its people and controlling the machinery of government to that end", which was "in accordance with the traditional policy of this Government" (Foreign Relations, 1895, Part I, Document 244, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1895p1/d244). In 1930 Stimson recognized three juntas because "these provisional governments are de facto in control of their respective countries, and that there is no active resistance to their rule. Each of the present governments has also made it clear that it is its intention to fulfill its respective international obligations and to hold, in due course, elections to regularize its status" (Foreign Relations, 1930, Volume I, Document 375, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1930v01/d375). In 1949 an ambassador still listed "three conditions viz. “de facto” control, ability and willingness meet obligations, and acquiescence great majority of people" (Foreign Relations, 1949, Volume II, Document 439, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1949v02/d439). The phrase "international obligations" turns up in every period, in roughly one working-set document in twelve (see the memo).

### 2. From 1913 to 1936, the manner of taking power was itself a test

Wilson's statement of 11 March 1913, sent to every mission in Latin America, held that "Cooperation is possible only when supported at every turn by the orderly processes of just government based upon law, not upon arbitrary or irregular force" (Foreign Relations, 1913, Document 7, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1913/d7). Applied to Tinoco's seizure of power in Costa Rica, it meant that Washington "will not give recognition or support to any Government which may be established unless it is clearly proven that it is elected by legal and constitutional means" (Foreign Relations, 1917, Document 378, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d378). The Central American treaties wrote that rule down; the 1907 text, quoted back to Washington by Tinoco's agent, bound the five republics not to recognize a government that came "into power in any of the five Republics as a consequence of a coup d’état, or of a revolution against the recognized Government" (Foreign Relations, 1917, Document 414, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1917/d414). The 1923 version was applied so literally that in 1932 the Department checked whether a candidate in El Salvador "is not related through consanguinity to one of the leaders of the revolution as specified in Clause 1 of Article II of the Treaty" (Foreign Relations, 1932, Volume V, Document 623, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1932v05/d623). In April 1936 it was dropped: "the United States should no longer be guided by Article II of the General Treaty of Peace and Amity of 1923 in extending or denying recognition to Governments in Central America" (Foreign Relations, 1936, Volume V, Document 112, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1936v05/d112). Documents naming the treaty appear only between 1913 and 1947 (see the memo).

### 3. Mexico: recognition as the price of specific undertakings

With Mexico after 1920 the conditions were concrete. In August 1920 the Under Secretary recorded the Mexican side's acknowledgment that "it was necessary to have a proper and final interpretation of Article 27 of the Constitution to the effect that this Article shall not be retroactive" (Foreign Relations, 1920, Volume III, Document 225, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1920v03/d225). Article 27 was the provision of Mexico's 1917 Constitution that vested subsoil oil in the nation. In June 1921 Secretary Hughes made recognition follow a treaty: "when it appears that there is a government in Mexico willing to bind itself to the discharge of primary international obligations, concurrently with that act its recognition will take place" (Foreign Relations, 1921, Volume II, Document 344, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1921v02/d344). Documents naming Article 27 occur only in these Mexican chapters of 1920–1923.

### 4. Wartime: consultation with the other republics, and an Axis test

In 1943–1944 recognition became a collective, political judgment. In June 1943 the ambassador in Buenos Aires insisted that "it was not my Government’s intention or desire to bargain with the new Government or to impose a break in relations with the Axis as a condition precedent to recognition" (Foreign Relations, 1943, Volume V, Document 335, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1943v05/d335). A year later the Department spelled out the procedure it had sponsored through the Montevideo committee: "consultation is automatically called for by the mere fact of a revolutionary change of government. The subject of the consultations is whether there is evidence in the possession of any government of Axis inspiration or tendencies" (Foreign Relations, 1944, Volume VII, Document 1023, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d1023). In Bolivia, "the pro-Nazi record of the MNR leaders made recognition by the other American republics impossible. There is now no MNR official in any position of prominence in Bolivia"; the editors' chapter heading records the recognition that followed (Foreign Relations, 1944, Volume VII, Document 432, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v07/d432). Mentions of the Axis occur only in 1933–1947, and most mentions of "the other American republics" fall in those same years (see the memo).

### 5. After Bogota: continuity without approval, and the conditions that stayed

Resolution XXXV of the Bogota conference declared "That the establishment or maintenance of diplomatic relations with a government does not imply any judgment upon the domestic policy of that government" (Foreign Relations, 1948, Volume IX, Document 74, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d74). Months later, facing Peru's military junta, the Department concluded that "In the absence of any compelling reason to the contrary, it is desirable for the United States to maintain diplomatic relations with all Latin American governments" (Foreign Relations, 1948, Volume IX, Document 89, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1948v09/d89). Conditions did not vanish; they changed. Recommending prompt recognition of Castro's provisional government, the Secretary of State noted that it "appears free from Communist taint and there are indications that it intends to pursue friendly relations with the United States" (Foreign Relations, 1958–1960, Volume VI, Document 217, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1958-60v06/d217). After the 1962 Peruvian coup, the Under Secretary reported that suspending relations had "led the junta to take a position with respect to new elections, with respect to current civil rights" (Foreign Relations, 1961–1963, Volume XII, Document 421, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d421). In 1973 the question was timing: Washington would "take the public position that we recognize governments, not heads of government, and that question of recognition has not arisen" (Foreign Relations, 1969–1976, Volume XXI, Document 348, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v21/d348). In the documents after 1948, communism comes up in about half and "human rights" in about one in ten, against a few in a hundred for communism and none at all for human rights earlier. Read that contrast with care: the later documents were chosen by a passage-level test rather than by the editors' chapter headings (see the memo).

## How this was done

I worked from a read-only copy of the FRUS Explorer search index covering all 553 published volumes, the TEI source files, and the reference data bundled with the app. I first read the editors' chapter headings for every volume and took those naming recognition of a Latin American government, adding by hand the chapters on the 1907 and 1923 Central American treaties, the 1936 decision on Article II, and the 1948 Bogota resolution. For volumes after 1950, whose editors no longer title chapters this way, and for revolution chapters whose headings do not name recognition, I admitted a document only when recognition language fell within 150 characters of coup, junta or new-government language. Counts of condition language were made by exact pattern matching over each document's full text, footnotes included, with a strict and a looser form of each phrase (the looser form allowing plurals and punctuation between words), rather than with the index's word-root search, which treats different words alike. A positive and a negative control were run over every volume searched. The word "recognition" appears in about one in three documents in the chosen chapters against about one in eight elsewhere in the same volumes, while the control phrase does not rise. So the term measures the question, not the corpus. Archival sources were tallied in two separate ways, never added together: the source each printed document came from, and the files its footnotes cite. Both were resolved against the bundled NARA series data. Every quotation was checked character for character against its document.
