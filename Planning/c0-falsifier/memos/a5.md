# Scoping memo — fishing rights and territorial waters in FRUS

**To:** the historian
**Re:** How the United States handled disputes over fishing rights and territorial waters with other governments
**Corpus:** local FRUS Explorer installation — 552 volumes, 316,839 documents, all 552 TEI files present on disk
**Date of pass:** 2026-08-31. All figures below were measured, not estimated; the scripts are listed in §8.

---

## 1. Bottom line

This question is unusually well served by FRUS, and for a specific structural reason: **the editors themselves treated it as a standing subject heading for roughly 120 years.** I found **132 named compilation/chapter headings across 89 volumes** whose titles are explicitly about fisheries, sealing, whaling, territorial waters, the three-mile limit, the continental shelf, or the law of the sea. Those headings enclose **1,829 documents in 81 volumes**. That is an editor-curated corpus on your exact question, and it is machine-extractable from `volume_structures` — you do not have to reconstruct it from keywords.

Around that spine sits a much larger, noisier penumbra: **6,905 documents in 523 of 552 volumes** matched a broad topical vocabulary. Only **18% of those (1,240) fall inside a named topical compilation**, and conversely the keyword sweep **misses 32% (589) of the editor-defined documents**. Neither route alone is adequate. Use both, and expect to reconcile them by hand.

The topic is also **continuous across the whole series** — it is one of the few questions where the 1870s and the 1980s are both dense — but the *kind* of evidence changes completely at about 1925 (§4).

## 2. What I could reach, and what I did with it

Three resources, all local:

- the SQLite search index (`frus-copy.db`, 3.4 GB) — `document_cache` (full body text of every document), `frus_documents` (FTS5, porter-stemmed), `document_sources` (parsed archival source notes incl. State Department decimal file numbers), `external_citations` (footnote pointers), `document_subject_refs` (a 491-term controlled vocabulary), `volume_structures` (each volume's compilation/chapter tree **with the document ids under each heading**);
- the TEI corpus (552 files, verified all present);
- the bundled reference JSON — `manifest.json`, `document-subject-index.json`, `decimal-class-labels.json` (the State Department's own 1910–49 classification schedule), `semantic-map-index.json`.

I ran four independent retrievals and cross-checked them against each other: (a) keyword/phrase over full text, (b) the editors' own section headings, (c) the controlled subject vocabulary, (d) the archival decimal file classes recorded in source notes. Agreement and disagreement between them is the substance of §5 and §6.

## 3. The editorial spine — the most useful finding

`volume_structures` stores each volume's chapter tree with its document ids. Matching heading titles against a topical pattern yields a chronological spine. This is not a keyword artefact: these are the titles the Office of the Historian's editors gave to compilations. A condensed walk (volume → heading, abridged):

| Period | Volume(s) | Compilation heading (abridged) |
|---|---|---|
| 1793–1874 | frus1873p2v3 | "II.—The fisheries" (Treaty of Washington / Geneva papers) |
| 1872 | frus1872p1 | Fisheries in waters adjacent to the British North American possessions |
| 1880 | frus1880 | Alleged outrage upon American fishermen at Fortune Bay, Newfoundland |
| 1885–1888 | frus1885, frus1886 | Agreement respecting the fisheries; American fishing vessels molested in Canadian waters |

| 1904–1910 | frus1904–frus1910 | Newfoundland fishery question (70 docs in 1906p1); modus vivendi on the Treaty coast; **North Atlantic Coast Fisheries Arbitration, The Hague, 1910** |
| 1911–1912 | frus1911, frus1912 | Fur seal convention of 1911; **Russia's extension of the three-mile limit to twelve miles for customs and fisheries control (22 docs)** |
| 1914–1918 | frus1914Supp–1918Supp | Limits of territorial waters — the Italian six-mile rule; belligerent warships "hovering"; Norway mining its territorial waters |
| 1918–1924 | frus1918–frus1924 | US–Canada Fisheries Conference; salmon convention; port privileges, lobster and halibut; halibut convention of 1923 |

The chapter titles are self-documenting and often carry explicit continuation notes ("Continued from *Foreign Relations*, 1928, vol. ii, pp. 7–30"), which means **you can follow a single dispute across volumes without searching at all**. That is the cheapest high-yield move available here.

Two of the 120 heading-matched sections are false positives you should drop: `frus1920v03`, where "territorial waters" belongs to a chapter on naval protection during the Mexican revolution (75 docs), and `frus1916Supp`, on belligerent submarines in neutral waters (30 docs). Both are cases of the neutrality confound described in §5.

A clear U. The topic's share of the record collapses through the world wars — not because the disputes stopped but because the corpus's denominator explodes with war diplomacy — and recovers to nineteenth-century levels by the 1970s. If you want a "relative salience" argument, this table is your evidence, and the denominator column is the part reviewers will check.

**Counterparties.** Co-mention rates within the 3,591 fishing-word documents (a document may name several, so these do not sum to 100%): Great Britain/UK 40.9%, Russia/USSR 37.3%, Japan 29.1%, Canada 25.4%, France 19.5%, China 18.3%, Mexico 9.6%, Korea 9.0%, Cuba 8.9%, Spain 8.4%, Norway 8.0%, Brazil 6.8%, Peru 6.5%, Newfoundland 5.8%, Chile 5.8%, Ecuador 5.6%, Denmark 5.3%, Iran/Persia 4.6%, Iceland 4.4%, Argentina 4.3%. Treat as orientation only — a long NSC policy paper mentions a dozen countries.

**Genre shift, c. 1925.** This matters more than any count. Before roughly 1925 the printed documents *are* the dispute: consular reports on seizures, protest notes, instructions to legations. `frus1870/d270`, Secretary Fish to a consul-general, opens "Several seizures of American fishing vessels are reported by the consular officers…"; `frus1886/d182` narrates the *Marion Grimes* running into Shelburne, Nova Scotia under stress of weather. After 1945 the printed documents are almost entirely *internal deliberation*: NSC memoranda, NSDMs, Legal Adviser memoranda, interagency task-force reports. `frus1981-88v41/d114` is a Defense memorandum to Weinberger; `frus1969-76ve03/d1` is a report of the Interagency Law of the Sea Task Force. **The same question therefore yields correspondence with foreign governments in the first half and US internal argument in the second.** Any claim about "how the United States handled" disputes has to be indexed to which of those two evidence bases it rests on.

**Archival handles.** The State Department's own classification schedule (bundled as `decimal-class-labels.json`) gives three exact file numbers:

- `8NN.628` = **Fisheries** — 101 documents in source notes (Japan 71, Mexico 9, Taiwan 5, Ecuador 5, Costa Rica 5, Peru 1);
- `8NN.0145` = **Territorial waters. Marginal sea** — 108 documents (Asia 34, United States 33, USSR 21, Mexico 10, Turkey 3, Iran 3, Honduras 2, Ecuador 1);
- `8NN.627` = **Fur seals** — **zero**, because the fur-seal disputes predate the 1910 decimal file entirely;
- plus `711.428*` (US–Canada) — 157 documents, the single largest class in my set;
- and `399.731`, the Law of the Sea conference file — 120 documents in my set.

Of the 6,905 topical documents, 5,019 carry a parsed source note. Repositories: Department of State 3,471; National Archives 413; Nixon Presidential Materials 287; Carter 137; Reagan 97; Johnson 89; Ford 80; CIA 68; Eisenhower 47; Kennedy 40; Library of Congress 36. Named lot files worth writing down: **`68 D 47` "Memoranda and Articles—Territorial Waters"** (Legal Adviser), **`85 D 357` "Law of the Sea—Third UN Conference, 1968–1983"**, **`77 D 302` L/OES**, and at the Reagan Library the **Michael A. Guhin files** on the Law of the Sea (17 footnote citations).

## 5. Traps — please read this section before you search

Everything here is measured, and each of these would have silently distorted a count.

**(a) Hamilton Fish.** The FTS index is porter-stemmed, so `fishing` stems to `fish`, and Hamilton Fish was Secretary of State 1869–1877. Capital-F "Fish" appears as a standalone word in **4,408 documents**. A full-text search for `fishing` returns **7,283** documents; the literal string "fishing" appears in **2,235**. I hit this live: `NEAR(iceland fishing, 20)` returned 97 documents of which **25 (26%) were spurious**, the top hits being "Mr. Andrews to Mr. Fish" despatches from Stockholm about Nordenskiöld's Kara Sea voyage. Any stemmed search touching `fish` is contaminated across the entire 1869–77 run, which is exactly the decade of peak fisheries diplomacy. **Use `fisheries`/`fishery`/`fisherman` (which stem safely) or match literal strings.**

**(b) Substring matching on "tuna".** `LIKE '%tuna%'` returns **15,322** documents; word-bounded, **143**. The difference is "fortunate", "unfortunately", "opportunity". Only 66 documents contain "tuna boat", "tuna clipper", "tuna fish" or "tuna vessel" — and those, not the 15,322, are the Ecuador/Peru seizure literature.

**(c) "sealing" → "seal".** Stemmed search returns **5,162**; the literal string "sealing" appears in **574**. The department's own seal, sealed envelopes and sealed orders account for the rest.

**(d) "high seas" is the largest single noise source.** 996 documents entered my set on "high seas" alone. Reading a random sample, these are WWI neutrality and submarine warfare (`frus1915Supp/d203` on flag sovereignty on the high seas), the 1885 Chinese blockade, the Mayaguez crisis ("Perhaps we can seize a Cambodian ship on the high seas"), and Antarctica. Related to law of the sea, yes; about fishing or territorial-water disputes, no. **Exclude "high seas" from your primary query and add it back deliberately.**

**(e) Precision of the loose union is roughly 50%.** In a random sample of 30 keyword hits outside the editorial spine, about half were genuinely on the question (Korea's Rhee Line seizures of Japanese fishing vessels, Chinese fishing boats rammed by US shipping, Bahamian archipelagic claims, Western Sahara fishing and sovereignty) and about half were passing mentions or the neutrality/navigation confound.

**(f) The controlled subject vocabulary will under-recall you badly.** The 491-term vocabulary has a whole "Law of the Sea" facet — *Territorial waters* (1,622 docs), *Territorial sea limit* (347), *Law of the sea* (588), *Continental shelf* (268), *Innocent passage* (172), *EEZ* (25) — but **no "Fisheries" subject at all**; only *Fishing limit* (10 documents) and *Fishing agreement* (22). Those seven LOS subjects together tag 2,386 documents, **2,357 of which (98.8%) my text retrieval already found**; the 29 unique additions are seabed arms-control papers (DEF 18 files), i.e. not your topic. **The subject index is a useful confirmation and a poor primary handle.**

**(g) Duplicate volumes.** Three second-edition twins are in the manifest (`frus1951-54IranEd2`, `frus1969-76ve15p2Ed2`, `frus1977-80v09Ed2`), holding 1,181 documents of which 28 are topical. Dedupe or you will double-count.

**(h) Front matter.** 99 of my 6,905 hits are prefaces, tables of contents and abbreviation lists (the volume preface for `frus1952-54v01p2` matched "law of the sea"). `document_cache.is_front_matter` filters them.

## 6. What I would actually search, and why

**Step 1 — take the editorial spine first.** Extract every compilation/chapter from `volume_structures` whose title matches `fisher|fishing|territorial water|territorial sea|law of the sea|sealing|fur seal|three-mile|continental shelf|whaling|halibut|salmon|marginal sea`, then collect the `documentIds` of that node **and all its descendants** (many headings sit above the level that carries the ids — restricting to nodes that carry ids directly gives only 302 documents instead of 1,829, a 6× under-count I made on my first pass). Drop `frus1920v03` and `frus1916Supp` by hand. Result: 1,829 documents, 81 volumes, already grouped by dispute.

**Step 2 — keyword sweep for what the editors filed elsewhere.** Search full text (not the stemmed index) for:
- `fisheries`, `fishery`, `fishermen`, `fishing vessel`, `fishing rights`, `right to fish`
- `territorial waters`, `territorial sea`, `marginal sea`, `maritime belt`, `three-mile limit`, `marine league`, `continental shelf`, `contiguous zone`, `law of the sea`, `exclusive economic zone`
- period-specific: `pelagic sealing`, `fur seal`, `modus vivendi` + `fisheries`, `inshore`, `headland`, `12 nautical miles`, `200-mile`, `Rhee Line`, `Santiago`
Then intersect the two clusters (Tier A) rather than unioning them. That is 824 documents, of which 569 also carry seizure/protest/claim language.

**Step 3 — walk the runs, do not trust the keywords.** This is the single most important procedural point, and I can quantify it. Against the archivally-classified documents — the ones whose source notes carry `8NN.628` (Fisheries) or `8NN.0145` (Territorial waters) — my keyword retrieval found **92 of 101 (91%)** and **81 of 108 (75%)** respectively. The **36 misses are all genuinely on topic**, and they fail for one reason: they are short covering notes and continuation despatches inside a run whose *earlier* documents carry the vocabulary. Examples, verbatim from the misses:

- `frus1912/d1735` (`861.0145`), Russian Embassy memorandum: "a law recently promulgated in Russia and subjecting to the Russian customs regulations the waters of the sea within 12 nautical miles from the Russian shores" — no "territorial waters", no "three-mile limit".
- `frus1935v01/d802` (`891.0145`), Minister in Persia transmitting an Act before the Majlis.
- `frus1937v04/d893` (`894.628 Vessels/181`), Secretary of State to the Japanese Ambassador — a two-line courtesy note inside the Alaska fisheries file.
- `frus1923v02/d779` (`867.0145`), Lausanne, on the Straits.

**So: whenever a document hits, read its neighbours by document id and read its decimal file number.** The file number is the run's identity; the keyword is not.

**Step 4 — pivot on the archive.** Query `document_sources.decimal_class` for `%.628`, `%.0145`, `711.428%`, `399.731%` and on `external_citations` for the lot files named in §4. This finds documents by where they were *filed* rather than by what they say, and it is the only route that recovered the 36 misses above.

## 7. Limits, and where I would not over-claim

- **FRUS is a published selection.** Only 101 documents in the whole corpus carry the `.628` Fisheries file number. That is a fact about editorial choice, not about the archive; the fisheries files at NARA are vastly larger. Do not present FRUS counts as measures of how much fisheries diplomacy occurred.
- **Coverage ends in 1988.** UNCLOS III's signature (1982) and the Reagan administration's refusal of Part XI are in (`frus1981-88v41`, 89 documents, 1981-02-12 → 1988-12-09); the 1994 Part XI Agreement and US ratification debates are outside the corpus entirely.
- **Coverage effectively begins in 1861.** Only two topical compilations reach earlier, both retrospectively: `frus1902app1` (whaling and sealing claims against Russia, enclosures back to 1820) and `frus1873p2v3` ("II.—The fisheries", back to 1793). The Convention of 1818 — the legal basis of the entire North Atlantic dispute — appears only as citation: "convention of 1818" in 56 documents, "treaty of 1818" in 87. **There is no documentary run for pre-1861 fisheries diplomacy here.** Same caution for Proclamation 2667 (the Truman continental shelf proclamation): 44 documents mention the number; the deliberation behind it is the 35-document compilation in `frus1945v02`, which begins with Ickes to Roosevelt, 5 June 1943.
- **Some episodes are thin.** "Cod war" appears in 11 documents; the Gulf of Maine/Georges Bank boundary in 5 and 1 respectively; the Bartlett Act in 2; Magnuson/Fishery Conservation and Management in 15. These are real events poorly represented in the printed series — likely because they fall in the corpus's post-1976 thinning, not because the diplomacy was slight. Do not read low counts as low importance near the end of the series.
- **My "both" and precision figures rest on one 30-document read.** A 50% precision estimate from n=30 is indicative, not firm. If precision matters to your argument, sample 150 and code them properly.
- The semantic map's 179 clusters contain exactly one topical cluster (id 36, terms `salmon, constantinople, roseau, fishery`), which is small and contaminated. I would not use it as a retrieval route here.
