# Scoping memo: fishing rights and territorial waters in the FRUS corpus

**To:** the historian
**Re:** "How did the United States handle disputes over fishing rights and territorial waters with other governments?"
**Corpus:** local FRUS Explorer index — 316,839 documents across 552 volumes, coverage 1861–1988 (one 1989–92 volume)
**Date of pass:** 2026-08-31. Scoping only: I measured the shape of the evidence, I did not read the documents.

---

## 1. Short answer

The corpus is unusually good for this question, and better than the raw counts suggest, because
**the FRUS editors themselves treated fisheries and territorial waters as a named, recurring
compilation heading for more than a century.** I found **123 editor-declared topical sections
across 91 volumes**, holding **2,027 documents** — section titles like "Newfoundland fishery
question," "Fisheries and related questions," "Formulation of United States policy on the resources
of the Continental Shelf and on coastal fisheries," "United States Policy Regarding the Law of the
Sea," "Oceans Policy." That editorial layer, not a keyword search, is the spine of the topic.

Around it, a vocabulary search returns **4,999 documents in 501 of 552 volumes** — 1.6% of the
corpus. It is not concentrated: the top twenty volumes hold only 1,269 of those 4,999. This is a
long-running, low-intensity thread that flares into three or four discrete crises.

The material divides into two strands that are mostly *separate* and only sometimes the same
dispute. Fishing-rights documents (2,854) and territorial-waters/jurisdiction documents (2,898)
overlap in only **753 documents**. Nineteenth-century fisheries disputes are about *access under a
treaty*; twentieth-century territorial-waters documents are heavily about *neutrality, submarines,
and naval passage*. They fuse into one question only after 1945, when coastal states start using
jurisdictional claims to exclude foreign fishermen.

## 2. The one thing that will wreck a naive search — read this before you search anything

**Do not search for `fishing` or `fish` in this index.** The FTS index uses a Porter stemmer.
`fishing`, `fished`, `fishes` and `fish` all collapse to the token `fish` — and so does the surname
of **Hamilton Fish, Secretary of State 1869–1877**, who signs or receives a large fraction of every
despatch printed in the FRUS volumes for those years.

Measured, from the FTS vocabulary table and per-year counts:

| token | documents |
|---|---|
| `fish` (i.e. what a search for "fishing" returns) | 7,283 |
| `fisheri` (fishery / fisheries) | 1,969 |

| volume year | docs matching `fishing` | docs matching `fisheries` |
|---|---|---|
| 1872 | 735 | 22 |
| 1873 | 499 | 21 |
| **1874** | **760** | **8** |
| 1875 | 650 | 3 |
| 1876 | 322 | 2 |

In FRUS 1874, 760 of the volume's 817 documents (93%) "match fishing." Eight of them are about
fisheries. A representative snippet from that set: `…Hamilton [Fish], Secretary of State.` The
1870s volumes are the *most* fisheries-relevant years in the nineteenth century for real reasons —
the Treaty of Washington and the Halifax Commission — which makes this trap especially dangerous:
the false positives sit exactly where the true positives should be, so the error looks like a
finding.

Use `fisheries`, `fishery`, `fishermen`, and disambiguated phrases (`"fishing vessel"`,
`"fishing rights"`, `"fishing grounds"`). My queries are recorded verbatim in `queries.txt` beside
this memo.

Two smaller versions of the same problem:
- `"hot pursuit"` returns 192 documents, but they cluster in the Korea and Vietnam volumes
  (frus1964-68v01, frus1951v07p1, frus1950v07) — that is *military* hot pursuit across a land
  border, not the maritime doctrine.
- `seal` (5,162 documents) is mostly "hand and seal," "sealed." Use `"fur seal"` (306) and
  `"pelagic sealing"`.
- A section-title search on `ocean` pulls in "interoceanic canal," "Indian Ocean," and "Pacific
  Ocean" compilations. I excluded those by hand; the count of 123 sections is after exclusion.

## 3. What I actually measured

**Retrieval sets.** Two phrase-and-term strands, then their union and intersection:

| set | documents |
|---|---|
| fisheries vocabulary (QF) | 2,854 |
| waters / jurisdiction vocabulary (QT) | 2,898 |
| union | 4,999 (501 volumes) |
| **intersection (both strands in one document)** | **753** |

**Editor-declared compilations.** I parsed the `volume_structures` table — each volume's section
tree, which is the printed volume's own topical organisation — and matched section titles against a
topical pattern, then subtracted false friends. Result: **123 sections, 91 volumes, 2,027
documents**. The full list is in `sections_clean.tsv`. This is the most trustworthy instrument here,
because a section title is the FRUS editors asserting that the compilation *is about* the topic.

**Subject tags.** The bundled taxonomy has a "Law of the Sea" family: Territorial waters (1,622
docs), Territorial sea limit (347), Law of the sea (588), Continental shelf (268), Innocent passage
(172), Exclusive economic zone (25), Fishing agreement (22), Fishing limit (10). I verified the tag
IDs against the bundled vocabulary (they match exactly). **These tags add almost nothing over a
phrase search**: of 2,467 documents carrying any tag in that family, 2,348 (95.2%) are already in my
phrase union; only 119 are tag-only. Conversely the tags miss ~19% of the phrase hits. They appear
to be largely term-derived. Treat them as a convenience, not as independent editorial judgment.
Note also that there is no "Fisheries" subject at all — only "Fishing limit" (10) and "Fishing
agreement" (22). The taxonomy is a weak instrument for the fisheries half of your question.

**Chronology.** Using document-level dates (`document_dates`), normalised against the corpus's own
documents-per-decade, the union set gives a clear U:

| decade | topic docs | corpus docs | per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1860s | 96 | 11,253 | 8.5 |
| 1870s | 211 | 5,808 | 36.3 |
| **1880s** | 275 | 6,482 | **42.4** |
| 1890s | 233 | 9,721 | 24.0 |
| 1900s | 187 | 9,938 | 18.8 |
| 1910s | 374 | 30,391 | 12.3 |
| 1920s | 347 | 19,740 | 17.6 |
| 1930s | 390 | 39,202 | 9.9 |
| 1940s | 601 | 75,680 | 7.9 |
| 1950s | 602 | 46,521 | 12.9 |
| 1960s | 578 | 29,859 | 19.4 |
| 1970s | 766 | 23,275 | 32.9 |
| **1980s** | 237 | 6,123 | **38.7** |

Absolute volume peaks in the 1970s; *density* peaks in the 1880s and again in the 1970s–80s, with a
deep trough in the 1930s–40s when the corpus is swamped by war diplomacy. If you are writing about
the salience of ocean questions in American diplomacy, that curve is a finding in itself — but note
the denominator is doing a lot of work, and the 1940s trough is an artefact of the war volumes as
much as a statement about oceans policy.

## 4. What the corpus holds — the six episodes worth building a chapter on

I list these with the measured document counts of their editor-declared compilations, so you can
judge weight before opening anything.

**(a) The North Atlantic / Canadian inshore fisheries, 1870s–1912.** The long treaty-access
argument with Great Britain and Newfoundland.
- `frus1873p2v3` "II.—The fisheries" and `frus1872p1` "Fisheries in waters adjacent to the British
  North American possessions" — the Treaty of Washington / Halifax Commission layer.
- `frus1885` "Papers Relating to the Agreement Between the United States and Great Britain
  Respecting the Fisheries" (10 docs); `frus1886` "Selections from Correspondence of the Department
  of State with parties interested in American Fishing Vessels…" (38 docs) — the seizures crisis
  after abrogation; the correspondence is Bayard–Phelps.
- `frus1880` "Correspondence with the Government of Great Britain in regard to the Alleged Outrage
  upon American Fishermen…" (16 docs) — the Fortune Bay affair.
- `frus1905` and `frus1906p1` "Newfoundland fishery question" (21 and **70** docs), `frus1906p2`
  "Seizure of American fishing vessels" (11), `frus1907p1` (3), `frus1908`, `frus1909` "Special
  agreement… relating to North Atlantic coast fisheries."
- **The Hague arbitration itself is thin and you should know that now.** The 1910 award is
  represented by `frus1910` "The North Atlantic Coast Fisheries Arbitration" — **3 documents**, of
  which the substantial one is `frus1910/d625`, Chandler P. Anderson's agent's report to the
  Secretary of State, 14 November 1910, ~44,000 characters. It opens: *"The undersigned, agent of
  the United States in the North Atlantic coast fisheries arbitration recently held at The Hague,
  has the honor to submit his report…"* The pleadings and award were printed elsewhere; FRUS gives
  you the summary, not the case.

**(b) Bering Sea, fur seals, and the *mare clausum* claim, 1886–1911.** This is your best
nineteenth-century *territorial waters* material, and it is large: 667 documents match the
Bering/fur-seal vocabulary.
- `frus1888p2` "Supplement C: Papers relating to the seizure of British sealing-vessels in Behring
  Sea" (68), `frus1891` (96 docs match), `frus1894app1` "II. Enforcement of regulations respecting
  fur seals" (**144**), `frus1895p1` "Protection of fur seals in Bering Sea" (76), `frus1896`,
  `frus1897` (70), `frus1911` on the four-power convention.
- The doctrinal core is in `frus1890`: `d266`, `d273`, `d276` argue the *mare clausum* question
  explicitly — *"That Behring Sea is a mare clausum"* against *"No sea is now mare clausum."* That
  is the United States arguing, in 1890, precisely the expansive coastal jurisdiction it would spend
  1945–1980 resisting. If you want an irony to organise a chapter around, it is here.
- Related and separable: `frus1902app1`, an entire appendix volume, *Whaling and Sealing Claims
  Against Russia* — seizures of the *Cape Horn Pigeon*, *James Hamilton Lewis*, *C. H. White*, *Kate
  and Anna* in the Sea of Okhotsk (45 docs in the Cape Horn Pigeon case alone). Also `frus1900`,
  arbitration of those claims (39 + 12).
- Note `frus1912` "Extension by Russia of the three-mile limit of territorial waters to twelve miles
  for customs purposes" (22 docs) — an early, clean instance of the US objecting to a twelve-mile
  claim.

**(c) The interwar Pacific: Japan, Alaska salmon, halibut, and the treaty-commission model,
1923–1941.** The United States handling disputes by *building bilateral conservation commissions*
rather than by litigation.
- `frus1923v01` "Signing of a convention… for the preservation of the halibut fishery" (16),
  `frus1924v01` ratification (11), `frus1928v02`/`frus1929v02` the Fraser River sockeye salmon
  conventions and the replacement halibut convention.
- `frus1935v03`, `frus1936v04` "Disinclination of Japan to negotiate a convention… regulating
  fisheries off the coast of Alaska" (12, 10); `frus1937v04` and `frus1939v02` "Representations to
  Japan in regard to regulation of fisheries off the coast of Alaska" (**40** and **37**). A first
  document in that run (`frus1937v04`) is the Secretary of State to Ambassador Grew, 5 June 1937, on *"the participation of
  Japanese in the salmon fisheries in certain waters of Bering Sea and Bristol Bay,"* filed under
  decimal class `711.008 North Pacific/92`.
- `frus1926v02`, `frus1928v03`, `frus1940v04`, `frus1941v05` — the slow Japanese abrogation of the
  1911 fur-seals convention.
- Also a distinct whaling thread: `frus1937v01`, `frus1938v01`, `frus1943v01`, `frus1944v02`,
  `frus1945v02`, `frus1946v01` (International Whaling Conferences), and, much later,
  `frus1981-88v41` "International Regulation of Whaling Practices" (48).

**(d) 1945: the Truman proclamations — the hinge.** `frus1945v02`, "Formulation of United States
policy on the resources of the Continental Shelf and on coastal fisheries" — **35 documents**, and
they are policy-formulation documents, not diplomatic exchanges: Ickes to Roosevelt (5 June 1943),
Roosevelt to the Secretary of State (9 June 1943), Hull to Roosevelt (10 June 1943), a run of
Ickes–Stettinius letters through 1944, memoranda by the Office of Economic Affairs. Source notes
give decimal file `811.0145` (33 documents in the corpus carry it, all in this volume). This is the
single tightest, best-documented decision file in the corpus for your question, and it is an
*interagency* file: Interior versus State, resources versus legal exposure.
- The consequences follow immediately: `frus1946v11` "Continental Shelf policy of Mexico and its
  effect on United States–Mexican fisheries relations," `frus1947v08` (Mexico; Costa Rica),
  `frus1948v09` "Policies of the United States and certain other American Republics with respect to
  the continental shelf and…", `frus1950v01` and `frus1951v01` "United States policy regarding the
  delimitation of the territorial sea," `frus1950v02` on El Salvador's territorial sea.
- Precursors exist: `frus1935v01` (Iran's attempted extension of exclusive jurisdiction),
  `frus1935v04`/`frus1936v05` (Ecuadoran fishing regulations "purporting to ex[tend]…"),
  `frus1936v01` (Britain refusing US control beyond territorial waters — the anti-smuggling
  context), `frus1936v05` (a Mexican decree). The pattern of Latin American claims predates Truman;
  Truman is what makes them defensible.

**(e) The Law of the Sea conferences, 1952–1988.** The largest and most institutionally coherent
body of material, and it sits in a small number of volumes.
- `frus1952-54v01p2` "United States policy regarding the law of the sea" (28), `frus1955-57v11`
  (29), **`frus1958-60v02` "LAW OF THE SEA" (108)** — UNCLOS I and II, with the delegation traffic
  from Geneva. Source notes here are decimal class `399.731` (124 documents corpus-wide, in 3
  volumes — effectively a dedicated file). `frus1961-63v25` "Law of the Sea" (20).
- **`frus1969-76ve01` "Oceans Policy" (113)** and `frus1969-76ve03` "Law of the Sea" (54) — UNCLOS
  III preparation and negotiation, plus the seabed question; `frus1981-88v41` "Law of the Sea" (89)
  — the Reagan refusal to sign Part XI. Deep-seabed mining vocabulary returns 153 documents,
  concentrated in exactly these three (59 / 44 / 27).
- Post-1963 documents in this line carry subject-numeric file `POL 33` (279 documents, 28 volumes)
  and `POL 33-8` (27). Pre-1963 ones carry `399.731` and `811.0145`. Those four strings are your
  archival handles at NARA RG 59; source notes also point to lot files, e.g. *"RG 59, L/OA Files:
  Lot 72 D 505, Box 2, Law of the Sea NATO"* and *"D/LOS Files: Lot 75 D 243."*
- Useful editorial notes summarise the conferences narratively — e.g. `frus1958-60v02/d334` and
  `/d372`, the latter listing the four 1958 conventions adopted. 57 editorial notes fall in the
  topic set; they are the fastest way to orient.

**(f) The 200-mile claims and the seizure of American vessels, 1952–1980.** The operational
counterpart to (e), and the place where "fishing rights" and "territorial waters" finally become one
dispute.
- 103 documents match the Santiago Declaration / 200-mile-claim vocabulary, concentrated in
  `frus1969-76ve01` (25), `frus1969-76ve10` *Documents on American Republics* (16),
  `frus1969-76ve03` (8), `frus1958-60v05`, `frus1969-76ve11p2`, `frus1955-57v11`.
- Tuna-boat seizures: 37 documents, `frus1969-76ve10` (12) and `frus1969-76ve01` (10) leading; also
  `frus1964-68v31`, `frus1977-80v24`, `frus1977-80v27`. `frus1977-80v23` (Mexico, Cuba, Caribbean)
  carries 30 union hits.
- Sample texture from `frus1969-76ve10`: *"Several nations were firmly pursuing a policy designed to
  establish their sovereignty over an expanded territorial sea, in several cases up to a 200-mile
  limit. There was growing support in Latin America for…"* (d58); and a document linking arms sales
  to *"Argentine claims to the 200 mile maritime limit"* (d70) — i.e. the fisheries dispute as an
  instrument in a broader bilateral relationship, which is probably the most interesting single
  thing I saw.

**Smaller threads that are real but thin.** Korea–Japan: the Rhee/"Peace Line" returns 71 documents,
led by `frus1964-68v29p1` (which also carries 92 `POL 33` documents and 71 union hits — the
normalisation talks) plus `frus1952-54v14p2`, `frus1952-54v15p2`. Iceland: 67 documents on fisheries
limits, led by `frus1981-88v41` (12) and `frus1958-60v02` (12); "cod war" as a phrase returns only
15. Great Lakes / boundary waters and the Missisquoi Bay commissions: `frus1927v01`, `frus1928v02`,
`frus1929v02`. Gulf of Maine / Georges Bank: **6 documents corpus-wide** — the Canadian maritime
boundary case is essentially absent, presumably because it was decided at the ICJ in 1984 and the
1981–88 volumes are selective. Gulf of Sidra: 12, in `frus1981-88v24`. Straits/transit passage: 37.

## 5. What I would search for, in order

1. **Start from the section tree, not from keywords.** `sections_clean.tsv` beside this memo is the
   list of 123 compilations with document counts. Reading the 15 largest gets you ~1,000 documents
   that the editors themselves declared on-topic.
2. **Then the intersection set** (`hits_both.tsv`, 753 documents) — documents that use both
   vocabularies, which is where the two strands are actually the same dispute. Its top volumes are
   `frus1958-60v02` (89), `frus1969-76ve01` (82), `frus1969-76ve03` (35), `frus1969-76ve10` (27),
   `frus1945v02` (26), `frus1955-57v11` (25), `frus1981-88v41` (21), `frus1886` (19).
3. **Then archival file strings**, if you want to go past FRUS to the records: `811.0145`,
   `399.731`, `711.008 North Pacific`, `POL 33`, `POL 33-8`, and decimal subject suffixes `.628`
   (Fisheries — 120 documents, 20 volumes) and `.627` (Fur seals — 32). Those suffixes compose with
   a country number under the 1910–49 schedule, so `812.628` is Mexico/Fisheries.
4. **Then the named-episode probes** in §4, each of which is a one-line phrase query.

I would *not* lead with the subject taxonomy (§3), and I would not use free-text `fishing` at all.

## 6. Caveats and things I did not check

- **This is a scoping pass.** I read section titles, headers, source notes, and a few hundred
  characters of a handful of documents. Every count below the level of "this compilation exists and
  has N documents" is a vocabulary count, not a reading. Vocabulary counts overcount (a passing
  mention of "territorial waters" in a neutrality telegram) and undercount (a fisheries seizure
  described without either phrase).
- **The 1910s spike is largely not your topic.** The territorial-waters strand has 247 documents
  dated in the 1910s, and the editor-declared compilations behind them are wartime ones —
  "Actions of belligerent warships outside and within territorial waters–Hovering" (20 + 15 docs,
  frus1915Supp / frus1914Supp), "The Treatment of Belligerent Submarines in Neutral Waters: the
  Mining by Norway of Its Territorial Waters" (18), "Treatment of belligerent war and merchant
  submarines in neutral ports and waters" (30 + 4). That is neutrality law, not fisheries. I did
  not count precisely how much of the 247 it accounts for; treat the direction as measured and the
  proportion as an impression. Whether neutrality belongs in your question is a judgement call,
  not a retrieval problem — but be deliberate about it. (One genuine
  crossover: `frus1918Supp01v01`, the British reservation on "freedom of the seas" in the armistice
  negotiations — Point 2 of the Fourteen Points.)
- **Three second-edition volumes duplicate content** already in the corpus: `frus1951-54IranEd2`,
  `frus1969-76ve15p2Ed2`, `frus1977-80v09Ed2`. They contribute 27 documents to my union set and
  should be de-duplicated against their first editions before any count is published.
- **Eleven of the 123 sections report zero documents.** These look like table-of-contents entries
  whose documents are attached elsewhere in the tree (e.g. `frus1919v02` "Fisheries and related
  questions (Canada)", `frus1949v04` "Multilateral convention on northwest Atlantic fisheries").
  Do not read a zero as an absence; check the volume.
- **Coverage gaps.** No volume carries a 1869 subseries label. The corpus effectively stops at 1988
  (a single 1989–92 volume, on START, with one union hit), so the 1982 signature and the 1994
  Part XI agreement are past the edge. And FRUS is a *selection*: the absence of Georges Bank
  material is a statement about the editors, not about the diplomacy.
- **The country co-occurrence counts I ran are base-rate contaminated** and I would not print them.
  For the record, within the 2,854-document fisheries strand: Great Britain 1,154, Russia/Soviet
  940, Canada 890, Japan 859, France 553, China 509 — but "France" and "China" are almost certainly
  riding on volume base rates rather than fisheries disputes. The section-title evidence in §4 is
  the honest answer to "with whom."
- **6,556 documents in this index carry a machine-generated summary** and 12 carry a human note;
  451 of the summarised ones fall in my union set. Those summaries are model output, not evidence —
  do not cite them, and be aware that if you search the `user_content` table you are searching
  generated text.
- **I did not examine** the cross-reference graph, the external-citation index, the semantic-vector
  neighbourhoods, or the person tables. A person-centred pass (Bayard, Blaine, Elihu Root, Chandler
  Anderson, Arthur Dean, John Stevenson, Elliot Richardson) would probably be the highest-value next
  step and I did not attempt it.
