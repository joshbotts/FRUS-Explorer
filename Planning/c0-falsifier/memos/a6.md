# Scoping memo — The Department of State and international narcotics control before 1970

**To:** the historian
**Re:** what the FRUS Explorer corpus (552 volumes, 316,839 documents) holds on this question
**Date:** 2026-08-31
**Status:** scoping pass, ~40 queries. Everything below is measured; every number has a query behind it and I say which.

---

## 1. Bottom line

The corpus holds a real, coherent, and *editorially pre-assembled* body of material on this
question — but it is front-loaded and lumpy. **Roughly 1,100 documents dated before 1970 touch
narcotics**, out of 285,060 pre-1970 dated documents (**0.39%**). More usefully, FRUS's own editors
built **44 named topical compilations** on drug control across the series, and 43 of them are
pre-1970. Those compilations, not a keyword sweep, are the spine of the answer.

The shape over time is the finding. State's engagement as FRUS records it comes in four bursts
with two near-total silences between them:

| Period | Character | Volume of material |
|---|---|---|
| 1880–1905 | Treaty-based prohibition on *American* participation in the China opium trade; consular enforcement | thin, scattered |
| 1906–1915 | State *convenes* the regime: Shanghai Commission 1909, the three Hague opium conferences | the single densest block in the corpus |
| 1923–1940 | League of Nations Opium Advisory Committee; Geneva 1925, Limitation Convention 1931, Bangkok 1931, Illicit Traffic 1936; China/Manchuria/Japan | steady, ~15–30 docs/year |
| 1944–1947 | Wartime/postwar reconstruction of the regime; transfer of League functions to the UN; Mexico | a short revival |
| **1948–1960** | **near silence** — narcotics survives only as a clause inside other files | 3–15 docs/year |
| 1961–1963 | one dedicated compilation: the Single Convention and the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs | 20 documents |
| **1964–1968** | **near silence again** | 1–3 docs/year |
| 1969– | the Nixon escalation; a whole e-volume | 31 in 1969, 80 in 1971 |

The 1954–60 and 1964–68 troughs are not artifacts of my search. Two independent axes (below) agree
on them, and the archival axis explains them: **State had no narcotics office before 1971.** The
Secretary's Coordinator for International Narcotics Matters (S/NM, Sheldon Vance) is a 1971
creation and its lot file (Lot 77 D 120) appears in the corpus only in 1969–76 volumes.

## 2. What I searched, and why two axes rather than one

I did not want the answer to be an artifact of my vocabulary, so I measured the same question two
independent ways and compared them.

**Axis A — lexical.** FTS5 over `frus_documents.body_text` (porter-stemmed):

```
narcotic* OR opium OR cocaine OR morphine OR heroin OR marihuana OR marijuana
OR cannabis OR "coca leaf" OR "coca leaves"
```

**Axis B — editorial subject tags.** The corpus ships an assigned subject taxonomy
(`document_subject_refs` + `document-subject-index.json`) with a **"Narcotics" super-subject**
holding eight terms. I read their vocabulary indices out of the bundled JSON and confirmed each
one's corpus document-frequency against the database exactly:

| idx | subject | corpus docs |
|---|---|---|
| 364 | Opium | 1,016 |
| 107 | Narcotics | 631 |
| 481 | Heroin | 175 |
| 395 | Narcotics control | 168 |
| 114 | Cocaine | 100 |
| 321 | Morphine | 99 |
| 264 | Narcotics traffic | 69 |
| 415 | Drug industry | 8 |

(1,518 distinct documents corpus-wide carry at least one of these.)

**Agreement, restricted to documents dated before 1970:**

| | docs |
|---|---|
| Axis A (lexical) only | 102 |
| Axis B (subject tags) only | 34 |
| **both** | **982** |
| **union** | **1,118** |

That is 88% agreement between a stemmed word search and a human-assigned tag, which is the main
reason I believe the 1,118 figure is close to the real extent rather than to my imagination. It
also means neither axis is a substitute for the other: the tags miss ~9% (passing mentions inside
long telegrams), and the words miss ~3% (documents about the traffic that never name a drug).

**Caveat on axis B:** subject tagging is not complete. Per-decade tagging coverage of the corpus
runs 60%–86% (1890s 60%, 1940s 78%, 1960s 86%). So axis B under-counts everywhere, roughly evenly.

### 3.2 The 44 editorial compilations (the actual finding aid)

I walked every volume's table of contents (`volume_structures.structure_json`) looking for section
titles naming a drug. The full list is saved at `compilations.txt`; the pre-1970 spine is:

- **1906, 1907** (China): *Restriction upon the importation, growth, and use of opium*
- **1908, 1909, 1910, 1911** (China): *Joint International Commission for the Investigation of the Opium Question in the Far East* — i.e. the Shanghai Commission and its aftermath
- **1909** (Circulars): *International Opium Conference*
- **1912** (China): *First International Opium Conference — International Opium Convention and Protocol de Clôture*
- **1913, 1914, 1915** (China / Netherlands): *Second* and *Third International Opium Conference; report of the American delegates*
- **1914, 1916** (Circulars): *Opium laws and regulations*
- **1915** (International Conventions): *Convention … for the suppression of the abuse of Opium and other drugs*
- **1918** (China): *Opium and morphine traffic in China*, incl. *Purchase by the Chinese Government of reserve stock of opium and mandate for the destruction thereof*
- **1923** (General): *Participation by delegates from the United States in the deliberations of the League of Nations Advisory Committee on Traffic in Opium*
- **1924, 1925** (Persia): *Cooperation of the United States with Great Britain in efforts to restrict the export of opium from ports in the Persian Gulf*
- **1925** (Canada): *Extradition convention … applying to offenses against laws for the suppression of traffic in narcotics*
- **1926** (General): *Statement to certain foreign Governments regarding efforts of the United States to control the production of and traffic in narcotic drugs*
- **1928** (General): *Interest of the United States in efforts of the League of Nations to control the traffic in narcotic drugs*
- **1929** (General): *Agreements for exchange of information regarding the traffic in narcotic drugs*
- **1931** (General): the Geneva *Convention … for limiting the manufacture and regulating the distribution of narcotic drugs*, and the *Conference on the Suppression of Opium Smoking, Bangkok, November 9–27, 1931*
- **1932** (General): *Desire of the United States for early implementation of the [1931] convention*
- **1934** (General): ***Disinclination* of the United States to participate in a draft convention for the suppression of illicit traffic in dangerous drugs** — note the framing; this is the interesting one
- **1934, 1935, 1936, 1937** (China): *Problem of controlling the traffic in opium and narcotic drugs in China, including Manchuria and Jehol*
- **1935** (General): *Attitude of the United States with respect to League of Nations activities in the regulation of traffic in opium and other dangerous drugs*
- **1936** (General): *Participation by the United States in the Conference for the Suppression of the Illicit Traffic in Dangerous Drugs, Geneva, June 8–26, 1936*
- **1938, 1939** (Far East): *Problem of controlling the traffic in opium and other narcotic drugs in parts of China under Japanese military occupation*
- **1944 (vols II and V), 1945 (vol II)**: *Initiative of the United States to secure cooperation among interested governments on measures to limit and control production and to suppress illicit traffic in …*
- **1947** (Mexico): *United States efforts to eliminate illicit traffic in narcotic drugs between Mexico and the United States*
- **1952–54** (Turkey): narcotics folded into the omnibus US–Turkey relations compilation
- **1961–63, vol. XXV**: **Narcotics** — a discrete compilation under *Organization and Administration of Foreign Policy*, documents **326–345**

Note what the titles themselves argue: from 1906 to 1939 the compilation headings are about
*conferences, conventions, and the League*; from 1944 they become about *initiative* and
*cooperation*; by 1947 they are bilateral and enforcement-shaped (Mexico). The vocabulary of the
finding aid tracks the shift from multilateral regime-building to source-country suppression.

### 3.3 The archival axis — this is the strongest checkable evidence

`document_sources` preserves each printed document's file citation. Because State's own decimal
file schedule had a dedicated subject suffix for this, the file numbers give an axis completely
independent of both text and tags.

From the bundled `decimal-class-labels.json` (the 1910–1949 schedule), **subject suffix `.114` =
"Traffic in liquors and habit-forming drugs"**, and class 5 = "Congresses and Conferences".
Measured in the corpus:

| file class | meaning | docs |
|---|---|---|
| `511.4*` | US participation in the opium conferences | **165** |
| `893.114` (+ `893.114-N`, `893.114N`) | China — traffic in narcotics | 54 |
| `890.114`, `891.114`, `891.114Narcotics` | Asia / Persia–Iran — narcotics | 19 |
| `812.114 Narcotics` | Mexico — narcotics | 3 (Mexico compilation) |
| `341.9` | post-1949: UN Narcotic Drugs Commission | **13 — all in FRUS 1961–63 v. XXV** |
| `345.2` | post-1949: UN Drug Supervisory Body | (same volume) |
| `SOC 11–5` | post-1963 subject-numeric: *traffic in narcotics* | 3 in 1961–63 v. XXV; 48 in 1969–76 volumes |

**Two things fall out of this that a keyword search would never tell you.**

First, **`341.9` appears in exactly one volume of 552.** The State Department's dedicated
narcotics central file for 1950–63 has been printed in FRUS essentially once. That is the sharpest
single statement of how thin the mid-century coverage is.

Second — **a trap.** `811.114` (United States — traffic in liquors and habit-forming drugs) is the
largest `.114` class in the corpus at **269 documents**, and it is almost entirely *Prohibition*.
Of those 269, **147 mention "liquor" and only 8 mention narcotics or opium.** The same file class
carries both regimes. Do not treat `.114` as a narcotics filter without checking the country
number and the text.

`volume_sources` also names the relevant lot files directly, e.g. for FRUS 1961–63 v. XXV:
`RG 59, IO Files: Lot 67 D 378, Deputy Assistant Secretary Richard N. Gardner Files, 1961–65,
Opium…` — a concrete archival lead. The S/NM files (Lot 77 D 120, Vance) and IO/T/HNP files
(Lot 81 D 327) exist in the corpus only from 1969 forward.

### 3.4 Where the noise is — four false-positive classes, each measured

1. **Opium as commerce, not as a control problem.** The earliest hits (1861–1900) are the China
   trade, blockade-running, customs tariffs and li-kin revenue. The earliest opium document in the
   corpus is 1861-12-24 (`frus1862/d669`, arms-and-opium trading). Most 1860s–70s hits are of this
   kind.
2. **Opium as a strategic commodity in economic warfare.** FRUS 1943 vol. IV has 8 documents
   naming opium — every one is *preclusive purchasing* from Turkey, opium listed beside copper,
   chrome, mohair and valonia in Anglo-American denial lists. Real, but a different question.
3. **Opium as domestic political scandal reported by diplomats.** FRUS 1894 Appendix II — 41 hits,
   the third-largest volume on the lexical axis — is the Hawaiian revolution, where the "opium
   ring" and the opium licensing bill figure as causes of the overthrow. FRUS 1948 vol. VI is
   similar: Dutch charges of opium smuggling against Indonesian Republican negotiators at Batavia.
4. **Treaty boilerplate.** The Japanese Peace Treaty drafts (FRUS 1950 v. VI, 1951 v. VI pt 1)
   generate ~19 hits from a single recurring narcotics-obligations clause. Similarly the Albanian
   treaty-succession list (1946) and the German occupation directives (1949).
5. Outright homonyms: FRUS 1948 vol. IV's two "narcotic injection" hits are the Kasenkina
   defenestration case.

My rough read from sampling: of the 1,118 pre-1970 union documents, something like a quarter to a
third are of these kinds. I did not classify all 1,118 — that would be the next pass, and it is
mechanisable (start from the 44 compilations, treat everything outside them as needing a look).

## 4. What the corpus cannot tell you, and you should know before you rely on it

- **FRUS is a State series, and US drug diplomacy was largely Treasury's.** Harry J. Anslinger,
  Commissioner of Narcotics 1930–62 and the dominant American figure at Geneva and at the UN
  Commission, appears in **18 documents in the entire 552-volume corpus** (2 in the 1920s, 3 in
  the 1930s, 3 in the 1940s, 4 in the 1950s, 6 in the 1960s). Six of the eighteen are in the one
  1961–63 compilation. Whatever the corpus says about State's role, it will systematically
  understate the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, and the absence of Anslinger is not evidence of his
  absence from the policy.
- **Related name counts, for calibration:** "bureau of narcotics" 39 docs; "opium advisory
  committee"/"advisory committee on traffic in opium" 66; "permanent central"/"central board" 66;
  "supervisory body" 59; "commission on narcotic drugs" 51; "single convention" 67; "opium
  commission" 71; "illicit traffic" 168; "dangerous drugs" 119; "coca leaf(ves)" 41;
  "hamilton wright" 17; "stephen g. porter" 11; "bishop brent"/"charles h. brent" 25.
  These are corpus-wide, all dates.
- **The coca/cocaine side is thin.** 41 "coca leaf" documents corpus-wide, 100 tagged Cocaine.
  The 1949–50 UN Commission of Enquiry on the Coca Leaf and the Peru/Bolivia dimension are not, on
  this evidence, well represented before 1970.
- **Only 5 editorial notes** pre-1970 mention narcotics — the editors rarely used the narrative
  note form on this topic, so context has to come from the documents themselves.
- **Dates are document dates, not volume years.** 314,676 of 316,839 documents carry a parsed date
  (99.3%), so the temporal cuts above are reliable, but 2,163 documents are undated and fall out
  of every by-decade figure here.

## 5. What I would search next, concretely

**Start from the compilations, not from words.** Read `compilations.txt` as a reading list. The
1906–1915 China/Netherlands run and the 1923–1939 General/China run are the two blocks that will
carry an argument.

**Then run these, in this order:**

1. `source_note` / archival axis — `document_sources.decimal_class LIKE '511.4%'` (165 docs, 20
   volumes) is the cleanest single query for "US participation in international drug conferences,"
   independent of any word.
2. `document_sources.raw_text LIKE '%.114 Narcotics%'` — 58 documents, concentrated in 1944 v.II
   (12), 1936 v.IV (10), 1934 v.III (7), 1935 v.III (7). This is the country-file traffic material.
3. Institution names as phrases, not stems: `"opium advisory committee"`, `"permanent central
   opium board"`, `"drug supervisory body"`, `"commission on narcotic drugs"`, `"single
   convention"`. These separate regime-machinery documents from commodity documents cleanly.
4. **The wartime survival of the League machinery** — FRUS 1940 vol. II, documents d373–d415, the
   Tittmann (Consul General, Geneva) correspondence, is a 20-document run on whether the Opium
   Advisory Committee would be subordinated to a new League "Central Committee." I sampled eight
   and all eight are on point. This looked at first like a false positive and is not; it may be
   the least-worked thing in the pre-1970 material.
5. **FRUS 1934 vol. I**, *"Disinclination of the United States to participate in a draft convention
   for the suppression of illicit traffic in dangerous drugs"* — the one compilation title that
   states a refusal rather than a participation.
6. The 1946 transfer question: FRUS 1946 vol. I (`d70`, `d96`, `d102`, `d320`, `d508`) on moving
   the League's opium control into ECOSOC and standing up the Commission on Narcotic Drugs.
7. For the 1950s trough, don't look for a compilation — look inside the UN and area volumes.
   Anslinger's 1950s appearances are in FRUS 1950 v.V, 1951 v.II, 1952–54 v.IV, 1955–57 v.XXVII.
   FRUS 1958–60 v.XV's source list names file `892.53: Narcotics traffic in Thailand` and the
   volume carries a memorandum on Thailand's opium ban and its effect on farmers (`d121`).
   Iran recurs: opium-for-wheat barter, FRUS 1949 v.VI `d272` and 1950 v.V `d207`.
8. For the earliest State role: FRUS 1881–1888, the 1880 US–China commercial treaty's Article II
   prohibiting American citizens and vessels from the opium trade, and Treasury/State enforcement
   circulars — e.g. `frus1881/d133`, `frus1881/message-of-the-president` (Arthur, Dec. 1881:
   the treaty clauses "forbid the participation of citizens or vessels of the United States in the
   opium trade"), `frus1882/d76` (Frelinghuysen circular, April 1882), `frus1888p1/d200`, `d213`
   (the opium smuggling statute and consular jurisdiction). This is the pre-history that the
   1909-forward literature usually skips.

## 6. Evidence you can check me with

Files written to this run's directory:

- `compilations.txt` — all 44 volume-TOC section titles naming a drug, with volume ids
- `union_pre1970_by_volume.txt` — the 1,118 pre-1970 documents by volume (215 volumes), descending
- `vol_hits_pre1970.txt` — the lexical-axis-only version (211 volumes), for comparison
- `union_by_decade.txt` — the decade table in §3.1
- `volumes.csv` — volume id, coverage start/end year, subseries, title, for all 552

Reproduce any number with, e.g.:

```sh
sqlite3 -readonly /Users/jbotts/frus-analysis/frus-copy.db "
WITH u AS (SELECT DISTINCT volume_id v, document_id d FROM (
  SELECT dc.volume_id, dc.document_id
    FROM frus_documents f JOIN document_cache dc ON dc.rowid=f.rowid
   WHERE frus_documents MATCH 'narcotic* OR opium OR cocaine OR morphine OR heroin
        OR marihuana OR marijuana OR cannabis OR \"coca leaf\" OR \"coca leaves\"'
  UNION
  SELECT volume_id, document_id FROM document_subject_refs
   WHERE subject IN (107,114,264,321,364,395,415,481)))
SELECT count(*) FROM u JOIN document_dates dd ON dd.volume_id=u.v AND dd.document_id=u.d
 WHERE CAST(substr(dd.date_iso,1,4) AS INT) < 1970;"
-- 1118
```

**Where I am least confident.** (a) I sampled rather than classified: the signal/noise split in §3.4
is an impression from reading maybe 90 snippets, not a count. (b) My lexical vocabulary is
English-only and drug-name-only; documents about "the traffic" or "the Geneva convention" that
never name a substance are caught only by the subject tags, and the tags cover 60–86% of documents.
(c) The TOC walk in §3.2 keyed on section *titles*; a compilation whose title says
"Illicit traffic" without naming a drug would be missed — I re-ran with `smuggl|contraband|illicit
traffic` and found nothing narcotics-related that the first pass had missed, but that is a weaker
check than I would like. (d) I did not open the TEI XML at all; every claim here comes from the
SQLite index and the bundled JSON, and the index's own parse of source notes is what I trusted for
§3.3.
